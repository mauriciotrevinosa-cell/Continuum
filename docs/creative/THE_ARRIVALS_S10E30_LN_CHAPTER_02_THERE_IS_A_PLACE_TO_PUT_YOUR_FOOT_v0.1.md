# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 63
## Chapter 24 — There Is a Place to Put Your Foot

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S10 v0.2
**Volume:** 63
**Reading order:** 24
**Source episode:** S10E30
**Continuity state:** Day475 old night to the return threshold. Original Frieren and Mau acknowledge their real love and their choices. She sees that her later life holds happiness and helps him return freely to his wife. The road is a brief seam of this encounter, not a repeatable travel ability.

*When: Mau tries to tell her, the first thing he remembers is a compass pointing across a table.*

“You gave me something that always knew where you were,” he says. “And then I kept putting it where I could watch it find you.” ᴹ

Frieren looks at him.

“On the table.” ᶠ

“Yes.” ᴹ

“You said you'd forgotten it.” ᶠ

He rubs his palm over one knee. He has walked into this room with things to say about worlds and choices and the life beyond hers. He hadn't thought he'd have to confess this.

“I was embarrassed.” ᴹ

She lets out a small breath. It becomes a laugh before she can stop it. He looks up, and for a moment they are both looking at that table.

“I thought you weren't taking care of it.” ᶠ

“I was taking excellent care of it. I checked it constantly.” ᴹ

Her laugh breaks. She puts the cloth against her mouth. He reaches for her hand and stops where she can see him. She gives it to him.

The cloth stays in the other one.

“I was happy,” he says. “I need you to know that before I tell you anything else.” ᴹ

Frieren's fingers close around his.

He tells her about coming to find her with something so small he could have asked anyone, then making the conversation last because she had looked up from her book. About the pleasure of being expected in a room. About how much it had meant that she could be irritated with him and still move something off the place where he sat.

Some of it she remembers. She corrects one part. He tells her she is making his speech worse.

“You came here to tell me the truth,” she says. ᶠ

He laughs, and then he has to lower his head.

“Yes.” ᴹ

She keeps his hand while he gets enough of his voice back to go on.

* * *

“After a while,” he says, “it felt like the world only had to say your name to get me to walk into whatever it wanted. I thought I could make that stop if I became enough to stop it.” ᴹ

Frieren watches his face.

“And when I couldn't, I kept bringing it home. I was scared, and I hurt you, and then I wanted there to be a way nobody would have to remember how bad we'd made it.” ᴹ

“I thought if you were happy somewhere else, it meant I hadn't given you enough to stay.” ᶠ

He shakes his head.

“You gave me a life I loved. I'm sorry I took that away from you without asking. I should have let you tell me what you wanted.” ᴹ

She looks toward the door. He can hear Ori asking Rimuru something on the landing, too softly to make out the words. Rimuru answers. Their footsteps move a little farther away.

“I don't know what I would have said,” Frieren says. ᶠ

“I didn't give you time to know.” ᴹ

Her thumb moves once over the side of his hand.

There is no better version of the sentence that will make what he chose small enough to put away. He leaves it with her.

“When you showed me the meal,” she says, “I could see her listening to Ori. I know what she was doing. Sometimes Ori wants to get to the end so badly she forgets the thing she was trying to explain.” ᶠ

“You ask her to start again.” ᴹ

“She gets cross.” ᶠ

“She still does.” ᴹ

Frieren wipes her face with the cloth. She examines the wet patch with the faint annoyance of someone whose handkerchief has become less useful while she was using it.

“I wanted to know what was on the page,” she says. ᶠ

“What?” ᴹ

“The book you told me about. When you come in and I'm smiling at it, and I won't show you.” ᶠ

He remembers how nearly he passed over that small thing on the way to something he thought was more important.

“You won't tell me.” ᴹ

“Good.” ᶠ

He looks at her. She is crying again, but there is something else in her face now. He knows it from all the times a grimoire had contained a spell she couldn't yet imagine.

“Then there's something I still get to find out,” she says. ᶠ

* * *

“Do you love me now?” ᶠ

Mau feels the question through the hand he is holding. He raises his head.

“I love you for the life we had. I'm in love with my wife. I can say both of those to you and mean them.” ᴹ

Frieren shuts her eyes.

He gives her the time it takes to open them again.

“She knows?” ᶠ

“She knows I remember loving you. She knows I chose her.” ᴹ

Frieren looks at the ring. His hand is still in hers; she has been able to see it all this time.

“You put it on in front of everyone.” ᶠ

“Yes.” ᴹ

“I saw.” ᶠ

Her voice thins on the second word. He holds still while she looks. Then she lets his hand go and puts her palms against her knees.

“When you get there, don't spend the whole night trying to tell her this correctly,” she says. “Tell her you wanted to come home.” ᶠ

His breath catches hard enough that he has to turn away.

“I did.” ᴹ

“I know. You were running round the garden.” ᶠ

He laughs through the breath he hasn't quite managed to take.

She bends toward him. He puts his forehead against the side of the bed, and for a while her hand is in his hair. It is a kindness she can still give him. He lets himself receive it without asking it to become anything else.

“Thank you,” he says. ᴹ

Her hand moves once.

“I was happy too.” ᶠ

He nods against the blanket. She feels it.

* * *

Something taps against the window.

He lifts his head. Beyond the glass there is a strip of pale paper caught against a low wall. A road runs beside it. Then the tree is there again, and the dark roof he had been looking at from the garden.

He gets to his feet too quickly. Frieren catches his sleeve.

“What is it?” ᶠ

“The road.” ᴹ

He goes closer. The glass reflects his shirt. Beyond it the line of the wall appears, a crack through the mortar almost level with the hand he'd scraped when he fell.

He had looked for an opening in every direction he could reach. He had tried to force his way up. Now, with his weight on both feet, he can feel where the other ground should be.

Frieren stands beside him. He looks at her before he tries to move.

“I think that's where I go.” ᴹ

“To her?” ᶠ

“Home.” ᴹ

She looks through the window, then down at the floor under his boots. She can't see what he sees. She moves the chair out of his way anyway.

At the door, Ori says his name.

He turns.

Rimuru has brought her back. Behind them he can see the people who came up when the sound of their conversation changed. Maki's hand rests on the banister. Yuta is beside her. Farther down, Shoko has the cup he left on the table. They have made room in the narrow landing without making it look like a procession.

“Are you going?” Ori asks. ᴼ

He holds his hand over the pocket with her drawing.

“I found the way.” ᴹ

She comes to him. He crouches, and she puts both arms around his neck so quickly he has to catch his balance. He holds her while she presses her face against him.

“Both windows,” she says. ᴼ

“I'll ask you to show me both.” ᴹ

She pulls back far enough to see him promise it. Then she goes to Frieren, who takes her hand.

Rimuru rubs his face with one palm.

“They're going to be wondering where you've got to,” he says. ᴿⁱ

“I'll tell them.” ᴹ

“Try starting there this time.” ᴿⁱ

Mau looks at the people on the landing. He has tried to memorize the shape of so many terrible things. He makes himself look at this long enough to remember it too.

Maki nods once. Yuta's mouth trembles when he tries to smile. Shoko raises the cup a little, a reminder he can take home without needing a lecture attached.

Below them, where he can't see her, Yuka calls his name.

“Breakfast,” she says. ʸ

He knows what she means. He puts a hand over his mouth, nods, and makes himself answer aloud.

“I won't forget.” ᴹ

*I am here,* Ciel says. ᶜ

He rests his palm against his chest.

“I know.” ᴹ

* * *

Frieren comes close enough to put her free hand against the back of his shoulder. Ori is holding the other one.

“I love you,” Frieren says. “Thank you for coming to tell me.” ᶠ

He turns to look at her. She is crying openly now. She leaves her hand where it is.

“Go to your life, Mau.” ᶠ

“Thank you,” he says. ᴹ

He takes the step with her hand still warm through his shirt. For one instant he feels the small pressure that used to guide him past something she had left in his way.

Then his boot finds the slope of the ditch.
