# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 70
## Chapter 15 — One Drop

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S11 v0.1
**Volume:** 70
**Reading order:** 15
**Source episode:** S11E60
**Continuity state:** Local days 44–52 (outside ~2h56m–3h28m). Vertical shaft with constant falling water; drops strike with force priced by the field as hostile contact (modifier x2.5; outside display shows x3 from ~day 47, after he adapts, which Mau only feels at the fall). Breathing and still air remain harmless. Mau descends with neutral raised while moving and lowered under overhangs. After he adapts, a ledge gives under him; during the fall the neutral answers late and a single drop shatters his right forearm. Authority partly brakes the landing. Left wrist and thigh/hip injuries from before persist. No new technique, unit or outside contact.

*When: Day forty-four. The ceiling begins to drip a little after Mau leaves the hall, and outside nearly three hours have passed.*

The first drop lands on the back of his hand.

It stings like a thrown pebble.

He stops in the passage and looks at the place. There is a mark the size of a fingertip, darker than the skin around it and getting darker at the centre. Above him the stone is wet, and a second bead is gathering at the end of a small grey spike, getting heavier.

He moves his hand before it falls.

It strikes the floor where his hand was and leaves a pit.

Mau looks at the pit for a long moment. Then he laughs, because the alternative is to sit down in the passage, and he isn't sure he would get up again.

“Water,” he says. ᴹ

*Hits,* Noise says. ᴺ

“Yes. I noticed.” ᴹ

* * *

The passage opens into a shaft.

He stands at the lip and looks down, and for the first time since the first room he can't see the end of where he has to go. The walls fall away into grey. Ledges stick out of them at uneven heights, some wide enough to lie on, some barely wider than his boot. A path of sorts zigzags between them, made by nothing he can see.

And from somewhere far above, out of a dark he can't see the top of either, it rains.

Not hard. A steady patter, the sound of a quiet afternoon. Every drop that lands on stone leaves a small bright scar.

He puts the neutral up and steps onto the first ledge.

The rain stops at the edge of the space around him. He watches the drops slow and hang and slide aside, and feels the field lean on the hold the way it leaned in the hall. Every drop is a contact. Every contact costs.

Halfway to the second ledge he understands he cannot hold it all the way down.

* * *

So he learns the shaft.

It takes three days. The overhangs first: places where the wall bulges out above a ledge and the rain falls past instead of on him. Under those he can drop the neutral and breathe and let the cost stop. Between them he raises it and moves.

He learns which ledges are sound and which are cracked. He learns that the rain is heavier on the left wall, where a seam above must be feeding it. He learns to descend on his good side so the bad hip takes the step down and not the step across.

He learns not to look up. Looking up gives the drops his face.

On the second day he misjudges an overhang by a hand's width and a drop takes him on the top of the head. It is like being hit with a hammer through a towel. His vision goes white and comes back with blood running into his eye. He sits under the next overhang for an hour with his head in his good hand, closing the split in his scalp a little at a time.

After that he gets it right.

By the forty-seventh day he moves through the rain like a man walking between the drops of a waterfall he has known all his life. Neutral up, three steps, ledge. Neutral down under the bulge. Breathe. Drink, out of the flask, because the water in the shaft would break his teeth. Up, four steps, ledge.

He catches himself almost enjoying it.

He stops, under the overhang, and makes himself feel how much his hip hurts instead.

“It's going to change,” he says. ᴹ

*Yes,* Noise agrees. ᴺ

“I don't know what.” ᴹ

*No.* ᴺ

He waits a long time under that overhang, until his legs begin to stiffen and he has to move or lose the use of them for the next stretch.

Nothing happens.

He goes on.

* * *

It happens on the fiftieth day, on a ledge he has already stood on.

He has come back to it because the path below doubles round. The ledge is sound. He knows it is sound because his weight has been on it before and he remembers the feel of it under his boots. He puts his foot on the far end, where the next overhang begins, and lowers the neutral one breath early, because the bulge is right there and he has done this a hundred times.

The ledge isn't there.

It doesn't crack. There is no warning sound. One instant there is stone under his foot and the next there is a hole the shape of where his weight went, as though the wall had opened a mouth.

He falls.

He reaches for the neutral and it comes, but it comes late, the way everything comes late now, a stretched breath between asking and receiving. He reaches for Authority at the same time, both at once, too much at once, and the field takes its share of each.

The rain falls with him.

He sees it. That is the worst of it afterwards, that he sees it. A single drop, a little ahead of the rest, falling from a height so great that he has never seen where it began. It meets him in the half breath before the neutral closes.

It hits his right forearm.

There is a crack he feels in his teeth.

The arm does not bend so much as come apart. He feels the bones go, not one place but many, the whole length of it from the wrist to below the elbow, as though the drop had been the size of a wagon and had only pretended to be water.

Then the neutral closes, and the rest of the rain stops, and he is still falling.

* * *

Authority catches the wall.

Not cleanly. He gets a grip on the stone as it rushes past and the grip tears and he gets another and that one holds for a moment and burns. It slows him enough. He strikes a ledge below with his left side and his left hand and the bad wrist folds under him, and he rolls, and the edge of the ledge is under his hip, and then it isn't.

He stops.

His legs are over nothing. His left hand is clawed into a crack in the stone. His right arm lies beside him on the ledge in a shape he does not recognise as belonging to him.

He doesn't move.

The neutral is still up. The rain slides past around him. Every drop is a hand on his chest, pushing.

*Mau.* ᴺ

“I know.” ᴹ

*Up.* ᴺ

“I know.” ᴹ

He pulls with the left hand.

The wrist screams. He pulls anyway, and gets one knee over the edge, and then the other, and then he is lying on his back on a ledge in the rain with the neutral held over him like a roof he is paying for with every breath.

He drops it under the first overhang he can drag himself to. It takes him a long time to get there. He doesn't remember most of it.

* * *

When he can think again, he looks at the arm.

He makes himself look at all of it. The skin is not broken except in two places, but the forearm is the wrong length and the wrong shape, soft where it should be straight, swelling already, the skin at the wrist gone dark and tight. When he tries to move his fingers, one of them moves.

He lies back against the wall.

Above him, past the lip of the overhang, the rain keeps falling, steady and gentle, the sound of a quiet afternoon.

“A drop,” he says. ᴹ

His voice cracks on it.

He starts laughing. It hurts everywhere. It goes on for a while, longer than it should, and somewhere in the middle of it the sound turns into something that isn't laughing any more, and he lets it, because there is nobody here to be careful in front of.

Noise stays close.

It doesn't say *here* this time. It doesn't say anything. It sits with him in the only way it can, attention on him like a hand on the back of his neck, until he has run out.

Then, very quietly:

*Bleeding first.* ᴺ

Mau wipes his face on his shoulder.

“Bleeding first,” he says. ᴹ

He starts.
