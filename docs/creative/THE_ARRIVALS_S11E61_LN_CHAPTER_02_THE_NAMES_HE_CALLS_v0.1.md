# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 70
## Chapter 17 — The Names He Calls

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S11 v0.1
**Volume:** 70
**Reading order:** 17
**Source episode:** S11E61
**Continuity state:** Local days 70–108 (outside ~4h40m–7h12m). Sector of total darkness. Mau navigates by his reproduced eyes reading cursed energy and by sound; after he adapts, the field introduces constructs with no cursed energy, then dampens sound. Injuries: crushed left foot and two more broken ribs on the right. Own constrained RCT. While healing, he calls Ciel, Shoko, Beru and Yuka aloud, knowing none can come. Right forearm still short and weak, left wrist, hip and back as before. No new technique or unit; 6,616 bonds.

*When: Day seventy-one. Mau reaches the bottom of the shaft and the last of the grey light stays behind him; outside it is early afternoon.*

The dark is complete.

Not the dark of a room at night, where the eyes adjust and shapes come back. Here they do not come back. He holds his hand in front of his face and there is nothing, and when he brings it closer there is still nothing, until his own breath warms his palm.

Then he lets the eyes open.

The filter his body keeps for him narrows the flood to what he needs, and the dark fills with lines. Not light. The shape of energy moving in things that have it. A crawling body on the left wall, its small supply knotted at the centre. A seam in the floor where something old runs under the stone. His own hands, faint and warm, the right one with its crooked join.

The rest is black.

“All right,” he says. ᴹ

*See?* Noise asks. ᴺ

“Some.” ᴹ

He walks into it.

* * *

He learns the dark the way he learned the hall and the rain.

The things that live here all carry a little cursed energy, and his eyes read it before they move. A coil tightening in the dark means a lunge. A thinning means it is about to drop from the ceiling. He kills them as they come, with the daggers held in two weak hands, using Authority only when he has to. He listens, too. The scrape of a joint. The tick of a claw on stone. His hearing becomes something he leans his weight on.

By the eightieth day he can cross a cavern he cannot see with his eyes closed, so to speak, and come out the far side unbloodied.

He knows what that means now.

He sleeps that night in a crack in the wall and does not let himself feel pleased.

* * *

On the eighty-third day something hits his foot that he never saw.

He is walking a ledge he has walked before. His eyes show him the empty stone ahead, nothing living in it, nothing moving. His ears give him only his own steps.

The floor closes on his left foot like a jaw.

It is stone. Just stone, shaped and set to fall. There is no energy in it for the eyes to read and no living thing in it to make a sound, and it comes down on the top of his foot with the weight of a door and the field's price on top of that.

He hears the bones go.

He goes down screaming and doesn't care who hears. There is nobody to hear.

* * *

They come after that, the silent ones.

Pieces of the cavern that move. A pillar that leans. A slab that slides out of the wall at the height of his ribs. They have no supply for his eyes to catch and no life for his ears to find. They have only weight and timing, and the field's hand behind them.

He learns them too.

He has to. He learns the faint change in the air before a slab moves, a pressure on the skin of his face. He learns the grind they make the instant before they fall, a sound so small he has to hold his breath to hear it.

By the ninety-fifth day he can hear them coming.

On the ninety-sixth, the sound goes away.

Not only theirs. All of it. He shouts, to test it, and the shout leaves his throat and dies a hand's width from his mouth, as though the dark has swallowed it whole. His own footsteps are gone. The tick of the crawlers is gone. He can feel his heart in his ears and that is the only noise left in the world.

The slab takes him on the right side before he knows it is moving.

Two ribs, then a third that was already cracked. The breath goes out of him and doesn't come back for a long time.

* * *

He finds a corner.

He doesn't know how. He crawls on his hands and one knee, dragging the crushed foot, along a wall he can feel but not hear, until the wall turns and makes a place where nothing can reach him from two sides. He puts his back into it.

Then he sits there in the silent dark, and starts.

Bleeding first. The foot second. The ribs he can only hold together while the rest of him decides whether it is going to keep breathing.

RCT trickles through the narrow pipe. He feeds it to the ribs because breathing is the thing that kills you fastest, and the foot sits at the end of his leg like a bag of broken crockery, waiting its turn.

“Ciel,” he says. ᴹ

He doesn't hear it.

He says it anyway, out of habit, the way he would say it at home when something hurt and he wanted the count, the calm voice telling him which part to fix first.

*Ciel, the ribs or the foot.* ᴹ

Nothing.

He knows there will be nothing. He has known since the corridor smelled of wet iron. She is outside, on the other side of a wall that is not a wall, and she cannot hear him. He keeps asking anyway, because the asking is shaped like her, and that is the closest he can get.

* * *

On the second day in the corner the foot swells so badly the boot has to come off.

He cuts it off with the dagger, a little at a time, his right hand too weak to do more than saw. When the leather finally falls away he looks at the foot with his eyes, at the faint lines of his own energy running through it, broken and kinked, and has to look away.

“Shoko,” he says. ᴹ

His mouth moves. Nothing comes out into the dark.

“Shoko, it's bad. I don't know where the pieces go.” ᴹ

He can see her. That's the cruel part. He can see her exactly, kneeling at the edge of a bed with her sleeves pushed up, telling him to hold still, telling him he's an idiot, putting her hands on the wrong shape and making it the right one. He can almost feel it. The heat of her output going in, far more than his narrow trickle, sure of itself, knowing exactly what bones are supposed to do.

She doesn't come.

He works on the foot for nine hours. He gets three pieces to touch that weren't touching. It will never be right.

* * *

On the fourth day, Beru.

He doesn't decide to say it. It comes out of him while he is trying to sleep, the ribs keeping him from lying down and the corner keeping him upright.

*Beru.* ᴹ

The bond is there. It has always been there. He can feel Beru at the far end of it, the way he can feel all of them, six thousand six hundred and sixteen faint weights in the dark below his ribs. He can feel that Beru is awake, and waiting, and wanting.

The field won't let him through.

*I know you're there,* Mau tells him. *I know you'd come. I'm not angry. I just wanted to say it to somebody.* ᴹ

The weight at the far end of the bond doesn't change. It can't. Whatever Beru is doing on the other side of the frame, he can't hear this.

Mau puts his head back against the stone.

“You'd be so annoying about this,” he says. ᴹ

He laughs without sound, and it hurts his ribs, and he lets it.

* * *

He doesn't say her name until the sixth day.

He has been saving it. He doesn't know why. Maybe because the other names are people he can let himself need, and hers is the one he is afraid of.

The foot has stopped getting worse. The ribs hold if he breathes in short pulls. He has eaten two mouthfuls of bread, softened in water, and kept them down. Noise has been quiet beside him, saying *here* every few hours, so he knows which way is up in a dark without sound.

He says it.

“Yuka.” ᴹ

The word dies a hand's width from his mouth.

He says it again, louder, as loud as his ribs allow. It dies in the same place.

He knows she can't hear him. He knows she is outside, doing whatever there is to do, with Rimuru and Gojo and the rest. He knows that whatever she is thinking, she is not thinking it at him through a silent dark.

He calls her anyway.

“Yuka. Yuka, I'm here. I'm still—” ᴹ

He stops, because his voice has broken, and because he doesn't know what the end of that sentence is. *I'm still here.* *I'm still coming.* *I'm still yours, if you want that.* He doesn't know which one is true any more. He doesn't know which one she would want to hear.

So he just says her name.

Over and over, into the dark that eats it, until he is crying too hard to make the shape of it, and then he says it in his head, where it doesn't need a mouth.

*Yuka. Yuka. Yuka.* ᴹ

Noise doesn't interrupt.

When at last he stops, it says the one word it always says.

*Here.* ᴺ

“I know you are,” he whispers. ᴹ

He means it as thanks. It comes out sounding like an apology.

* * *

On the hundred and eighth day he stands on a foot that bends in the wrong place, binds it so tightly into a boot from the shop that it can't bend any further, and limps out of the corner.

The sound comes back as he leaves the sector. All at once, at a doorway: the scrape of his boot, his breath, the creak of the pack straps. It is so loud that he flinches.

He stands in the doorway for a long time, listening to himself breathe.

Then he goes on.
