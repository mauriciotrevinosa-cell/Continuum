# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 70
## Chapter 18 — A Room With a Bed

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S11 v0.1
**Volume:** 70
**Reading order:** 18
**Source episode:** S11E62
**Continuity state:** Local days 108–110 (outside ~7h12m–7h20m). A quiet room with light, a real bed, clean water and no threats; the way onward stays open. The Architect speaks to Mau for the first time since the corridor: no threat, an offer to stop wanting to leave, "something better can stand where you are standing." He never names his monarch or explains the Black Heart; he has no knowledge of private family matters. Mau sleeps one night because his body needs it and leaves in the morning. Injuries as #17. No new technique or unit.

*When: Day one hundred and eight. Mau steps out of the silent dark into light, and outside the afternoon is getting on.*

The room is warm.

That is the first thing. Not hot, not the damp heat of the shaft. Warm the way a house is warm in the evening when somebody has kept the stove going.

The second thing is the light. Plain and yellow, coming from a lamp on a small table, though he can see no oil in the lamp and no flame.

The third thing is the bed.

He stands in the doorway with his weight on the good foot and looks at it for a long time. A real bed. A frame of pale wood, a mattress, a pillow, a blanket folded at the foot with its corner turned down. Beside it a jug of water and a cup.

Nothing else. No arms in the walls. No grit on the floor. No slab waiting in a seam.

His eyes show him nothing in the room that holds any energy at all except the lamp, steady and small, and himself.

*Trap,* Noise says. ᴺ

“Probably.” ᴹ

He doesn't move.

* * *

He checks it the way he checks everything now.

He throws a scrap of cloth into the middle of the floor and watches it lie there. He touches the wall nearest the door with the point of a dagger. He listens: the lamp makes a faint hum, and there is nothing else to hear. He opens the shop and buys nothing, just to see whether the room changes when he does.

It doesn't change.

On the far side of the room there is another doorway, open, leading on into the dark. The way forward. It isn't blocked. He can see the first stones of the next passage.

He limps to the jug and smells the water. He pours a little on his hand and waits. Nothing happens to his hand.

He drinks.

It is the best thing he has ever tasted. He drinks the whole cup and has to sit down on the floor, his back against the leg of the bed, because his eyes have filled and his hands are shaking.

* * *

“You may stay,” says the Architect. ᴬʳ

Mau does not jump. He has been waiting for it.

The voice comes from nowhere in particular. From the room. It is the same voice that spoke in the corridor on the first day, calm and level, a voice that sounds as though it is reading a measurement aloud.

“As long as you like,” it says. “Nothing will come into this room. Nothing in it will change.” ᴬʳ

Mau looks at the bed.

“And if I go through that door?” ᴹ

“Then it continues.” ᴬʳ

“Until?” ᴹ

The voice does not answer that.

* * *

“What do you want?” Mau asks. ᴹ

He asks it the way he asked Makima by the broken wall. He has learned, since the clearing, that the people who hurt you will sometimes simply tell you, if you ask plainly enough and don't pretend you already know.

“I want you to stop,” the Architect says. ᴬʳ

“Stop what?” ᴹ

“Wanting to leave.” ᴬʳ

Mau almost laughs.

“You built a place that breaks my bones every time I learn how to walk in it, and you want me to stop wanting to leave.” ᴹ

“Yes.” ᴬʳ

The answer is so plain that it frightens him more than a threat would have.

“Why?” ᴹ

“Because you are holding something shut.” ᴬʳ

* * *

Mau waits.

The lamp hums. The water in the jug catches the light.

“You were never the point,” the Architect says. “You are a place. A good one, better than I designed. You have filled it with a great many things that do not belong there. Your name. Your habits. Your house.” ᴬʳ

The word *house* lands strangely in that voice. It says it the way a man might say *rust*.

“When you stop wanting to go back to them, the place will be empty. Then something better can stand where you are standing.” ᴬʳ

Mau doesn't ask what. He knows who the voice built this for. He has met him. He has made tea for him and opened a window for him and heard him say he wanted to rest.

“He said no,” Mau says. ᴹ

“He was asked while you were in the way.” ᴬʳ

“He said no.” ᴹ

“Or,” the Architect says, as though Mau has not spoken, “something new. It does not need to be him. It needs you not to be there.” ᴬʳ

* * *

For a while neither of them says anything.

Mau looks at his hands. The left one with its stiff fingers and the wrist that has healed wrong twice. The right one, shorter now, with the bend below the elbow. His foot in its tight boot, throbbing in time with his heart.

“You could kill me,” he says. “You've had about a hundred chances.” ᴹ

“A body that dies is not a place.” ᴬʳ

“So you need me alive and empty.” ᴹ

“I need you tired,” the Architect says. “You are already very tired. It is only a matter of whether you notice before or after you have lost something you would have liked to keep.” ᴬʳ

Mau closes his eyes.

“There are people trying to get in,” he says. ᴹ

He doesn't know that. He has no way of knowing it. He says it because he needs it to be true.

“Trying,” the Architect agrees. “Trying is not a door.” ᴬʳ

* * *

The voice stops after that.

He waits a long time for it to say something else. It doesn't. The room is quiet and warm and the lamp goes on humming as though it will hum forever.

He looks at the bed.

He knows exactly what it is. He is not stupid. A room where nothing hurts, at the end of a hundred days where everything hurt, offered by the person who arranged the hurting. Stay here and rest. Stay here and nothing will change, ever, and after long enough you will stop remembering why you wanted it to.

It is the kindest thing anyone has offered him since the corridor.

That is the trap.

* * *

He sleeps in it anyway.

One night. His body makes the decision for him, the way it made the decision about food in the shaft. He has not lain flat since the corner, and the ribs need it, and the foot needs it, and the back needs it more than anything. He tells himself it is triage. He tells Noise to wake him if anything changes.

He lies down on the mattress, and pulls the blanket over himself with his crooked arm, and puts his head on the pillow.

It smells clean.

He doesn't mean to cry. He has done so much of it in the last hundred days that he thought he might have used it up. But the pillow smells clean, like laundry dried outside, like the sheets at home on the day after Rem does the washing, and something in his chest gives way.

He turns his face into it so that, if anyone is watching, they won't get to see.

* * *

In the morning nothing has changed.

The lamp hums. The jug is full again, though he emptied it. The far door stands open on the dark.

He gets up.

It is harder than it has ever been. Every part of him has spent one night believing it would be allowed to stay, and every part of him objects. His foot, his ribs, his back, his arms. His heart, which is the part he hadn't expected.

He fills his flask from the jug. He folds the blanket and leaves it at the foot of the bed, corner turned down, the way he found it. He doesn't know why. It seems important not to leave a mess in someone else's trap.

At the far door he stops and looks back.

“I'm not staying,” he says to the room. ᴹ

The room doesn't answer. It doesn't need to. It will still be here.

“Not because you're wrong about me being tired,” he says. “Because you wanted me to.” ᴹ

He steps through into the dark.

Behind him the lamp hums on, steady and patient, keeping the room warm for whenever he comes back.
