# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 75
## Chapter 5 — The Monster Has a Name

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S12 v0.1
**Volume:** 75
**Reading order:** 5
**Source episode:** S12E29
**Continuity state:** B+273. Luminous proposes a tea exchange. The hearing opens on jurisdiction, the road declarations and what families may ask of the house.

*When: Luminous has samples. This would have been useful to know when Mau put his hands down on the table.*

The packet slides across the wood with a sound like a book being set down on its spine. Luminous keeps a finger on it until Mau looks up.

“I've acquired several things since our last conversation,” she says. “I was thinking about taking one to the next tea. I'd like to know whether anybody has a preference before I decide which.” ᴸ

“Do you want me to ask them, or do you want a list of what they already drink?” ᴹ

“A list. I'd like them to find out which one I chose when they pour it.” ᴸ

He has put a finger on the packet now, directly opposite hers. “I can get you a proper list.” ᴹ

“If any of them hate it, I'll say it was your recommendation.” ᴸ

“Then I can put something on it that I want to try.” ᴹ

He lifts the edge of the packet enough to smell it. There's something sharp in it he can't place. Luminous tells him he can have a better look after the meeting, when the clerk has finished with the table.

“Are you here about the forms?” he asks. ᴹ

“A merchant who makes that tea asked me which papers he should carry on his own road.” She takes one finger off the packet. “I'd like to be able to tell him before I buy anything from him again.” ᴸ

Mau opens his copy of the notice. She looks at the empty date and asks whether he saw it like that himself. He tells her where it was and who copied it. She goes back to the date once before she gives the page back.

“Tell them the part you saw,” she says. “I'll ask about the road when it's my turn.” ᴸ

She takes the packet back, but she has to turn her face a little before she can look towards the chair again. Mau catches it and goes back to his seat happier than he expected to be when he sat down.

The clerk makes a little space beside his book for anything they agree to put on the record. Two people set papers down at once. He asks them to wait until he's taken the names for both. Mau watches a man lift his whole stack and give it over without taking the top page off, as though it might all go in together if the clerk doesn't get to see where it starts.

The clerk starts with the invitation. He has been told to read it aloud, and the man three seats to his right tells him he can dispense with that if everyone has received a copy. The clerk looks at Mau. Mau would like to hear it. So would the woman at the end of that row, who puts down a page with a question half written on it. The clerk gets as far as *allegations arising at the crossings* before a man on the other side says they should start with the question that made everybody come.

“What do we do with the monster?” ᴰᵉ

“The monster has a name,” Mau says. ᴹ

The clerk takes his pen away from the page. Mau tells him how to write his name. He says it twice because the clerk had started it with the name of the village. Then the clerk reads the rest of the invitation, the way he was asked.

The second page really does sound different read out loud. There's a line about the storeyard that comes after a line about trade. By the time the clerk gets to the first complaint, anybody who came to ask about a sheet of paper has heard that they ought to ask about a man with a blade first.

Mau puts Fern's two pages down beside the book.

“Before we start, I'd like somebody to answer the question on top of these. What happens to the names after the clerks collect them?” ᴹ

“We have a statement about the yard,” the man who called him a monster says. ᴰᵉ

“I'll answer for what I did there. I'd still like the question put down.” ᴹ

The clerk reads Fern's line and copies it. Mau waits until he has finished it. He'd told her he'd ask. At least there is somewhere for an answer to go now.

The statement about the yard has been copied from a report taken on the north road. Mau asks where the person who gave it was standing. The delegate says the man heard about it at the crossing, from somebody who came from the direction of the yard. He reads as far as the part about the dead before Luminous asks him how many there were. The page doesn't say. She waits while he checks the back.

“I cut through a cart. I used fire in the yard,” Mau says. “There was nobody in the place I sent it. The people I was fighting were alive when I left, and I took the person I'd gone for out alive.” ᴹ

The delegate looks down at the statement. “So the cut and the fire were yours.” ᴰᵉ

“Yes. If you're asking what I did, I'd like that part written down too.” ᴹ

The clerk asks him which day it was. Eight days ago, Mau tells him. The clerk puts the date down and glances at the two places in his book where he's started writing. He puts a line between them. Mau watches him put the words *copied report* above the first account and his own name above the second.

“Can somebody ask the people who were there?” the woman at the far end says. ᴰᵃ

“They can ask. I'd leave it to those people to decide if they want to answer.” ᴹ

She nods and puts a finger beside her own question. She's been waiting for a place to come in since before the invitation was read.

A man from the eastern benches has copies of a list of places where armed people gave way to Mau. The first three places had come by report of one another. The fourth is the castle on the trade crossing. He puts his list down beside the report from the yard. The woman at the end of the row asks for the turn she'd arranged, and the clerk looks down at her name and gives it to her.

“A family came to me for a letter,” she says. “They were given a declaration on the road that asked who was going to give them shelter. They were afraid to write a name down. If somebody at your house had been the reason they were afraid, who would I have sent that family to?” ᴰᵃ

“Who gave them the declaration?” Mau asks. ᴹ

“I'd like an answer to both questions. I haven't got one to yours.” ᴰᵃ

Mau keeps his hand off the invitation for a moment. It was a thing somebody might ask Rimuru if they'd come home with that paper and a name they didn't want to say in a crowded room. He'd want Rimuru to sit down and listen before he called for him.

“You can send them to Fern or Rimuru. They can bring somebody they trust. If the complaint is about me, I'll come answer it when they've had a chance to talk.” ᴹ

“Could you compel me to deliver somebody to you with a letter like that?” ᴰᵃ

“No. If it has our name on it you can bring it to us, too. I'd want to know who wrote it.” ᴹ

She writes something down. A man from the east asks to see the declaration and she sends down the copy she brought. Mau recognises the blank line in the bottom third of it before he can make out the words. He's seen Fern cover that line with her hand to let somebody take the paper home without having to look at it again.

“Those questions are necessary if we are to know who has come through our territory,” the man says. ᴰᵉ

The woman takes her eyes off her own page. “Is this one of yours, then?” ᴰᵃ

He puts the copy down without answering. He starts to say that nobody can expect to take in everyone who crosses the road and keep the names to themselves. Testarossa asks how his people handle a name supplied by a traveller who has no other way to get food. He looks down at the copy again. There is nothing at the bottom to tell him who will answer for it if he says he wrote it.

The clerk asks whether he'd like to enter a copy from his own office. He declines. The woman holds out a hand for hers and gets it back creased down the middle.

She smooths it on the table. Fern's notice is still beside the clerk's book, within reach of somebody willing to put a date on it. Luminous asks whether any of the delegations can tell her which order the crossing clerks were given. The man from the east turns to his companion. The two of them talk quietly enough that the clerk sets his pen down.

“Our office can look for an order when we get home,” one of them says at last. ᴰᵉ

“Then I can tell the merchant that much,” Luminous says. ᴸ

Mau looks at Fern's line in the book. He can bring her an answer about what the man offered to look for. The space after her question is still empty.

Mau hears a chair move on the opposite side and sees a man with a Lugunican delegation put both hands on the table.

“Then I have a name for you,” he says. ᴰˡ
