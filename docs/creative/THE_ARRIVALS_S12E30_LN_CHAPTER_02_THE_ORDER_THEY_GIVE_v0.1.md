# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 75
## Chapter 7 — The Order They Give

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S12 v0.1
**Volume:** 75
**Reading order:** 7
**Source episode:** S12E30
**Continuity state:** B+273. An eastern delegate threatens the house and its allies. Mau states the limit of his response and tells his allies they can choose their own distance. He leaves by exchanging with the ant at the outer lodge.

*When: Mau had started putting the invitation back inside his coat. He finishes before he gets up.*

The man from the eastern bench asks which delegations here are prepared to keep offering the house safe conduct. He puts the question to the row where Testarossa is sitting. The sign for Tempest is directly in front of her. She moves a cup away from it so he can see the whole word.

“I'd like to know what you're asking us to give up,” she says. ᵀᵉ

“The roads your people use reach our territory. We may have to close some of them while the matter is resolved.” ᴰᵉ

“To the house?” ᵀᵉ

The man glances towards Elmesia. “To anyone issuing papers that make it possible for them to cross.” ᴰᵉ

The clerk turns to a clean page. Mau hears it come away from the rest of the book. The woman who brought the declaration asks whether this means a person with a letter from her office would be stopped as well. The man tells her there will be time to settle exceptions once everybody has agreed on the purpose of the restriction.

“You still haven't said what that purpose is,” she says. ᴰᵃ

Mau finishes putting the invitation back inside his coat. He looks at the doorway. There's a man with papers standing to one side of it, and a place on the other side where there was enough room for somebody to bring in two chairs at once.

“Getting him back to this table if he leaves it,” the man says. “If we bring in a few people from the house first, we may find we never have to go as far as the village.” ᴰᵉ

Mau had been about to ask which road he meant to close. He can think of three people at home who would leave by one of those roads without looking to see who was waiting at the first gate.

Mau is at his elbow when he takes his next breath.

The man makes a sound and turns his chair around. The edge of it catches on a seam in the floor. Mau puts one hand on the back and holds it still until the man stops trying to pull it out from under himself. The door behind them is still open. Mau has left him room to use it.

“I already took down a castle,” Mau says. “Edran ordered men to take Hollis off the road and bring him there. We got the people out before I touched the stone. You can ask Hollis who came out of it.” ᴹ

The man looks towards the clerk. The clerk has put his pen down to see whether he needs to leave.

“And that gave you the right to take down his castle?” asks the man who brought the list. ᴰᵉ

“I took it down. I'll answer for doing it.” Mau lets his hand rest on the chair without pulling it any farther back. “Hollis asked a woman named Meret how many people used the oven in the castle. She had a girl sleeping above it so she could make the first bread. We had to find out where the girl would sleep and where Meret would bake before we could count that room empty.” ᴹ

The clerk picks the pen up again. Mau tells him Meret's name slowly. It had taken a while to find the two doors whose steps shared stone with the castle. He can remember the light Yuka watched from the well after the last of the gatehouse came down. He can remember how long it took to get the grit out of the bucket.

“The order had Edran's name on it,” he says. “He renewed it after Hollis was home. The people who needed bread had their own names. They could still go to the well the next morning.” ᴹ

He looks at the man beside him.

“Whose name would go on the order you just described?” ᴹ

The man pulls his elbow closer to himself. He says the order would have to come from a decision of the delegations. From the end of the table the woman with the declaration says that he hasn't got a decision from hers. A few seats farther along, somebody closes the folder where they'd put his list.

Mau can hear them make those choices from where he's standing. He waits long enough to see the clerk put his pen back to the page.

“I would like to have dinner at my house,” Mau says. “I want the people I eat with to be able to go out the next day without somebody arresting them to get something from me. You can talk to me about what I've done. If you send people to my house to put me under your control, I'll take apart everything you use to do it. I'll come for the people who gave the orders and the ones who carried them out by choice.” ᴹ

“You'll come for people who had nothing to do with it,” another man says. “That's what it means when you say you'll come for a kingdom.” ᴰᵉ

“No. A soldier told he'll be killed if he comes home without my family is somebody I'd rather see go home alive. There's a difference between that person and the one who gave him the choice. I intend to find out which one I'm dealing with.” ᴹ

“You expect them to tell you which man gave the order?” the man asks. ᴰᵉ

“I'd like to hear from the people who were there. I asked whose name would go on the order because you have a chance to tell me yours now.” ᴹ

He leaves it there. He can see the woman at the end of the table looking towards the clerk. Her copy of the declaration still has a crease across it from the man who read it before he put it down.

“Is that an order you have authority to give today?” she asks. ᴰᵃ

The man says he brought it for discussion. The clerk puts those words down too. Mau looks at the two lines and thinks how much easier it would be to get home if everybody who had been asked to carry something out were allowed to ask to see it written down first.

The man in the chair has his left palm flat over the list now. Mau takes his hand off the back. The man pulls the page towards himself and straightens the chair without getting out of it.

“We can give the clerk the names of the places we mean,” he says. “The castle was only one.” ᴰᵉ

“Give him the people you want heard, too,” Mau says. “He has a place for them in the book.” ᴹ

There is a silence long enough for Mau to hear the attendant by the door set the rest of the papers down. Then the man starts with the castle a second time. Mau listens until he gets to the name of the crossing. It is the same thing he'd said before he explained what he wanted to do about Mau's house.

There is still a question about the declarations written out in Fern's hand. Mau looks over at Luminous. She has put her tea packet down beside her copy of the notice to keep somebody from gathering it up with the other papers. The clerk can see it. So can the woman who brought the creased declaration. Mau leaves it where it is.

Mau crosses the floor. It takes him a little longer walking than the first trip did. Testarossa moves her own chair back before he gets to her so he doesn't have to squeeze in behind it.

“If you need to take some distance to keep your people safe,” he says, where Luminous and Elmesia can hear him too, “I'll understand. You can tell me what you're doing later if you want. I know who you came here for.” ᴹ

Testarossa looks him over. “Tempest can speak for itself.” ᵀᵉ

“I know.” ᴹ

“So can I,” says Elmesia. ᴱˡ

She has her hand on the edge of the table. The man with her papers leans in to ask whether she wants him to stay for the rest. She tells him she'd like a copy of what's been entered first. He goes to speak to the clerk. Mau looks towards the door again and finds the other attendant still there.

He'd wanted to know what would happen if they tried to turn the invitation into permission to keep him. He thinks of telling Yuka the night before that he would leave. His coat feels light enough when he puts his hand on it. There's nothing he needs to wait for somebody to bring him.

Mau manages a bow to her that isn't very good. She has the grace to return it at the same depth. He says goodbye to Luminous, who tells him she'll still want the list. Then he looks at the clerk. The clerk had been waiting for another direction all morning. Mau thanks him for reading the letter the whole way through.

“You can't leave while this is being decided,” says the man by the eastern bench. ᴰᵉ

Mau thinks of his finger on the paper at breakfast. They had asked him to hear what would be said in a room with him in it. He'd heard something he could take home. He sits for the fraction of a second it'll take to give the ant somewhere to put its feet.

“We will come for your head,” the man says. ᴰᵉ

“You can try.” ᴹ

The chair they had gone to so much trouble to put there is empty for a fraction of a second. Then an ant is on it. It has its forelegs on the edge of the seat, right where Mau showed it the exit would be.
