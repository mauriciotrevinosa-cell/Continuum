She takes Beatrice to the end of the north corridor upstairs. Past Aira's room. Past the cellar stair. To the room with no name.

Mau follows, with Yuka. Echidna doesn't tell them not to.

It's a small room. It always has been. She opens it.

Behind it, the lamp. The chair. And the shelves, going back and back and up and up, much further than the corridor outside could possibly hold, row after row into a dimness that doesn't seem to have an end. The smell of old paper. The quiet of a place that has been quiet for a very long time.

Beatrice stands on the threshold.

But the other end of it is wrong. It goes back toward the shelves and then it bends, and runs, of all places, to the chair by the lamp. And there it doesn't hold. It lies across the space between them slack, like a rope tied off to a post that was supposed to fall down and didn't.

“Contracts are made for something,” he says. “I've carried one. I know how they sit on you. Yours was made so that you'd keep this place until you found something. Someone. After she was gone.” He doesn't look at Echidna. He doesn't have to. “It was made for a world where she was dead.” ᴹ

And then she laughs.

Not the small amused breath she gives at the tea table when someone has said something she intends to take apart. A real laugh, short and startled, out of somewhere she doesn't usually let anyone hear. She puts the back of her hand to her mouth, as if to catch it, and doesn't quite manage.

Beatrice stares at her.

Echidna doesn't explain. She just shakes her head slowly and goes on looking at Mau, with her eyes very bright, the way she looked at him the first time he told her about a door.

So Mau does the other thing.

He calls the wheel.

Not all of it. He doesn't need its weight in a room full of paper. Just the shape: the great eight-spoked thing, still, not turning, risen over his head among the shelves, so large that its upper rim vanishes into the dark between the stacks where the library goes on further than the room should allow.

Beatrice's mouth opens. Nothing comes out of it.

“I've done this twice for someone else,” he says. “Coco's mother was crystal. I let the crystal take me, and the wheel learned it, and she came out. And with Nazuna and Ko, I held what Ko's body does inside mine, with the wheel ready, so we'd know before anyone got hurt.” ᴹ

He looks up at it again.

“It only turns at what's happening to me,” he says. “So I'd have to take the knot on myself. Carry its weight for a moment, the way I carry Makima's rule. I've never done that with a contract. I don't know if it can learn one. It might turn. Or nothing happens and we try something else.” ᴹ

He looks back down at Beatrice.

“I don't know,” he says. “I'm telling you because you should know there are ways. Not because you have to choose one.” ᴹ

“I should very much like to know,” she says, “whether that is possible.” ᴱᶜ

“Later,” says Mau. ᴹ

“You're already in a house that's going to want you here,” he says. “It's what this house does. I don't know what the contract will say about that. But maybe you've already found it.” ᴹ

Beatrice doesn't say anything at all.

She sits very still in her circle of books with her hands clenched on the cover in her lap, and Mau can see, with the Six Eyes and without them, that four hundred years of waiting doesn't come off in one afternoon just because somebody has said something kind.

In the chair, Echidna has gone quiet too. She isn't looking at the place where the wheel was any more. She's looking at Beatrice, and for once she doesn't seem to have a question.

“Betty will think about it,” she says. “I suppose.” ᴮᵉᵃ

“Good.” He keeps his voice gentle. “Then until you decide, I'll make a smaller rule. Don't sit in here waiting. Not for anyone. Everyone you'd be waiting for is outside, in the yard, and they'd like you to come and watch Ori give Emilia a paper badge.” ᴹ

He waits.

“There's a place for you at supper,” he says. “And next time Ori wants you, can she knock on the hall door even if you've moved the library? You can tell her to go away yourself.” ᴹ

Beatrice looks at him for a long time.

Then she looks past him, at Echidna.

Echidna turns a page.

“He means it,” she says, without looking up. “He makes rules like this for everyone. They are very tiresome. You will find you keep them anyway.” ᴱᶜ

Beatrice closes the book in her lap.

“This is a terrible rule,” she says. ᴮᵉᵃ

“I know.” ᴹ

“Betty will be watching it very closely, I suppose.” ᴮᵉᵃ

“That's fine.” ᴹ

She gets up. She steps carefully out of the circle of books without disturbing any of them, and walks past him to the door, where Yuka is waiting, and stops.

Yuka holds out her hand.

Beatrice looks at it. Then, as if it is entirely her own idea, takes it.

Mau gets up and follows them out. At the door he looks back. Echidna has put her book down on her knee and is watching the empty doorway where Beatrice was, and her face, for a moment, before she notices him, is not interesting or sharp or curious at all.

Then she picks the book up again.

“Close it behind you,” she says. “There are a great many doors now. I shall have to learn which one is the draught.” ᴱᶜ

The Fort inducts Emilia at sunset.

Umaru has invited her to come and see the banner. Emilia is still asking what a Fort member actually does when Ori brings out the paper badge and asks if she would like one. She looks from the badge to the expectant faces and says yes.

Ori presides. She stands on a crate in the yard in her explorer clothes, with the banner from the castle day held up behind her by Milim and Umaru, and Chloe and the others in a row, and the rest of the house watching from the porch. Emilia stands in front of her, looking slightly bewildered, with Puck on her shoulder looking extremely amused.

“Do you,” says Ori, very seriously, “promise to defend the Fort?” ᴼ

“I—yes,” says Emilia. “I promise.” ᴱᵐ

“Even against Kamish?” ᴼ

Emilia looks at the enormous dragon lying in the orchard. Kamish opens one eye.

“Even against Kamish,” says Emilia bravely. ᴱᵐ

“And do you promise,” says Ori, “never to tell anyone where the tunnel is?” ᴼ

“There's a tunnel?” ᴱᵐ

“*There was never a tunnel*,” says Kamish, from the orchard, without lifting his head. ᴷ

The whole porch laughs.

Ori waits, with great dignity, for it to stop. Then she climbs down off her crate, and goes to Emilia, and reaches up, and pins a small paper badge to her coat: a castle, drawn in pencil, very carefully.

“Welcome to the Fort,” she says. ᴼ

Emilia looks down at the badge. Her face does something soft.

“Thank you,” she says. ᴱᵐ

Mau watches from the porch with Yuka in his lap.

She's laughing at Kamish. She's clapping for Emilia. She's leaning back against him with her whole weight and her head under his chin, warm and loose and happy, the way she's been every evening since the island.

He holds her and watches the yard.

Near the posts, Cross is holding the end of the banner where Milim left it. When Ori goes back for it, he brings it over without letting it drag. She stops him halfway and makes him carry it higher, because otherwise it looks like they're surrendering.

Cross does as he's told. He has to go on holding it that high while Ori explains to him how it came to be the official banner. By the time she's got to Kamish's part, Kamish has raised his head from the grass.

“Tell him what I got in exchange for the castle,” says Kamish. ᴷᵃ

“Ice cream,” says Ori. ᴼ

“Two,” says Kamish. ᴷᵃ

Yuka twists round on Mau's lap, laughing. He's laughing, too, mostly because he can already see Ori working out how to sell two ice creams as a victory for both parties.

“There wasn't a day I thought I'd get to show him all this,” Yuka says into his collar. ʸ

Mau can feel her smiling even after her voice goes unsteady. He turns his cheek into her hair and stays where he is until she's ready to go down and help Ori fold the banner.
