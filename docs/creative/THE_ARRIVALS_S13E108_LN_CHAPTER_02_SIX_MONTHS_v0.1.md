# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 83
## Chapter 10 — Six Months

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 83
**Reading order:** 10
**Source episode:** S13E108
**Continuity state:** B+422 ("65"). Yuka's point of view. Yuka follows through on the walk she promised Cross. They visit the village and stop at the orchard wall on their way back. He tells her what happened after she vanished: he looked for her; he was shot by a sorcerer while the talks were collapsing and nearly died; he thought she'd died of the tumour somewhere alone. She tells him the truth as canon has it: the operation worked, the tumour came out, and then the coin found her and brought her here. He's the only person in this world who saw her when she was dying and frightened and pretending not to be. She tells him about the harder training gate and remembers how Mau held her afterward. Cross asks about who decided to go in, a question she takes at face value. At home she asks Mau to listen while she tells him about Cross's search. Mau listens and holds her; part of what she says stays with him after she sleeps.

*When: B+422. Early walk to the village, back by lunch, then evening. Yuka wrote “65” and drew a small dragon beside it.*

Cross is waiting on the porch when she comes down with her tea. He picked the right morning. The fog has lifted from the road and a man is carrying a tray of buns out from the bakery when they get down to the village.

Yuka buys three. Cross lifts his hands before she can give him one.

“I have money.” ᶜʳ

“I have the buns.” She breaks a corner off the first one and tastes it. “You can buy my terrible breakfast next time.” ʸ

He accepts a bun. He waits until the man has got the tray onto the counter before he asks the price, then goes and buys one more, all on his own, as if it were an exercise she'd set him. The man asks him what he wants inside it. Cross looks at her. She tells him to try the red bean.

* * *

They stop at the orchard wall on the way back.

It's the same wall where Mau sat with Yuta, the night after the ring. Old stone, warm in the sun, with the two old trees behind it and the oval of light between them, humming quietly again now, back to the shape she gave it. Something warm in it, somewhere.

Cross sits too close. She moves along a little and rests a hand on the stone between them. He doesn't move closer again.

For a while he doesn't say anything. He looks at the trees. At the oval. At the house.

“You're alive,” he says. ᶜʳ

“You said I could stand up.” ʸ

“I'd been practicing what to say if you couldn't.” ᶜʳ

He picks at the moss on the wall with one long finger.

“I looked for you,” he says. ᶜʳ

She didn't know that.

She knew he'd been there, at the end, before. She knew he'd come to the hospital with her when she asked him to, because she couldn't ask anyone else, because if she'd asked Tsurugi he'd have known, and if she'd asked Granny it would have been real. Cross didn't know what a hospital was. He didn't know what a tumour was. He sat in the plastic chair in the corridor with his knees up round his ears and listened to the doctor say *six months* and didn't understand any of it except her face.

She remembers his face. Afterward. In the corridor. When she told him what it meant.

She didn't know he'd looked for her.

“When you were gone,” Cross says. “After. You just stopped being anywhere. Your brother didn't know. Nobody knew. And I thought—” ᶜʳ

He stops.

“I thought you'd gone somewhere to do it alone,” he says. “The dying. I thought you'd gone somewhere nobody could see, so nobody would have to watch. That's the kind of stupid thing you'd do.” ᶜʳ

Yuka doesn't say anything.

It *is* the kind of stupid thing she'd do. That's the worst part. He's right.

“So I looked,” says Cross. “For a long time. Everywhere. And then the talks happened. My brother's talks. And everything went wrong.” ᶜʳ

He lifts his shirt.

She flinches. She can't help it.

There's a scar on his side. Two of them, close together. Round and puckered and old.

“One of yours,” he says. “A sorcerer. With a gun. Right in the middle of my brother shaking hands with someone.” ᶜʳ

He lets the shirt fall.

“I nearly died,” he says. “I don't remember much of it. And when I woke up I was here, in this world, in the snow, with my brother, and all the others, and I thought: well. That's that. She's dead and I'm here and I'll never know where she went.” ᶜʳ

He looks at her.

“And then your brother started talking about a house,” he says. “And a man who was basically a king. And his *wife*. And he said your name.” ᶜʳ

Yuka looks at her hands.

“I'm sorry,” she says. ʸ

“Don't,” says Cross. ᶜʳ

“I didn't know you'd—” ʸ

“Don't,” he says again. “I don't want sorry. I want to know what happened. How you're alive. You said six months.” ᶜʳ

So she tells him.

She tells him what happened. She and Mau have spent too much time learning how to say difficult things to each other for her to turn the operation into an easy little miracle now.

She tells him about the operation. The one the doctors said probably wouldn't work, that she said yes to anyway, because six months of waiting seemed worse than a chance. She tells him about waking up afterward with her head shaved and bandaged and a doctor at the end of the bed with a face she didn't understand at first, because it was the face of someone who couldn't believe their luck.

“It came out,” she says. “All of it. They got all of it.” ʸ

Cross is very still.

“And then?” he says. ᶜʳ

“And then I went home,” she says. “To recover. To my room. And my ring was across the room, off the cord, on the desk, and I was going to get it. And something—found me. A coin. And I was here.” ʸ

She looks at the house. At the porch.

“And then I was here,” she says again. ʸ

Cross doesn't say anything for a long time.

He looks at her. Really looks. At her face, her hair, which has grown back long, her hands. As if he's checking. As if he's making sure every part of her is still there.

She lets him take a minute. She can think of a dozen things to say about what her life is like now. None of them are quite as useful as the fact that she ate half a hot bun on the way up the hill and left him to keep up.

“You were so scared,” Cross says. ᶜʳ

She goes still.

“In the corridor,” he says. “After the doctor. You were so scared. You kept laughing. You kept telling me it was fine. You told me it was *fine*, Yuka. Six months. And your hands were shaking so much you couldn't open the door.” ᶜʳ

He looks away.

“I didn't know what a tumour was,” he says. “But I knew what your hands were doing.” ᶜʳ

She remembers the door. In her mind it has always been the kind that opens when you lean on it. Now she can see the little silver catch and her own hand trying twice to turn it. She'd thought he'd been looking for the stairs.

“You remember that?” she asks. ʸ

“I remember being too slow to open it for you.” ᶜʳ

“You got it in the end.” ʸ

She looks at him. At his hard closed face, looking at the trees.

“Thank you,” she says. ʸ

He looks back at her, surprised.

“For coming,” she says. “To the hospital. I never said. You were the only one I could ask.” ʸ

Cross doesn't say anything.

But his face does something she's never seen it do. It opens. Just slightly. Just for a moment. Like a door left ajar.

Then it closes again, and he looks at the trees.

Cross points at a notch on the wall as they get down. It's wide enough for three of her fingers.

“Tsurugi said you and Mau fought something in a gate. He said there were three of you. I mostly remember the part where he said you were fine.” ᶜʳ

“Oh, sure. I was fine once we got home.” ʸ

“What about while you were there?” ᶜʳ

She looks down at her thumb, rolling the crumbs at the bottom of the paper into a corner. It comes back in an ugly little piece. A breath that ought to have gone all the way in. The sound of the air moving where there was nowhere to move to.

“I thought I might die,” she says. “Yuta got the thing off me. Mau put up Infinity for me while he already had something else running. He was next to me the first second he could be.” ʸ

Cross stops walking. “Was going in your idea?” ᶜʳ

“I wanted to lead. I saw an opening and went before Yuta was ready. I should've waited for him.” ʸ

She twists the paper closed. She could tell him about the look on Mau's face afterward, the way she'd kept her hands round his neck even after they'd sat down because she was still working out what to do with all the relief. That part she wants to keep for the two of them.

“For the record,” she tells Cross, “I'm planning to do a great many boring things now that I get to.” ʸ

“Like buy the wrong number of buns,” he says. ᶜʳ

“I bought the right number.” ʸ

At the orchard they find Ori standing on the wall with both arms out, walking the top of it. Beru is taking a step every time she does. His hands are behind his back. This looks as though it has taken a great deal of practice.

“How do I get down on the other side?” Ori asks. ᴼ

“There’s a stone below your left foot,” says Cross. “Back up one step. I'll show you.” ᶜʳ

He goes over just below her, sets his boot on the stone and holds it there so she can see it from above. She gives Beru a look. He keeps his hands behind him while she finds it herself.

“We've been going all the way round,” she tells Cross when she's on the ground. “Are there more stones like this?” ᴼ

“Three on this wall. I can show you the other ones later.” ᶜʳ

Ori looks over at Yuka. “Did you have a good walk?” ᴼ

“I did. I brought Mau a bun. You can have anything you find that fell out of the paper.” ʸ

Ori puts her hand in. It comes out dusted with sugar. “It's a very small bun,” she tells Beru, and licks each finger in turn. ᴼ

Cross walks with them as far as the training posts. By the time Yuka reaches the porch he has a stick in his hand and Ori has him drawing the wall in the dirt so she can put the three steps on her map. When Mau looks up from his tea, she holds the bun high to show him that at least one of her errands has arrived intact.

She puts the parcel down on the arm of the chair and climbs into his lap. She gets one good breath with her face against his shoulder before he asks if the bun belongs to him or if it's holding his side of the chair.

“Hi,” he says. ᴹ

“Hi,” she says into his shoulder. “It's yours. Can I have a bite?” ʸ

He puts his arms around her. “How was the walk?” ᴹ

“Good. More than I was expecting. I'll tell you tonight, okay?” ʸ

They split the bun. He gives her the half with the sugar still stuck to the top. She gives him the corner of that half in return. By lunch the paper has gone translucent on the arm of the chair and neither of them has gotten up to throw it away.

In the evening Shuna sends Mau to the kitchen ahead of her to cut carrots. Yuka follows him through the swing door with two spoons and no intention of using either of them.

“It's unfair if you stand there with both hands occupied,” she tells him. ʸ

He slides the first slice toward her without interrupting his work. She eats it. He slides the second one. The third one she goes after before it clears the knife, and he lifts the blade away until he can put the piece in her hand.

“Predatory,” he says. ᴹ

“Married.” ʸ

“Then take the good spoon and taste this for me.” ᴹ

She lifts the pot lid with a cloth. It takes her a while to fish a piece of potato out without letting all the broth run through the spoon. Mau comes over to see what she's doing. He could get his own spoon out of her other hand, where it's still tucked between two fingers. Instead he waits for her to blow on it and hold it up to him.

“Tastes like it needs salt,” he says. ᴹ

“Really?” She dips the same spoon and tries it. “I think you just wanted another bite.” ʸ

“I was hoping you'd give me your opinion first.” ᴹ

She puts her hip against his, reaches around him and turns the heat down the way Shuna taught them both. “Give us another minute,” she calls when she hears Shuna at the door. ʸ

“Nothing,” says Mau over Yuka's shoulder, and Yuka loses the argument with the spoon laughing.

At bedtime, she's lying on his chest in the dark, drawing shapes on his arm.

“Cross told me what happened,” she says. ʸ

“Yeah?” ᴹ

“After I was gone. He looked for me. He got shot. He thought I was dead.” ʸ

Mau's hand stops moving on her back for a second. Then starts again.

“That's a lot,” he says. ᴹ

“It is a lot.” ʸ

She waits a moment before continuing. His hand is moving again, and she takes the small nod beneath her cheek as an invitation to go on.

“You know when I told you he talked to me about dinner till I could stand up?” she asks. “He remembers my hands shaking at the door. I didn't even know he could see them. I was working so hard to keep them still.” ʸ

Mau puts his hand over the one moving on his arm. Waits until she turns it over.

“Did you get to tell him what it meant to you?” he asks. ᴹ

“Yes.” It comes out on the same breath as a yawn. “And I got to walk home afterward. That was nice.” ʸ

His thumb strokes the side of hers.

She yawns. She settles her head more comfortably on his chest. She's warm and full and sleepy and happy, and she has an old friend back, and her husband's hand is on her back, and everything is fine.

“I'm glad he's alive,” she says. ʸ

“Me too,” says Mau. ᴹ

She falls asleep a minute later.

---

Mau stays awake after her breathing settles. His hand keeps moving on her back, slowly, steadily. Once she stirs and presses closer; he bends his mouth to her hair until she relaxes again.

He thinks about being able to see her hands and knowing why they shook. About how grateful he is that someone was there who finally understood. From the stories she'd told him before Cross arrived, he'd taken that dinner conversation for a kindness somebody might forget by morning.

He can see now what it would have meant to lose her after that.

He kisses the crown of her head again, even though she isn't awake to feel it.
