# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 84
## Chapter 19 — Rain and Doors

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 84
**Reading order:** 19
**Source episode:** S13E128
**Continuity state:** B+443 ("44"). Rain all day. The house stays in. Mau and Yuka take the window seat in Frieren's room; Yuka in his lap with a blanket; he reads aloud from one of the translations by the window while Frieren reads her own book and Beatrice, keeping Mau's rule, is out of the library and on the floor with Ori and a pile of books. Since Beatrice opened Echidna's library onto doors all over the house, any door might be a shelf: Ori opens the linen cupboard and finds poetry. Echidna steps out of the pantry with a book and joins them uninvited. Beatrice and Echidna argue about a translation; it is the first time anyone has heard them argue, and it sounds like family. Nothing happens. That's the point.

*When: B+443. "44." Under it, in Ori's hand: "RAIN (inside day)". In Yuka's: "window seat".*

It rains from before dawn.

Not a storm. The steady, patient kind that sets in like it means to stay the week, grey on grey on grey, drumming on the porch roof and running off the eaves in strings and filling the yard with puddles that Ori has already been out to measure in her boots and been fetched in from.

Nobody goes anywhere.

The house turns inward the way it does on rain days. Umaru declares a tournament before breakfast is over. Senku disappears into the workshop with Mai and something that hisses. Shuna starts bread. Gojo lies on the floor of the common room with his blindfold off and his arms flung out and announces that he's *going to do nothing all day, competitively*, and Milim takes this as a challenge.

Yuka takes Mau by the hand after breakfast and leads him upstairs without a word, past their room, along the corridor, to Frieren's.

* * *

The window seat in Frieren's room is the best place in the house to be when it's raining.

Everybody knows it. It's deep and wide and padded, with cushions that Frieren has been quietly collecting for years, set into the big window that looks out over the orchard. When it rains, you can sit there with your back against the side and your feet up and watch the water run down the glass and the pear trees going soft and grey beyond it, and hear the rain on the roof right above your head, and feel like the only dry thing in the world.

Frieren is in her chair by the shelves with a book.

She looks up when they come in. At Yuka, in the hoodie, leading Mau by the hand. At Mau, letting himself be led.

She doesn't say anything. She just moves her teacup an inch along the side table, to make room for two more, and goes back to her book.

Yuka climbs into the window seat. She arranges the cushions. She arranges the blanket. Then she looks at Mau, standing there, and points at the cushions behind her, very firmly, the way Ori points at Kamish.

He sits.

She climbs into his lap, sideways, with her back against his chest and her head under his chin and her legs along the seat, and pulls the blanket over both of them.

“Read,” she says. ʸ

“Read what?” ᴹ

She reaches over to the little shelf beside the window seat, the one with the translations Frieren lets people borrow, and takes one without looking, and puts it in his hands.

“That,” she says. ʸ

He opens the book over her knees. She's got her toes tucked away, but her fingers are cold from the walk over. He takes her hand in his and slips it under the blanket against his ribs. It warms slowly there, one knuckle at a time.

“Do you want me to do voices?” he asks. ᴹ

“I want the voice you use when it's just me.” ʸ

Frieren turns the next page of her own book with great composure.

Mau bends to kiss the top of Yuka's ear. “This one?” ᴹ

Yuka pulls the blanket up over her smile. She makes him say the first sentence twice.

* * *

He reads.

It's a book of stories from somewhere he's never been. Old ones. A girl who goes looking for the wind. A fox who borrows a coat. A king who forgets his own name and has to ask everyone in his kingdom until somebody remembers it for him. Short things, simple, the kind you'd read to a child.

He reads them out loud, slowly, with the rain on the roof above them.

Yuka doesn't say anything. She lies against him with her eyes half closed and her hand on his arm where it holds the book, and every so often, at a good part, her fingers tighten a little. When the king forgets his name, they tighten a lot.

Frieren turns a page in her chair.

He can feel her listening.

* * *

Beatrice arrives halfway through the fox.

She comes through Frieren's door. Not the door from the corridor. The other one, the old cupboard door in the corner, which used to have Frieren's winter boots in it and which now, since Beatrice put her hand on the frame in the room with no name and pinched the world into a seam, opens onto shelves. Rows of them. Going back and back into a soft dimness much further than the corner of a bedroom should allow.

She comes out of the shelves with an armful of books and a look of deep disapproval.

“The rain,” she announces, “is very loud in the library, in fact.” ᴮᵉᵃ

“It's raining everywhere,” says Frieren, without looking up. ᶠ

“It's louder in the library.” ᴮᵉᵃ

“It isn't. There's no roof on the library. The library is in a different place.” ᶠ

Beatrice looks at her for a long moment with her chin up.

“Betty would *prefer* company,” she says, “I suppose.” ᴮᵉᵃ

And she sits down on the floor of Frieren's room, right there, in the middle of the rug, with her armful of books, and opens the top one.

Mau doesn't stop reading. But he feels something warm go through his chest.

*She's keeping the rule*, says Ciel. *She's out. She chose to come out.* ᶜ

*I know*, he says. ᴹ

* * *

Ori arrives at the fox's coat.

She comes in from the corridor in her socks, damp at the edges from being fetched in from the puddles, and sees Beatrice on the rug, and sits down beside her without asking, the way she sits beside everyone.

Beatrice looks at her.

Ori looks at the pile of books.

“Can I have one,” says Ori. ᴼ

“They are very difficult books, in fact,” says Beatrice. “Most of them are not for children.” ᴮᵉᵃ

“I'll have a difficult one,” says Ori. ᴼ

Beatrice regards her with suspicion for some time. Then she selects a book from the pile, inspects it, puts it back, selects another, and hands it over with enormous ceremony.

Ori opens it. Frowns at it. Turns it the right way up.

“It's all pictures of mushrooms,” she says. ᴼ

“It is a very *difficult* book about mushrooms,” says Beatrice. ᴮᵉᵃ

Ori considers this. Then she lies down on her stomach on the rug and starts to read it with tremendous seriousness, kicking her feet.

* * *

The doors are a problem, in a good way.

Mau hadn't understood, the day Beatrice did it, quite how many doors she'd done it to. He finds out over the course of the afternoon. Every so often somebody in the house opens a door expecting one thing and finds shelves instead.

Umaru opens the door of the cupboard under the stairs, looking for the spare game controllers, and finds a shelf of books about the sea, and is gone for an hour.

Milim opens the linen cupboard looking for a blanket to make a fort and finds poetry. A great deal of poetry. She comes and stands in Frieren's doorway with a volume in each hand and an expression of betrayal.

“The *towels*,” she says. “Where did the towels *go*.” ᴹⁱ

“They're on the shelf below,” says Beatrice, without looking up from her book. “Betty is not a monster.” ᴮᵉᵃ

And then, in the middle of the king who forgot his name, the pantry door downstairs opens, and somebody comes up the stairs, and along the corridor, and stops in Frieren's doorway.

Echidna.

* * *

She has a book under her arm. She's looking at the room: at Frieren in her chair, at Beatrice and Ori on the rug, at Mau in the window seat with Yuka in his lap and the blanket over them both. At the rain on the window. At the teacups.

“I came through the pantry,” she says. “I wasn't expecting to. I was looking for a commentary on the third chapter of the thing Frieren lent me, and I opened the wrong door, and found myself standing next to the flour.” ᴱᶜ

She looks at Beatrice.

“You've connected it to the *pantry*,” she says. ᴱᶜ

“The pantry is a very good door, in fact,” says Beatrice. “Shuna uses it often. Shuna likes books.” ᴮᵉᵃ

Echidna considers this.

Then she comes into the room without being invited, and sits down in the other chair by the shelves, beside Frieren's, and opens her book.

Nobody tells her to leave.

* * *

They argue about a translation around four o'clock.

It starts with Echidna, reading her commentary, saying, half to herself, “That's wrong,” and Beatrice, on the rug, saying immediately, without looking up, “It is not wrong.” And Echidna saying, “You haven't seen which part I'm reading,” and Beatrice saying, “It doesn't matter which part. You always say that one's wrong. It isn't.” And then they're off.

Mau doesn't understand any of it. It's about a word in a language he's never heard of, in a book older than the house, and whether it means *waiting* or *keeping*. They go at it for twenty minutes. Beatrice sits up on the rug with her chin out. Echidna leans forward in her chair with her book on her knee. Their voices go up. Frieren doesn't look up once. Ori watches them with her mouth open, back and forth, like a game.

He keeps reading, quietly, under it. Yuka doesn't listen to him anymore. She's listening to them.

“They sound like Maki and Mai,” Yuka whispers. ʸ

They do. That's exactly what they sound like. Not like strangers. Not like a keeper and the one who made her. Like two people who've had the same argument a hundred times, at a hundred tables, and will have it a hundred more, and don't mind.

Like family.

At the end of it, Echidna sits back in her chair.

“Keeping, then,” she says. ᴱᶜ

“Keeping,” says Beatrice, with deep satisfaction, and goes back to her book. ᴮᵉᵃ

Then, after a moment, without looking up:

“It can mean both, in fact,” she says. “Betty only argues so you'll stay.” ᴮᵉᵃ

Echidna doesn't say anything.

But she doesn't leave, either. Not until supper.

* * *

The rain doesn't stop.

He finishes the book of stories as the light goes. The king remembers his name in the end. Somebody he'd forgotten, an old woman in a kitchen, remembers it for him, and says it to him, and he goes home.

Yuka is asleep by then. Has been for the last two stories. Heavy and warm against his chest, breathing slow, with her hand still on his arm.

He closes the book.

Frieren looks up from her chair, for the first time all afternoon. She looks at Yuka asleep, and at him, and at Beatrice and Ori asleep on the rug in a tangle of books about mushrooms, and at Echidna in the other chair, reading, pretending not to notice any of it.

“That's a good day,” Frieren says. Quietly. ᶠ

He thinks about it.

Nothing happened. Nobody fought. Nobody was in danger. Nobody needed anything. It rained, and they read, and two women argued about a word, and everybody fell asleep.

“Yes,” he says. ᴹ

Frieren nods. She goes back to her book.

Outside, the rain goes on and on, grey on grey on grey, and he sits in the window seat with his wife asleep on him, and listens to it, and doesn't check on anyone at all.
