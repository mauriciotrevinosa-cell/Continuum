## Chapter 21 — Applause

*When: B+445. "42." Under it, in Makima's hand, which nobody has ever seen in the notebook before: a small drawing of a wheel, and two exclamation marks.*

“I've been meaning to tell you,” says Makima, “about the wheel.” ᴹᵏ

They're at the table in the inner room. It's early. Mau came in to sit with Ciel and Noise for a while before breakfast, the way he's started doing every morning now, not because anything's wrong, just because they're there. Makima came in a few minutes after him and sat down across the table, which she doesn't usually do, and picked up the cup, which she never does, and turned it round in her hands.

She looks pleased. Makima doesn't look pleased often. When she does, it's the small private pleasure of someone who has seen something done very well.

“What wheel?” he says. ᴹ

Makima stops turning the cup.

“Yuka's,” she says. “On the orchard wall. Four days ago.” ᴹᵏ

He looks at her blankly.

Makima looks at him for a long moment. Then she looks at Ciel. Ciel looks at the table.

“He doesn't know,” says Makima. ᴹᵏ

“He was in DIO's room,” says Ciel. “And then in Elmesia's garden. And then in the clinic. And then he was busy being forgiven.” ᶜ

“Nobody told him,” says Makima. ᴹᵏ

“Yuka didn't want to make it about Cross,” says Ciel. “She said so. She wanted the week to be about them.” ᶜ

Mau puts his hands flat on the table.

“Told me what?” he says. ᴹ

* * *

Makima tells him about the wheel first.

She tells it the way she told the war, with the same precise report-voice, but there's something under it this time that he's never heard. Admiration. Real admiration. She tells him about Yuka on the orchard wall, and Cross leaning on it beside her, and the wheel coming up over Yuka's head in the afternoon light, enormous, old, and turning once with a click that carried across the whole yard.

“I heard it from the north wing,” says Makima. “I thought someone was about to die. I came down at a run.” ᴹᵏ

She puts the cup down.

“She didn't need me,” she says. “She stood up off the wall with that thing over her head and told him *say it again*, very calmly, in a voice like a knife. And he didn't. He couldn't. And then she took him apart. Everything he'd been doing. All of it, named, out loud, in front of him. *You're using it like a knife.*” ᴹᵏ

Makima's mouth does something.

“I've been applauding it ever since,” she says. “Quietly. In my room. It was magnificent.” ᴹᵏ

He's very still.

“What had he been doing?” he says. ᴹ

* * *

Makima looks at him for a long moment.

She's deciding something. He can feel her deciding, the way he could feel Yuka deciding in the clinic chair. She's weighing it. What Yuka told her on the wall, afterwards, crying, with the sun going down. What Yuka would want. What Mau would want. What's true.

“She told me on the wall,” says Makima, “after. Not just that day. All of it. What he'd been saying to her for two weeks.” ᴹᵏ

“You don't have to tell me,” he says. ᴹ

“No,” says Makima. “I don't.” ᴹᵏ

She tells him anyway.

* * *

She tells him about *before you*. He knew about that.

She tells him about *you just stopped being anywhere*. He knew that too.

She tells him about the doorways. *He goes to everyone, and you sit in doorways.* He didn't know that one. He feels it go in, under his ribs, cold.

And then she tells him the last thing.

“He said going back to you was stupid,” says Makima. “When she was thinking about it, that week. When she was trying to work out how to come to you. He said it was stupid. That everyone could see you'd left her already, in every way except saying it, and she was the only one who couldn't.” ᴹᵏ

Mau doesn't move.

“And then he said,” Makima goes on, in the same calm voice, “that it was like the day she got the diagnosis. In the hospital. The cancer. He said she'd sat there and been told she was dying, and the doctors had told her what she could do, the treatment, and she'd said no, she'd rather have the time. He said going back to you was the same thing. Getting the diagnosis and refusing to treat it. Just because you're used to it. Just because it's familiar. Choosing to stay in the thing that's killing you because you're too frightened to leave it.” ᴹᵏ

The inner room is completely silent.

Ciel has gone very still. Noise, by the wall, has stopped being an outline and become something solider and darker, the way he does when he's about to stand in front of something.

“He compared me,” says Mau, very quietly, “to her cancer.” ᴹ

“Yes,” says Makima. ᴹᵏ

* * *

He doesn't remember standing up.

He's in the inner room, at the table, with his hands flat on the wood, and then he's in the clinic, in the chair by the window where his body was sitting, and then he's on his feet, and the chair is on its back on the floor behind him, and he's walking.

Out of the clinic. Across the yard. Toward the river.

Something has gone up in him. He doesn't have a word for it. It's not the cold thing from the yard, the floor going, the well. It's the opposite. It's hot. It's a rocket. It went up from somewhere very deep, past every door he keeps things behind, straight up the middle of him, and it's still going, and the whole of him is lit up in its light.

*She was dying.*

*She was alone in a hospital corridor with her hands shaking too much to open a door and somebody told her she was going to die, and she chose the time, she chose to have the time, and he took that, he took the worst day of her life, and he made it about ME—*

The air bends round him.

He can feel it. He isn't doing it on purpose. It's just happening, the way it happened in the basin, the infinity coming up round him on its own, the grass flattening in a ring at his feet as he walks, the dew lifting off it. Something cracks in the training post as he passes it. He doesn't look.

*Mau*, says Ciel. Inside. Very fast. *Mau. Stop. You're—* ᶜ

He doesn't stop.

He knows where Cross is. He can feel him. Down at the river, past the orchard, with Maru, skipping stones. He can feel exactly where he is, the way he could feel the dog-thing on the rim of the basin. He can feel every step between them.

He means it.

For about forty seconds, walking across his own yard in the morning sun, he completely means it.

* * *

The house is already there.

He sees it without seeing it. They didn't run. They were just there, the way the house is always there, already in position, as if they'd been waiting for this since the morning Makima came down at a run from the north wing.

Gojo on the orchard wall. Sitting, legs dangling, perfectly relaxed. Blindfold pushed up. Watching Mau come across the yard with those eyes that see everything, and not smiling at all.

Maki at the corner of the workshop with her staff, not raised. Just held.

Jin-Woo at the gate to the river path. Standing in the middle of it. Not in the way, exactly. Just there.

Beru at the edge of the orchard, wings half open, very still.

And behind Mau, a step behind his right shoulder, where she always is: Makima. She came out of the clinic after him. She hasn't tried to stop him. She's just there.

None of them move.

They don't need to. Because Yuka is in front of him.

* * *

She comes from the porch. He didn't see her. She's in the hoodie, barefoot, with a piece of toast in one hand that she's forgotten about, and she walks straight across the yard into the path between him and the river and stops.

He doesn't stop.

She doesn't move.

He walks right up to her. The air is still bending round him. The infinity is still up, on its own, and she walks into it.

She walks into it like it isn't there. Because it isn't there for her. It's never been there for her. It opens round her the way it always has, the way it opens for nobody else in the world, and she steps through it and puts her hand flat on his chest.

Right in the middle. Over his heart. Where the rocket is.

He stops.

* * *

“Makima told you,” she says. ʸ

It isn't a question.

He can't answer. His jaw is locked. Everything in him is still going up, and up, and her hand is on his chest, and it's like pressing your palm against a door that something enormous is pushing on from the other side.

She doesn't push back. She just holds her hand there.

“I know,” she says. “I know what he said. I was there. I heard it first.” ʸ

She looks up at him. Her face is very calm. Calmer than he's ever seen it.

“And I already dealt with it,” she says. “I called the wheel. I told him *say it again*. He didn't. I told him he was using it like a knife. I told him never again. He said sorry. He meant it, I think. And I said friend, if he keeps it that. And he went with Maru.” ʸ

She takes a breath.

“It's done,” she says. “I did it. It was mine to do, and I did it, and it's done.” ʸ

Her hand is still on his chest.

“Please don't take it,” she says. “That's mine. Like the apology. Don't take it off me and do it again, bigger. I don't need you to go to the river. I need you to stay here. With me.” ʸ

* * *

The rocket stops.

Not all at once. It doesn't come down. It just stops going up. It hangs there, in the middle of him, very bright, and slowly, slowly, starts to go out.

The air stops bending.

The infinity folds down. On its own, the way it came up. The ring of flattened grass at his feet stays flattened, with the dew gone off it. The training post he passed has a long split down one side.

He looks down at her.

She's still holding the piece of toast in her other hand. There's jam on it.

“You've got toast,” he says. His voice comes out very strange. ᴹ

“I was having breakfast,” says Yuka. “Somebody started walking across the yard like the end of the world.” ʸ

He breathes out. A long one. All the way.

He puts his hand over hers, on his chest.

“Okay,” he says. ᴹ

The good kind.

* * *

Behind him, on the orchard wall, Gojo pulls his blindfold back down.

“Shame,” he says, to nobody in particular. “I'd have paid to watch.” ᴳ

“You would *not*,” says Maki, from the workshop corner, lowering her staff. ᴹᵃᵏ

“I'd have paid to watch him get *stopped*,” Gojo amends. “By a girl with toast. Best thing I've seen all week.” ᴳ

At the river gate, Jin-Woo steps out of the middle of the path. He doesn't say anything. He just looks at Mau for a moment, and nods once, the way you'd nod to someone who's put down something heavy, and goes back inside.

Beru folds his wings.

Makima, a step behind Mau's shoulder, lets out a long slow breath that he feels more than hears.

*I'm sorry*, she says. Inside. *I thought you should know. I didn't think about what you'd do with it.* ᴹᵏ

*I'm glad you told me*, he says. And means it. *I'm glad she stopped me.* ᴹ

*So am I*, says Makima. *I'd have had to go with you. It would have been very awkward.* ᴹᵏ

He almost laughs.

* * *

Yuka takes him back inside.

By the hand. Across the yard, past the split training post, up the porch steps, into the kitchen, where breakfast is still on the table and Shuna is standing by the stove with a ladle in her hand and a face like thunder, which clears, slowly, when she sees them come in.

Yuka sits him down in his chair.

She sits down in hers, beside him. She puts the toast on his plate.

“Eat that,” she says. “It's got jam on. You like jam.” ʸ

He eats it.

She steals the pickles off his plate.

Under the table, very quietly, she puts her foot on his, and leaves it there, all through breakfast, as if to make sure he doesn't get up and walk anywhere again.

He doesn't.
