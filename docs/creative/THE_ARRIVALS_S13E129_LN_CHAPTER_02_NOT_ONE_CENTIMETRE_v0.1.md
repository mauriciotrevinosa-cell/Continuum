# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 84
## Chapter 25 — Not One Centimetre

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 84
**Reading order:** 25
**Source episode:** S13E129
**Continuity state:** B+448 ("39"). Cross heard about the yard: Mau walking toward the river, and Yuka stopping him with toast in her hand. He comes to the workshop door in the morning, where Mau is helping Mai, and asks to be forgiven. Mau tells him the truth: the only reason Cross is still standing is what he did for Yuka once, in a corridor, when nobody else saw her. Otherwise Mau would already have sent him back. Cross can stay; he can live here, eat here, share rooms with everyone. In a group conversation they'll speak. But he isn't to greet Mau or address him otherwise, and Mau won't pretend he's fine with him. Cross accepts it too easily. Mau notices. Mai notices him noticing. Seed: Cross may one day try to win Mau the way Makima once tried to win people. Mau isn't going to give him a centimetre.

*When: B+448. "39." Mai's workshop, mid-morning. Mai wrote nothing in the notebook. Mai never writes in the notebook. She left a small pressed flower on the page instead.*

He's in the workshop when Cross comes.

It's the first time he's been there since the letter. He came after breakfast and stood in the doorway with his hands in his pockets, and Mai looked up from her bench and looked at him for a long time over the top of the clasp she was filing, and said, “You read too much into things, in letters,” and then, “Hold this,” and handed him one end of a bolt of cloth. ᴹᵃ

That was an hour ago. He's still holding it.

She hasn't said anything else about the letter. She's said a great deal about the cloth, which is for something she won't tell him, and about the way he's holding it, which is wrong, and about the light, which is bad. She's said nothing about the line between them, open again, quiet and steady, which she hasn't touched once and which he can feel her not touching, deliberately, the way you'd leave someone's gift unopened on the table so they can see you're saving it.

Then the door opens and Cross is standing in it.

* * *

Mai stops filing.

Mau doesn't turn round. He doesn't need to. He can feel who it is, the way he could feel him at the river yesterday, the way he could feel the dog-thing on the rim of the basin.

“Can I talk to you,” says Cross. ᶜʳ

To Mau. Not Mai.

Mau lowers his end of the cloth. Carefully, so it doesn't drag on the floor, because Mai would kill him. He folds it over the end of the bench.

Then he turns round.

* * *

Cross looks worse than he did at the gate.

He's lost weight somewhere in the last two weeks, or stopped sleeping, or both. He's standing in the workshop doorway with his hands at his sides, open, the way his people hold them when they want you to see they aren't going to move fast. His face is very carefully arranged.

“I heard about yesterday,” he says. “The yard. Maru told me.” ᶜʳ

Mau doesn't say anything.

“He said you were coming to the river,” says Cross. “He said you were coming for me. He said—he said the grass went flat round you. He said Yuka stopped you with a piece of toast.” ᶜʳ

Something moves in his face.

“I'd have deserved it,” he says. “Whatever you were going to do. I know I would. I said things to her I didn't—that I shouldn't have—I said the thing about the hospital. I *know*.” ᶜʳ

He takes a breath.

“I'm sorry,” he says. “I'm asking you to forgive me. I know I've got no right to. But I'm asking. I want—I'd like to start again. With you. Properly. I'd like to be—” ᶜʳ

He stops. He looks for the word.

“Not enemies,” he says. ᶜʳ

* * *

Mau looks at him for a long time.

He thinks about the yard. About the rocket going up in him, straight up the middle, past every door. About walking toward the river with the grass going flat at his feet and meaning it. About forty seconds of meaning it.

About Yuka's hand on his chest. *That's mine. Don't take it off me and do it again, bigger.*

He doesn't take it.

“You want to know why you're still standing,” he says. ᴹ

Cross goes still.

“It isn't because I forgave you,” says Mau. “I haven't. I'm not going to. Not today.” ᴹ

He keeps his voice very quiet. Very even.

“It's because of one thing,” he says. “Once. A long time ago, in another world, in a hospital corridor, my wife was the most frightened she'd ever been in her life, and nobody saw it. Not her brother. Nobody. And you did. You sat in a plastic chair with your knees up round your ears and you saw her, and you didn't look away.” ᴹ

He watches that land.

“That's the only reason,” says Mau. “That's the only thing standing between you and me. If you'd never done that, I'd already have sent you back. To the north. To wherever your people are. I wouldn't have walked to the river. I'd have just done it.” ᴹ

Cross has gone very pale.

* * *

“You can stay,” Mau says. ᴹ

Cross blinks.

“You can live here,” says Mau. “With Maru. With your people. You can eat at the table and sleep under this roof and walk in this yard and be in the same room as everyone else. I'm not going to send you away. Yuka called you a friend if you keep it that, and that's hers to give, and I'm not going to take it off her.” ᴹ

He pauses.

“But not with me,” he says. ᴹ

Cross opens his mouth.

“Don't greet me,” says Mau. “In the morning. In the yard. Don't say my name. Don't come and find me. Don't stand in doorways and ask to talk.” ᴹ

He doesn't raise his voice.

“If we're in a group, and the group's talking, and you've got something to say, say it. I'll answer. I'm not going to make a scene at the table in front of Ori. But that's all it is. That's all it's going to be.” ᴹ

He looks Cross in the eye.

“I'm not going to pretend I'm all right with you,” says Mau. “I'm not. I'm not going to smile at you over breakfast so everyone feels better. I spent two weeks pretending I was all right, and it nearly cost me everything. I'm not doing it again. Not for you.” ᴹ

* * *

Cross doesn't say anything for a long moment.

Then he nods.

“All right,” he says. ᶜʳ

That's it. Just that. *All right.* He doesn't argue. He doesn't push. He doesn't ask for anything else. He nods once, slowly, with his face very carefully arranged, and says *all right*, as if it's a perfectly reasonable thing to have been told, as if it doesn't cost him anything at all.

“That's fair,” he says. “That's more than fair. Thank you.” ᶜʳ

And he turns, and goes out of the workshop door, and closes it quietly behind him.

* * *

Mau stands there.

Something is wrong. He can't say what. He stands in the middle of Mai's workshop with the bolt of cloth folded over the bench behind him and looks at the closed door and turns it over in his head.

*All right.*

*That's fair. That's more than fair.*

*Thank you.*

Too easy. That's what it is. Too smooth. A man who walked into the workshop looking like he hadn't slept in a week, asking to start again, being told *no, not with me, not ever, don't even say my name*, and walking out saying *thank you*. Without a flicker. Without a crack. With his face so carefully arranged.

He's seen that before.

He's seen it on Makima. Before. In the beginning, when she still came to the outer post with her requests and her perfect calm, and agreed to every term he set, and thanked him for every limit, and went away, and came back, and agreed again, and every time she agreed she was a little closer. Not because she'd changed. Because she was patient. Because agreeing cost her nothing, and every *yes* was a step she didn't have to take by force.

He doesn't know that's what this is.

He doesn't know it isn't.

* * *

“You noticed,” says Mai. ᴹᵃ

He turns round.

She's sitting at her bench with the clasp in one hand and the file in the other, and she hasn't started filing again. She's looking at the door. Then at him.

“Noticed what?” he says. ᴹ

“Don't,” says Mai. “I watched your face. You noticed.” ᴹᵃ

He's quiet for a moment.

“It was too easy,” he says. ᴹ

Mai nods slowly.

“I used to do that,” she says. “When we were small, in that house. When I wanted something from the old men and couldn't get it. I'd stop asking. I'd say *yes, sir* and *thank you, sir* and go away very quietly. And everyone thought I'd given up.” ᴹᵃ

She puts the clasp down.

“I hadn't,” says Mai. “I was waiting for them to stop watching.” ᴹᵃ

She picks up the file again. She starts filing, slowly.

“Maybe he's just sorry,” she says. “Maybe he really means it. People do, sometimes. I'm not saying he doesn't.” ᴹᵃ

“But.” ᴹ

“But don't stop watching,” says Mai. “That's all.” ᴹᵃ

* * *

He picks the cloth back up.

He holds his end. Properly, this time. Mai tells him it's still wrong.

*Do you want me to keep an eye on him?* says Ciel, very quietly. *Not a report. Just—an eye.* ᶜ

He thinks about it. About Makima at the outer post, all those months ago, agreeing to everything. About what it cost, in the end, when she finally got close enough to do what she'd been waiting to do. Yuka in the clinic. The red gate. The trap.

About Makima now, at his shoulder, holding his hand on the road to the basin. Who changed. Who really did change, slowly, against everything she was.

People do. Sometimes.

*Yes*, he says. *Just an eye. Tell me if anything changes. Not a report. Tell me.* ᴹ

*I'll tell you*, says Ciel. ᶜ

And on the other side of the workshop wall, walking away across the yard toward the river with his hands in his pockets, Cross doesn't look back once.

Not once.

That's the other thing Mau notices.
