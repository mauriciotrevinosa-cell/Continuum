# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 84
## Chapter 26 — Swallowed Words

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 84
**Reading order:** 26
**Source episode:** S13E130
**Continuity state:** B+448 ("39"). Yuka told Yuta, Maki and Mai everything they did for her during the two weeks: Maki's "when has that ever meant fine", Mai's workshop mornings, Yuta sitting up with her. Mau asks the three of them to the workshop after supper and thanks them. He says he was wrong to swallow his words and he'll try not to do it again. Then he offers Mai a new contract. Everything stays the same: the cost of construction on his body, the CE his. But he gives up trying to stop her telling him everything she makes; instead it's written in: whenever she wants to create something, he'll listen, and pay attention. And the most important clause: he can't close her door, ever, because he trusts her blindly. Mai says she read his letter. Mai signs.

*When: B+448. "39." Under it, in Yuka's hand: "workshop (go)". It's an instruction, not a note.*

Yuka tells him after breakfast.

She doesn't say it like a confession. She says it while she's stealing the pickles, with her mouth half full, as if it's the most ordinary thing in the world.

“I told Maki and Yuta and Mai,” she says. “About the two weeks. What they did. For me.” ʸ

He puts his chopsticks down.

“What did they do?” ᴹ

Yuka looks at him. Then she looks across the table to where Maki is sitting with Yuta, arguing about something to do with a spear, and Mai is sitting at the end not eating, looking at a drawing of something that might be a clasp.

“Maki found me on the porch the morning you closed the doors,” says Yuka. “When I'd said *it's fine*. She said *when has that ever meant fine*. And she sat with me. All morning. She didn't make me talk. She just sat.” ʸ

She takes another pickle.

“Mai let me come to the workshop every morning,” she says. “The whole two weeks. Even when I was horrible. Even when I didn't help. She'd give me something to hold and let me sit there and not talk. She never asked. Not once.” ʸ

He's very still.

“And Yuta,” says Yuka, more quietly. “The night after the yard. When I said the worst thing. I couldn't sleep. I went down to the kitchen at three in the morning, and he was there. He'd heard me on the stairs. He made tea. He sat with me till it got light. He didn't say anything about you. Not one word. He just let me cry and kept refilling the cup.” ʸ

She puts the pickle down without eating it.

“Grandpa,” she says. ʸ

He looks across the table at Yuta, who is explaining something about the spear with both hands and has no idea he's being talked about.

“I didn't know,” Mau says. ᴹ

“No,” says Yuka. “You were in the clinic.” ʸ

She doesn't say it unkindly. She says it like a fact. Then she picks up the pickle again and eats it, and makes the face, and wipes her fingers on his sleeve.

“You should go and say thank you,” she says. “Properly. All three. Tonight.” ʸ

* * *

He asks them to the workshop after supper.

He doesn't say why. He just asks. Maki looks at him with her eyebrows up. Yuta says *of course* at once, the way Yuta always does. Mai looks at him for a long time over the top of her drawing and then says, “It's my workshop,” as if he's asked permission, and goes there first to clear a space.

When he comes in, she's cleared the bench. All of it. The cloth and the clasps and the files and the half-made things. She's put four stools round it. And a lamp. And a pot of tea, which nobody asked her to make.

Maki is sitting on one stool with her staff across her knees. Yuta is on another. Mai is behind the bench, standing, with her arms folded, the way she stands when she isn't sure what's going to happen in her own room.

He sits on the fourth stool.

He looks at them.

He'd had something ready. A whole thing. He'd thought about it all afternoon. It's gone.

* * *

“Thank you,” he says. ᴹ

It sounds very small in the workshop.

“Yuka told me,” he says. “This morning. What you did. While I was—” ᴹ

He stops. He makes himself not say *in the clinic*. Makes himself not soften it.

“While I'd shut everyone out,” he says. ᴹ

Maki's eyebrows come down.

“Maki,” he says. “The porch. *When has that ever meant fine.* You sat with her all morning.” ᴹ

Maki shrugs one shoulder.

“She's my granddaughter,” she says. “What was I going to do. Leave her on the porch?” ᴹᵃᵏ

“Mai,” he says. “Every morning. For two weeks. You let her come here and you didn't ask.” ᴹ

Mai doesn't say anything. She looks at the lamp.

“Yuta,” he says. “Three in the morning. The tea.” ᴹ

Yuta looks down at his hands.

“She was on the stairs,” he says. “I could hear her. I wasn't going to just lie there.” ʸᵗ

* * *

Mau takes a breath.

“I was wrong,” he says. ᴹ

They look at him.

“I swallowed my words,” he says. “All of them. For two weeks. Everything I should have said to her, everything I should have said to you, to anyone. I just—swallowed it. Kept it down. Put it behind a door. And told myself that was the kind thing. That if I didn't say anything, nobody else had to carry it.” ᴹ

He looks at his hands on the bench.

“And you carried it anyway,” he says. “All of you. Her. You sat with her on the porch and made her tea and gave her things to hold, because I wouldn't. You did what I should have been doing. And I didn't even know.” ᴹ

He looks up.

“I'm going to try not to do it again,” he says. “I can't promise I won't. It's—it's very old. It's the oldest thing I know how to do. But I'm going to try. And when I don't manage it, I want you to tell me. To my face. As loud as you want.” ᴹ

* * *

Nobody says anything for a while.

Then Maki puts her staff down against the bench.

“Took you long enough,” she says. ᴹᵃᵏ

He almost laughs.

“I know,” he says. ᴹ

“Two lives, Yuka says.” Maki shakes her head. “Two whole lives of swallowing things. You should have the worst stomach in the world.” ᴹᵃᵏ

“I've got RCT,” he says. ᴹ

“That's cheating,” says Maki. ᴹᵃᵏ

Yuta is smiling. Not his polite smile. The real one, the one that goes up into his eyes.

“You're welcome,” he says. “For the tea. I'd do it again.” ʸᵗ

“I know you would.” ᴹ

“I'd rather not have to,” Yuta adds. ʸᵗ

“So would I,” says Mau. ᴹ

* * *

Mai hasn't said anything.

She's still standing behind the bench with her arms folded, looking at the lamp.

He turns to her.

“There's something else,” he says. “For you. I've been thinking about it since the letter.” ᴹ

Mai's eyes come off the lamp.

“The contract,” he says. “Ours. The supply. I want to change it.” ᴹ

Something goes across Mai's face. Fast. Gone before he can name it. Fear, maybe. The old fear. The one she's had since the Zen'in house, that whatever she's been given will be taken away.

“Not take anything away,” he says quickly. “Nothing. Everything stays the same. The cost of construction stays on my body, not yours. The CE stays mine. Everything you can make now, you'll still be able to make.” ᴹ

Mai's arms loosen very slightly.

“Then what changes?” she says. ᴹᵃ

“Two things,” he says. ᴹ

* * *

“The first one,” he says, “is about you telling me things.” ᴹ

Mai's mouth goes thin.

“You tell me everything,” he says. “Everything you make. Every clasp. Every needle. Every time you build something with the supply, you come and find me and you tell me what it is and how much it cost and whether it's going to hurt. Every single time.” ᴹ

“It's your body,” says Mai. “The cost lands on you. You should know.” ᴹᵃ

“It costs me nothing,” he says. “You know that. The CE doesn't run out. The body barely notices. It's like air on my face.” ᴹ

“You should *still* know,” says Mai. ᴹᵃ

“I've been trying to make you stop,” he says. “For months. I keep telling you you don't have to. That I trust you. That it's fine.” ᴹ

He hears the word. *Fine.* He lets it sit there.

“I give up,” he says. ᴹ

Mai blinks.

“I'm not going to try and stop you anymore,” he says. “You're going to tell me anyway. So I want it in the contract. Not that you *have* to tell me. That whenever you want to make something, and you come and find me, I'll *listen*. I'll stop what I'm doing. I'll sit down. I'll pay attention. Properly. Every time. Not *it's fine, go ahead*. Actually listen. Because you want me to know, and I've been treating that like a chore when it's the opposite.” ᴹ

Mai stares at him.

Maki, on her stool, has gone very quiet.

* * *

“And the second thing,” says Mau. ᴹ

He looks at Mai.

“I can't close your door,” he says. ᴹ

She goes still.

“Ever,” he says. “That's the clause. Whatever happens. However bad it gets. However much the lines are pulling, however loud everything is. I can close every other door I've got, if I have to. But not yours. Not again. It stays open. Always.” ᴹ

“Why,” says Mai. Barely a sound. ᴹᵃ

“Because I trust you,” says Mau. “Blindly. Completely. You're the one person in this house who's never once pushed on a door I didn't want opened. You'd never use it. You'd never come through it uninvited. And I closed it anyway, with all the others, because I couldn't think. And you came and stood in front of me with your arms folded and asked me why, out loud, instead of quietly.” ᴹ

He swallows.

“So I want it written down,” he says. “That I can't do that to you again. Not because you'd hold me to it. Because I would.” ᴹ

* * *

Mai doesn't say anything for a long time.

She unfolds her arms. She puts her hands flat on the bench, the way he puts his.

“I read your letter,” she says. ᴹᵃ

He nods.

“Twice,” says Mai. “Then once more. Then I put it in the drawer with the patterns I don't show anyone.” ᴹᵃ

She looks down at her hands.

“You said I was the person in this house who's best at leaving a thing alone when it needs to be left alone,” she says. “Nobody's ever said that about me. They always say I'm prickly. That I push people away. That I'm difficult.” ᴹᵃ

“You are prickly,” says Maki. “And difficult.” ᴹᵃᵏ

“Shut up,” says Mai, without looking at her. ᴹᵃ

She looks at Mau.

“Write it,” she says. “Both clauses. I'll sign it.” ᴹᵃ

And then, after a moment, much more quietly:

“Thank you for the letter.” ᴹᵃ

* * *

He writes it at her bench, by the lamp, with her pencil.

It's a short contract. Contracts that matter usually are. The old terms, all of them, unchanged. And under them, in his handwriting, the two new ones. *He listens. Every time. He can't close her door.*

He signs it. He feels it settle, the way contracts settle: a new thread, very fine, laid alongside the old one in the line between them, binding.

He pushes it across the bench to her.

Mai picks up the pencil. She reads it once more. Then she signs it, small and neat, the way she does everything.

The thread tightens. He feels her feel it.

She puts the pencil down.

And for the first time since he's known her, Mai smiles at him. Not the small crooked thing she does when something goes right in the workshop. A real one. Brief, and startled, as if it escaped before she could catch it.

Then she turns round very fast and starts putting the cloth back on the bench, as if nothing has happened.

Maki is watching her sister with an expression Mau has never seen on Maki's face.

He'll understand it tomorrow.
