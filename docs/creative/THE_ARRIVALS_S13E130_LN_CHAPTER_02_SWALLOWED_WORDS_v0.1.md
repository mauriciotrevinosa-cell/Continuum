# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 84
## Chapter 29 — Swallowed Words

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 84
**Reading order:** 29
**Source episode:** S13E130
**Continuity state:** B+450 ("37"). Yuka tells Mau how Maki pushed her to face what the band meant, how Mai helped her work out what she could say and stayed to eat with her, and what Yuta told her about walking Mau to Shoko after the quarry. Mau asks the three of them to the workshop after supper and thanks them. He says he was wrong to swallow his words and he'll try not to do it again. Then he offers Mai a new contract. Everything stays the same: the cost of construction on his body, the CE his. But he gives up trying to stop her telling him everything she makes; instead it's written in: whenever she wants to create something, he'll listen, and pay attention. And the most important clause: he can't close her door, ever, because he trusts her blindly. Mai says she read his letter. Mai signs.

*When: B+450. "37." Under it, in Yuka's hand: "workshop (go)". It's an instruction, not a note.*

The night before, Yuka waited in the workshop while Mai filed a burr off a clasp. Mau was across the yard. From the high window she could see him making a space on the bench for Makima and asking whether she wanted the rest of his tea. She used to be able to watch that for as long as she liked and still come away with only a question about Mau. She let herself watch it now until Makima sat down.

“What did you think when I said it?” she asked Mai. ʸ

The file went on moving. “Which thing?” ᴹᵃ

“That maybe I shouldn't have married him.” ʸ

Mai stopped, got the cloth out from under her wrist and wiped the filings into it. “I thought you wanted him to be scared. I saw him looking at your hand afterward. When you dropped the band at the table, I thought he'd been looking there ever since.” ᴹᵃ

Yuka had seen it too, once she let herself think about why he'd look at that hand even with the band back on it. She went to find Maki at the stove. Maki put another pan to warm before she answered, then told her that she'd thought Mau might follow Yuka across the yard and that she had been glad when he didn't.

“I would have had something worse to say when he followed me,” Yuka said. She remembered how good it had felt to have somebody tell her to let him be the one who had to ask. “Why did that seem like a thing he deserved?” ʸ

“I don't know,” Maki said. “Tell me when it started seeming fair to you.” ᴹᵃᵏ

Yuka had to take the pan completely off the heat. The answer wasn't the time he shut the line. She'd been frightened then and wanted to find him. She could hear the words in the order Cross had given them to her. You came second. He knows you'll be here. Let him be the one with something to lose. She'd supplied the marriage herself. He'd only had to wait while she worked out what would hurt most.

Yuta was coming in through the porch with his coat over his arm. She asked him about the clinic after the quarry. By the time he told her where he'd had to slow down so Mau could keep up, she had taken a chair by the door and put his coat across her lap. His hand had been at Mau's elbow the whole way. She listened long enough for him to tell her exactly how far they'd gone before Mau managed to ask for Shoko a second time.

“I kept telling him that Cross understood me,” she said. “Who else heard what I did and thought it made sense?” ʸ

Yuta had to think to answer that. “Shizue understood why you wanted to know where he'd gone. Maki understood why you were angry. I did too.” He sat down, leaving the coat where it was. “Cross was the one who thought you should do something to frighten him.” ʸᵗ

She had asked herself why Cross would do that and run out of answers after *he thinks he's helping*. She asked Mai once she was back in the workshop with her. Mai had the good clasp in her hand by then. She turned it over to show Yuka how the edges met.

“Where did he ask you to go if you left?” she said. ᴹᵃ

“North. With him.” ʸ

Mai turned the clasp over again. “I think he wanted you to pick him. He'd have had a much harder time asking for that with Mau still beside you.” ᴹᵃ

Yuka looked out at the bench again. Mau was still there. So was Makima. She'd been afraid to see how much of what Cross told her she'd wanted to hear. She had the words now, in his voice and then in hers. She could still go out and sit with Mau if he wanted her there.

* * *

Yuka tells him the next morning after breakfast.

She doesn't say it like a confession. She says it while she's stealing the pickles, with her mouth half full, as if it's the most ordinary thing in the world.

“I talked to Maki and Yuta and Mai,” she says. “About when we weren't talking. There's a part I never told you.” ʸ

He puts his chopsticks down.

Yuka looks across the table. Maki is sitting with Yuta, showing him how much space there ought to be between his hands when he turns a staff. Mai has a drawing that might be a clasp beside her bowl, far enough from the rim that it won't get wet.

“Maki saw you ask me to stay and talk,” says Yuka. “Later, she asked me if I'd wanted the band off for good. I kept starting with what happened at the Gate. She sat there until I'd answered what she'd actually asked.” ʸ

She pushes a pickle back to his side of the plate without looking down. Across the yard Cross has come out to help Maru with a load of poles for the ford. He and Rimuru talk across the cart for a while. He can see the table from where he is. Once Maru has the rope free, Cross walks round to his end of the load.

“Mai caught the band when I dropped it,” she says. “She gave it back and asked what I could do if you'd only give me a minute. Later, when I saw your comb at Shoko's, I told her I wanted you home. She told me you'd get a say in where you slept. Then I asked her to eat with me and she brought her work and sat with me.” ʸ

He remembers coming out to eat on Shuna's bench and being glad there was somewhere he could sit without having to tell anybody why he'd come.

“Grandpa told me what happened after I went upstairs with the band,” Yuka says. “You asked him to walk you to Shoko. I thought if I went back down I'd find you at the table. I didn't even know you could barely get her name out.” ʸ

“I remember asking him to walk with me,” says Mau. “I don't remember going across the yard.” ᴹ

Yuta is still holding his hands out where Maki put them, waiting for her to tell him which way to turn. Yuka picks up the pickle and eats it. She makes the face and wipes her fingers on Mau's sleeve.

“You should go and say thank you,” she says. “Properly. All three. Tonight.” ʸ

* * *

He asks them to the workshop after supper.

He doesn't say why. He just asks. Maki looks at him with her eyebrows up. Yuta says *of course* at once, the way Yuta always does. Mai looks at him for a long time over the top of her drawing and then says, “It's my workshop,” as if he's asked permission, and goes there first to clear a space.

When he comes in, she's cleared the bench. All of it. The cloth and the clasps and the files and the half-made things. She's put four stools round it. And a lamp. And a pot of tea, which nobody asked her to make.

Maki is sitting on one stool with her staff across her knees. Yuta is on another. Mai is behind the bench, standing, with her arms folded, the way she stands when she isn't sure what's going to happen in her own room.

He sits on the fourth stool.

He looks at them.

He'd had something ready. A whole thing. He'd thought about it all afternoon. It's gone.

* * *

“Thank you,” he says. ᴹ

It sounds very small in the workshop.

“Yuka told me this morning what you did while I was keeping most of it to myself,” he says. ᴹ

Maki's eyebrows come down.

“You asked her about the band,” he tells her. “She was able to tell me about it at Shoko's. She didn't need me to say it first.” ᴹ

“She's my granddaughter,” says Maki. “I wanted her to know what she'd asked you to look at.” ᴹᵃᵏ

“Mai. You helped her figure out what to say and you made time when she asked you to sit with her.” ᴹ

Mai doesn't say anything. She looks at the lamp.

“And Yuta. Thanks for walking me to Shoko.” ᴹ

Yuta looks down at his hands. “You told me what you needed,” he says. “I could do that.” ʸᵗ

* * *

Mau takes a breath.

“I was wrong,” he says. ᴹ

They look at him.

“I swallowed my words,” he says. “A lot of things I should have told her. Things I could have told you. I thought if I kept them down, nobody else would have to carry them.” ᴹ

He looks at his hands on the bench.

“By the time I could tell Shoko what had happened, you'd already been helping us get through the day. I was still trying to figure out whether I could ask for anything.” ᴹ

He looks up.

“I'm going to try not to do it again,” he says. “I can't promise I won't. It's—it's very old. It's the oldest thing I know how to do. But I'm going to try. And when I don't manage it, I want you to tell me. To my face. As loud as you want.” ᴹ

* * *

Nobody says anything for a while.

Then Maki puts her staff down against the bench.

“Took you long enough,” she says. ᴹᵃᵏ

He almost laughs.

“I know,” he says. ᴹ

“Two lives, Yuka says.” Maki shakes her head. “Two whole lives of swallowing things. You should have the worst stomach in the world.” ᴹᵃᵏ

“I've got RCT,” he says. ᴹ

“That's cheating,” says Maki. ᴹᵃᵏ

Yuta is smiling. Not his polite smile. The real one, the one that goes up into his eyes.

“You're welcome,” he says. “If you ask me to walk with you again, I will.” ʸᵗ

“I know you would.” ᴹ

“I'd rather not have to,” Yuta adds. ʸᵗ

“So would I,” says Mau. ᴹ

* * *

Mai hasn't said anything.

She's still standing behind the bench with her arms folded, looking at the lamp.

He turns to her.

“There's something else,” he says. “For you. I've been thinking about it since the letter.” ᴹ

Mai's eyes come off the lamp.

“The contract,” he says. “Ours. The supply. I want to change it.” ᴹ

Something goes across Mai's face. Fast. Gone before he can name it. Fear, maybe. The old fear. The one she's had since the Zen'in house, that whatever she's been given will be taken away.

“Not take anything away,” he says quickly. “Nothing. Everything stays the same. The cost of construction stays on my body, not yours. The CE stays mine. Everything you can make now, you'll still be able to make.” ᴹ

Mai's arms loosen very slightly.

“Then what changes?” she says. ᴹᵃ

“Two things,” he says. ᴹ

* * *

“The first one,” he says, “is about you telling me things.” ᴹ

Mai's mouth goes thin.

“You tell me everything,” he says. “Everything you make. Every clasp. Every needle. Every time you build something with the supply, you come and find me and you tell me what it is and how much it cost and whether it's going to hurt. Every single time.” ᴹ

“It's your body,” says Mai. “The cost lands on you. You should know.” ᴹᵃ

“It costs me nothing,” he says. “You know that. The CE doesn't run out. The body barely notices. It's like air on my face.” ᴹ

“You should *still* know,” says Mai. ᴹᵃ

“I've been trying to make you stop,” he says. “For months. I keep telling you you don't have to. That I trust you. That it's fine.” ᴹ

He hears the word. *Fine.* He lets it sit there.

“I give up,” he says. ᴹ

Mai blinks.

“I'm not going to try and stop you anymore,” he says. “You're going to tell me anyway. So I want it in the contract. Not that you *have* to tell me. That whenever you want to make something, and you come and find me, I'll *listen*. I'll stop what I'm doing. I'll sit down. I'll pay attention. Properly. Every time. Not *it's fine, go ahead*. Actually listen. Because you want me to know, and I've been treating that like a chore when it's the opposite.” ᴹ

Mai stares at him.

Maki, on her stool, has gone very quiet.

* * *

“And the second thing,” says Mau. ᴹ

He looks at Mai.

“I can't close your door,” he says. ᴹ

She goes still.

“Ever,” he says. “That's the clause. Whatever happens. However bad it gets. However much the lines are pulling, however loud everything is. I can close every other door I've got, if I have to. But not yours. Not again. It stays open. Always.” ᴹ

“Why,” says Mai. Barely a sound. ᴹᵃ

“Because I trust you,” says Mau. “Blindly. Completely. You're the one person in this house who's never once pushed on a door I didn't want opened. You'd never use it. You'd never come through it uninvited. And I closed it anyway, with all the others, because I couldn't think. And you came and stood in front of me with your arms folded and asked me why, out loud, instead of quietly.” ᴹ

He swallows.

“So I want it written down,” he says. “That I can't do that to you again. Not because you'd hold me to it. Because I would.” ᴹ

* * *

Mai doesn't say anything for a long time.

She unfolds her arms. She puts her hands flat on the bench, the way he puts his.

“I read your letter,” she says. ᴹᵃ

He nods.

“Twice,” says Mai. “Then once more. Then I put it in the drawer with the patterns I don't show anyone.” ᴹᵃ

She looks down at her hands.

“You said I was the person in this house who's best at leaving a thing alone when it needs to be left alone,” she says. “Nobody's ever said that about me. They always say I'm prickly. That I push people away. That I'm difficult.” ᴹᵃ

“You are prickly,” says Maki. “And difficult.” ᴹᵃᵏ

“Shut up,” says Mai, without looking at her. ᴹᵃ

She looks at Mau.

“Write it,” she says. “Both clauses. I'll sign it.” ᴹᵃ

And then, after a moment, much more quietly:

“Thank you for the letter.” ᴹᵃ

* * *

He writes it at her bench, by the lamp, with her pencil.

It's a short contract. Contracts that matter usually are. The old terms, all of them, unchanged. And under them, in his handwriting, the two new ones. *He listens. Every time. He can't close her door.*

He signs it. He feels it settle, the way contracts settle: a new thread, very fine, laid alongside the old one in the line between them, binding.

He pushes it across the bench to her.

Mai picks up the pencil. She reads it once more. Then she signs it, small and neat, the way she does everything.

The thread tightens. He feels her feel it.

She puts the pencil down.

And for the first time since he's known her, Mai smiles at him. Not the small crooked thing she does when something goes right in the workshop. A real one. Brief, and startled, as if it escaped before she could catch it.

Then she turns round very fast and starts putting the cloth back on the bench, as if nothing has happened.

Maki is watching her sister with an expression Mau has never seen on Maki's face.

He'll understand it tomorrow.
