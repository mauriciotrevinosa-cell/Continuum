# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 84
## Chapter 29 — First Pin

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 84
**Reading order:** 29
**Source episode:** S13E132
**Continuity state:** B+451 ("36"). The first use of the new clause, and the first time in two weeks Mai builds through Mau; she reaches with the old numbers (a tingle per beam, when he was about 200 old levels), they test it on the porch step at his level 5 (500 old), and it's like air; two EVERYTHINGs now, hers and Maki's. Mai comes to find Mau in the yard in the middle of the morning to tell him what she's going to make today, and he stops what he's doing, sits down on the porch step and listens, properly, to all of it: a set of pins for Maki's first lesson, and the exact cost. It takes twenty minutes. It's the best twenty minutes of Mai's week, though she'd never say so. That evening Maki makes her first pin. It comes apart. She makes another. It comes apart. On the eleventh try it holds. Mau feels the small cost land in his body through the line, so small it's like air on his face, and has never been so pleased to be charged for anything.

*When: B+451. "36." Under it, in Maki's handwriting, very large and slightly aggressive: "PIN".*

Mai comes to find him in the middle of the morning.

He's in the yard with Jin-Woo and Suho, teaching Suho how to fall. Not fight; fall. Suho is small and has decided he wants to learn to fight like his father, and Jin-Woo has decided that before anybody in his family learns to throw a punch they learn to hit the ground without breaking anything, and has asked Mau to help, because Mau has hit the ground more times than anyone else in the house.

Suho is very bad at falling. He keeps trying to land on his feet.

“You're meant to land on your *shoulder*,” Jin-Woo is saying, for the ninth time. ᴶʷ

“But that *hurts*,” says Suho. ˢᵘ

“Less than your head,” says Mau. ᴹ

And Mai is standing at the edge of the training ground with her arms folded and a piece of paper in one hand, not saying anything, waiting.

* * *

He knows what it is the moment he sees the paper.

Last time Mai tried to tell him what she was going to make, he was holding a beam at the wrong angle and wanted her to go ahead before his shoulder slipped. He'd listened to the first line of the proposal and asked her if she had enough to start. She'd folded it up in front of him and said yes.

He stops.

He says, “Sorry, Suho,” and “Excuse me,” to Jin-Woo, and goes across the yard to her. ᴹ

Jin-Woo watches him go. He doesn't say anything. But Mau sees him notice.

* * *

“I'm going to make something,” says Mai. ᴹᵃ

She holds out the paper. She doesn't look at him. She looks at a point somewhere over his left shoulder.

“Under the new clause,” she says. “I'm telling you. You said you'd—” ᴹᵃ

“Listen,” he says. “Yes.” ᴹ

He sits down on the porch step.

Mai looks at him sitting there. She looks at the step beside him. She looks at the paper.

Then she sits down too, not close, with the paper on her knees, and starts to explain.

* * *

It takes twenty minutes.

It's pins. For Maki's first lesson tonight. Twelve of them, the simplest thing Mai knows how to make, and she's going to make them first, herself, as patterns, so that Maki has something to copy. She explains why pins: because a pin is one piece, no joins, nothing to come apart in the middle. Because it's small, so the cost is small. Because if Maki gets it wrong, nobody gets hurt, except possibly Maki's thumb.

She explains the cost.

And halfway through, he hears that she's explaining it the old way.

It isn't the first time she's built through him. Far from it. The second floor of the workshop stands over their heads because of it; half the list in her cracked notebook was written on the strength of it. But she hasn't touched the supply for two weeks. Not since he closed the lines and then opened them again and she didn't quite trust the door yet. And now that she's reaching for it again, she's reaching with the old numbers.

“Left thumb,” she says. “A tingle. A sharp one. About a minute. That's what it was for the frame, per beam.” ᴹᵃ

He remembers. The second floor. Every beam a small bright sting in his thumb, the body he had then noticing each one.

“That was then,” he says. ᴹ

Mai looks up.

“I was about two hundred, then,” he says. “Old levels. Now it's five on the new scale. Five hundred, old. The body's not the same body. I don't think you'll get a tingle out of it anymore.” ᴹ

Mai narrows her eyes.

“You *think*,” she says. ᴹᵃ

“Let's test it,” says Mau. ᴹ

* * *

She makes one pin, right there on the porch step.

Small. A plain one, round head, the pattern for tonight. She reaches down the line for the supply the way she always has, and he holds still and pays attention, all of it, to his left thumb.

The pin comes together in her palm.

He waits for the tingle.

It doesn't come. Not really. Something does: so small he'd miss it if he weren't watching for it. A touch. A breath of air across the skin of his thumb, there and gone.

“Well?” says Mai. ᴹᵃ

“Like air,” he says. “Like a bit of wind on my face. That's it.” ᴹ

Mai looks at the pin in her hand. Then at his thumb. Then at him.

He watches it go through her. Two weeks of not reaching. The old numbers in her head. Now *air*.

She opens the cracked notebook.

The first page. *EVERYTHING*, underlined twice, in her small hard capitals.

“Then I'm adding things,” she says. ᴹᵃ

“Add whatever you want.” ᴹ

“And Maki has one now,” says Mai. “Her own list. She started it last night. She thinks I don't know.” ᴹᵃ

He laughs.

“Okay,” he says. “Let's build an aqua-something. A water thing. With the arches. The aqua—” ᴹ

Mai hits him on the back of the head.

Not hard. A proper sisterly smack, the kind Maki gives Yuta.

“You *read* it,” she says. ᴹᵃ

“Only what you let me see,” says Mau. “The first day. Before your thumb came down. Bridges. Something with *aqueduct*—” ᴹ

She smacks him again.

“That's *mine*,” she says. “You don't get to say it before I've drawn it.” ᴹᵃ

And then she laughs. Out loud, on the porch step, with the pin in one hand and the notebook in the other, the way she almost never does where anyone can see.

“Two *everythings*,” she says. “Now there's going to be two. Hers and mine.” ᴹᵃ

“I can't wait,” says Mau. “Tell me every single thing.” ᴹ

Mai looks at him for a moment.

“I will,” she says. “That's the clause.” ᴹᵃ

She looks down at the pin in her hand. Then, without looking up, very quickly, as if it might escape:

“Thank you.” ᴹᵃ

“If you keep looking at me like that, I'm getting the good soda out and telling Momo it's for construction expenses.” ᴹ

Mai blinks at him. Then the corner of her mouth goes.

“You better get two,” she says. “Three if Momo's working.” She puts the notebook on the step where both of them can see it.

Mau holds up one hand. In the air in front of him, the Shop opens. The panel. The lists. He scrolls.

“Tea,” he says. “Cold or hot. The fizzy one Yuka likes. The plum thing. Coffee, three kinds. Something called a *milkshake*, which Umaru says is a meal. Juice. That yoghurt drink Ori hates—” ᴹ

Mai stops halfway to standing.

She stares at the floating panel.

“Oh,” she says. “I forget that.” ᴹᵃ

“It's really convenient, isn't it?” says Mau. ᴹ

Mai sits back down on the step. She looks at the panel for a long time. Then she points at something on it, without saying what, and Mau buys it, and it's in her hand, cold, with drops running down the outside.

“That was supposed to be for *you*,” she says. ᴹᵃ

“I know,” says Mau. He buys a second one. “Now it's for both of us.” ᴹ

Mai holds hers and doesn't drink it straight away.

“Convenient,” she says, as if trying out the word. Then she drinks it, and goes back to explaining the pins.

She explains why she's making twelve and not ten. Because Maki will lose two.

She explains the shape of the head, which is round, and why, which is so Maki can't stab herself with the wrong end.

He listens to all of it.

He doesn't say *it's fine*. He doesn't say *go ahead*. He doesn't say *you don't have to tell me*. He asks two questions: why the heads are round, which she's already explained, and whether Maki will be cross about starting with pins, which makes Mai's mouth twitch.

“Furious,” she says. “She'll be furious. She wanted to start with a spearhead.” ᴹᵃ

“You said no.” ᴹ

“I said a pin. A spearhead is two hundred joins. She'd take her own fingers off.” ᴹᵃ

“Thank you,” he says. ᴹ

Mai looks at him sharply, as if checking whether he's joking. He isn't.

* * *

When she's finished, she folds the paper up again.

She sits there for a moment on the step with it in her lap, not getting up.

“That's all,” she says. ᴹᵃ

“Okay,” he says. The good kind. “Thank you for telling me.” ᴹ

Mai doesn't move.

“You listened,” she says. ᴹᵃ

“I said I would.” ᴹ

“You *listened*,” says Mai again, as if he hasn't understood. “You stopped. You came over. You sat down. You asked about the heads.” ᴹᵃ

She looks at the paper.

“Nobody's ever—” she starts. And stops. ᴹᵃ

He waits.

She doesn't finish it. She stands up very abruptly and brushes off her skirt.

“Twelve pins,” she says. “This evening. Left thumb. Pay attention, or you won't even feel it.” ᴹᵃ

And she goes back across the yard to the workshop at a fast walk, and doesn't look back, and he sits on the step and watches her go.

*That was twenty minutes*, says Ciel. ᶜ

*I know.* ᴹ

*She'll talk about it for a week. She'll never say she's talking about it.* ᶜ

* * *

Maki is furious about the pins.

He hears about it at supper. Everyone hears about it. Maki sits at the table and eats very fast with her jaw set and explains to Yuta, at length, that she is a grown woman who has fought in a war and killed a curse the size of a house with a spear, and her sister is going to teach her to make *pins*.

“Small ones,” Maki says. “With *round heads*. So I don't *stab myself*.” ᴹᵃᵏ

Yuta, very wisely, says nothing.

Across the table, Mai eats her soup and doesn't look up, and looks extremely pleased.

* * *

The first pin comes apart in Maki's hand.

He's in the kitchen when it happens. He feels it: a flicker down the line, the beginning of a touch on his left thumb, like air, and then nothing. The construction starting, drawing a tiny thread of CE through him, and then falling apart before it finishes, and the CE coming back.

From the workshop, faintly, across the dark yard: “*It's GONE.*” ᴹᵃᵏ

And Mai: “I told you. You weren't holding the shape. Again.” ᴹᵃ

The second pin comes apart.

The third pin comes apart halfway and leaves a sort of bent wire, which Maki throws across the workshop.

He feels every one, because he's paying attention. A flicker. Half a breath. Nothing.

Yuka, at the table beside him, watches his face.

“Is that her?” she says. “Every time your thumb does that?” ʸ

He looks down. His left thumb is twitching, very slightly, every time. He hadn't noticed.

“Yes,” he says. ᴹ

Yuka takes his left hand in both of hers. His wrist is a little high against the lip of the table, so she pushes her chair in and sets it across her lap. The next flicker jumps into her thumb. She rubs the place once.

He could take his hand back. He turns it palm up instead, and when she slides her fingers between his he leans over and kisses the part in her hair. She tips against his upper arm with a pleased little sound. Through the open window, they hear Mai tell Maki to keep her fingers closed for another breath.

“Tell me when it works,” she says. ʸ

* * *

The fourth comes apart. The fifth. The sixth.

The seventh holds for a whole second before it goes.

“*Did you SEE that*,” says Maki, from the workshop. ᴹᵃᵏ

“It came apart,” says Mai. ᴹᵃ

“But it *held*. For a *second*.” ᴹᵃᵏ

“A pin that holds for a second is not a pin. It's a rumour. Again.” ᴹᵃ

The eighth. The ninth. The tenth.

He sits at the kitchen table with Yuka holding his hand, and feels every one. Shuna brings him tea. Umaru comes in and asks what's happening and Yuka explains, and Umaru sits down at the table too, with her chin in her hands, staring at the window that looks out at the workshop, and doesn't go away.

Ori comes in. Then Rem. Then, after a while, Yuta, who sits down at the end of the table and doesn't say anything and is clearly listening as hard as everyone else.

The eleventh.

He feels it start. The thread of CE, drawing through him, very thin. The construction catching. Holding. The shape closing round itself, all the way, one piece, no joins.

And then the touch. On his left thumb. So small it's barely there. Like a breath of air on the skin.

It stays.

He feels it land and stay, the way Mai said it would. The cost. Paid. The smallest thing he's ever been charged.

He laughs.

Out loud. At the kitchen table. With his thumb tingling.

Yuka looks at him.

“It worked,” he says. ᴹ

* * *

From the workshop, across the dark yard, there's a silence.

Then Maki's voice. Not loud. Quite small, for Maki. Almost wondering.

“It's a pin,” she says. ᴹᵃᵏ

A pause.

“It's a pin,” says Mai. ᴹᵃ

Another pause. Longer.

Then Maki, louder: “It's a *PIN*.” ᴹᵃᵏ

And then something that sounds very much like two people hugging, which is not a sound, exactly, but the whole kitchen hears it anyway.

Umaru starts clapping. Ori joins in. Then Rem, then Shuna at the stove, then Yuta at the end of the table, with his face doing something very soft. Yuka, still holding his hand, squeezes it.

He sits there with the tingle already gone from his left thumb.

He's been charged for a great many things, once. Gates. Domains. Purple. Things that cost him pieces of his body that took weeks to grow back. These days hardly anything costs him at all; the CE doesn't run out, and the body's grown past noticing most of it.

This cost him nothing. A breath of air on his thumb.

He's never been so glad to pay for anything in his life.

* * *

Maki comes to supper the next night with the pin.

She's put it through the collar of her shirt. Where everyone can see it. It's very small, and very plain, with a round head, and it's slightly crooked.

Nobody mentions it.

Maki doesn't mention it either.

But every so often, all through supper, her hand goes up to it. Just to check it's still there.

And Mai, at the end of the table, watches her do it, and doesn't say anything, and eats her soup, and looks happier than anyone at the table has ever seen her.
