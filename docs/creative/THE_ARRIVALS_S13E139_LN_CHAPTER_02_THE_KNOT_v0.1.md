# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 85
## Chapter 3 — The Knot

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 85
**Reading order:** 3
**Source episode:** S13E139
**Continuity state:** B+464 ("23"). Beatrice asks. Not because she must; because she's a Fort member now and has decided she'd like to be one without a contract under it. In the library, with Echidna in the chair by the lamp and Frieren in the doorway as witness, Echidna shows Mau where the knot was tied, four hundred years ago. Mau takes its weight onto himself, the way he carries Makima's rule. It hurts: the specific ache of having to wait for someone who may never come. The wheel turns once. Then a second time. On the second turn the knot can't hold him, and with nothing left to hold, it comes undone. Beatrice feels it go. Echidna, who wanted to know whether it was possible, now knows, and her face shows something that isn't curiosity at all. The library doesn't close. Beatrice is still standing in it.

*When: B+464. "23." Under it, in Beatrice's hand, very small and very neat: "asked". The same word Satella wrote.*

Beatrice asks at breakfast.

She doesn't make an occasion of it. She sits in her place beside Frieren, with her Fort badge on her dress and her owl beside her plate, and eats her toast, and tells Shuna the jam is acceptable, in fact, and then, without changing her voice at all, says:

“Betty would like to try the wheel. Today. If it is convenient.” ᴮᵉᵃ

The table goes quiet.

Mau puts his cup down.

Across the table, at the far end, in her usual place, Echidna goes very still.

“Today?” says Mau. ᴹ

“Betty has thought about it,” says Beatrice. “Since the oath. Four days. Betty is a Fort member now. Betty took an oath, even against the library.” ᴮᵉᵃ

She puts her toast down.

“Betty would like to be one,” she says, “without a contract underneath. Betty would like to be in the Fort because Betty chose it. Not because Betty is waiting for someone and has nothing better to do in the meantime, I suppose.” ᴮᵉᵃ

She looks at Mau.

“So Betty is asking,” she says. ᴮᵉᵃ

* * *

They go to the library after breakfast.

Not through the pantry. Through the room with no name, at the end of the north corridor upstairs, past the cellar stair. The first door. The real one. Echidna's.

Echidna goes first. She opens the door and goes to her chair by the lamp and sits down in it, and Mau can see that her hands, on the arms of the chair, are not quite still.

Beatrice goes in next. She stands in the middle of the floor, between the shelves, with her chin up.

Frieren stops in the doorway.

“Witness,” she says, when Mau looks at her. “Someone should be.” ᶠ

Mau goes in last.

* * *

“Show me,” he says to Echidna. ᴹ

Echidna doesn't get up.

She lifts one hand and points. At Beatrice. At the space round her. And Mau looks, with the Six Eyes, the way he looked in this room six weeks ago, the night Beatrice found it.

There it is. The binding. Old, wound round Beatrice and running back into the shelves, and then bending, and running to the chair by the lamp, and lying there slack. Like a rope tied off to a post that was supposed to fall down and didn't.

“There,” says Echidna. “Do you see where it turns?” ᴱᶜ

He sees.

“That's where I tied it,” she says. “Four hundred years ago. In a library that doesn't exist anymore. I sat in a chair very like this one and tied it with words I'd spent a long time choosing, and I made it so it would hold until she found the one person it was waiting for. And then I died, and left it.” ᴱᶜ

Her voice is very level.

“The knot is there,” she says. “Where it turns. Not on her. On *me*. On the place where I was supposed to be gone. That's what it's tied to.” ᴱᶜ

She looks at him.

“If you take its weight,” she says, “you'll be taking it from there. From me. You'll feel what it was made to feel.” ᴱᶜ

* * *

“What was it made to feel?” says Mau. ᴹ

Echidna doesn't answer straight away.

Beatrice does.

“Waiting,” she says. ᴮᵉᵃ

Mau looks at her.

“That's all it is, in fact,” says Beatrice. Her chin is very high. “Waiting. For someone who might come. Every day. Every hour. Every time a door opens. Waiting, and the door opening, and it not being them, and waiting again. For four hundred years.” ᴮᵉᵃ

She looks at him steadily.

“Are you sure you want to carry that?” she says. “Even for a moment, I suppose?” ᴮᵉᵃ

He thinks about it.

He thinks about a year in the Trial, practising goodbye. About a week in the clinic, with the door open a hand's width, waiting to see whose feet would come up the step.

“I've carried it before,” he says. ᴹ

* * *

He takes it.

He reaches out, with the eyes and with the part of him that reaches for contracts, the part that carries Makima's rule and Yuka's techniques and Mai's construction, and finds the place where the knot turns. On the chair by the lamp. On Echidna. On the place where she was supposed to be gone.

He lifts it off her.

He lays it on himself.

And it's *heavy*.

Not heavy like weight. Heavy like a long afternoon. Like an empty room. Like every evening you've ever sat by a window watching a road for someone who didn't come. It goes into him and settles there, under the ribs, in the place where he keeps things, and it *aches*. A specific ache. The ache of a door that might open. The ache of hoping, and not being allowed to stop.

He staggers.

He doesn't fall. But he puts a hand on the nearest shelf, and his breath goes out of him in a rush, and the room goes grey at the edges, greyer than it already is.

Frieren, in the doorway, takes one step in.

“I'm all right,” he says. ᴹ

“You're not,” says Frieren. ᶠ

“No,” he agrees. “Give it a second.” ᴹ

* * *

The wheel turns.

He doesn't call it. He doesn't have to. It comes the way it always comes: up out of the dark behind his eyes, enormous, eight-spoked, still. It rises over his head in the library, among the shelves, so big that its upper rim vanishes into the dimness between the stacks.

Echidna makes a sound.

Mau has never heard Echidna make a sound like it. A small, sharp intake of breath. The sound of someone watching the thing they have wanted to see for a very, very long time.

*It turns at what hurts*, Mau said once. *And learns its shape.*

It turns.

Once. A single great slow click, a sound that goes through the floor and the shelves and the books and every bone in his body.

The ache gets less.

Not gone. Less. The wheel has learned a little of it. The shape of waiting. The shape of a door that might open. It's turned toward that shape and taken its measure, the way it took the measure of the crystal, and of Ko, and of everything else that's ever hurt him.

The knot is still there. On him now. Tight. But less tight.

“Once,” says Echidna. Very softly. ᴱᶜ

“Wait,” says Mau. ᴹ

* * *

The wheel turns again.

The second click is louder. Or he's listening harder.

And this time something *gives*.

He feels it go. Not the wheel. The knot. The knot that was made, four hundred years ago, with words a woman spent a long time choosing, to hold until someone came. It has been lying on him for perhaps a minute, trying to hold him the way it held Beatrice, trying to make him wait.

And the wheel has learned it. The whole shape of it. And now it can't hold him.

And there's nothing else for it to hold.

It was tied to a woman who was supposed to be dead. She isn't. It was laid on a man it can't bind. It has nowhere left to be.

It comes undone.

Like a rope sliding off a post. All at once. Quietly. The whole long length of it, from the chair by the lamp to the shelves to the small figure standing in the middle of the floor with her chin up, going slack, and slipping, and falling away into nothing.

* * *

Beatrice gasps.

She puts both hands on her chest. Flat. Over her heart. As if something has just been taken out of it, or put back. She stands very still in the middle of the library floor with her eyes wide and her hands on her chest and her mouth open.

“It's gone,” she says. ᴮᵉᵃ

She sounds astonished. She sounds like someone who has just put down something she's been carrying so long she forgot it was heavy.

“It's *gone*,” she says again. “Betty can't—Betty doesn't—there isn't—” ᴮᵉᵃ

She looks round the library. At the shelves going back and back. At the lamp. At the chair.

“Betty doesn't have to wait,” she says. ᴮᵉᵃ

* * *

The wheel goes back into the dark.

Mau lets it. He's leaning on the shelf with one hand. The ache is gone. All of it. Not even a memory of it left under the ribs. The wheel took it, and learned it, and then there was nothing to learn anymore.

He looks at Echidna.

She's sitting in her chair by the lamp with both hands on the arms of it, gripping. She hasn't moved. She's looking at Beatrice.

She wanted to know whether it was possible. She's known it was possible since she first named the wheel, a long time ago, *the one thing in the house that learns from inside*. She told everyone. Nobody would let her find out.

She's found out.

And her face isn't doing the thing he expected. The sharp, total, delighted interest. The face of someone who's just been handed the best question in a century.

It's doing something else.

Something he's only seen on it once before. Six weeks ago. When Beatrice walked out of this library holding Yuka's hand, and Echidna was left looking at the empty doorway.

He doesn't have a word for it.

It isn't curiosity at all.

* * *

“Beatrice,” says Echidna. ᴱᶜ

Beatrice turns round.

Echidna is quiet for a long moment.

“You can go,” she says. “Anywhere. Now. You're not bound to it. Not to the library. Not to the waiting. Not to me. You don't have to stay in this room, or this house, or anywhere. You can go wherever you like. That's what it means.” ᴱᶜ

Her voice is very level. Mau can hear what it's costing her to keep it level.

“I'd like you to know that,” says Echidna. “Before you decide anything. That you can go.” ᴱᶜ

Beatrice looks at her.

She looks at her for a long time, standing in the middle of the library floor with her hands still on her chest.

Then she takes her hands off her chest. She turns round. She looks at the shelves, all of them, going back and back into the dimness, further than the room should allow.

“The doors are badly placed, in fact,” she says. ᴮᵉᵃ

Echidna blinks.

“There are not nearly enough of them,” says Beatrice. “Betty has been meaning to do the west wing. And the bath. Sekhmet has asked for a door to the bath. And the Fort. The Fort should have a library door. That's obvious.” ᴮᵉᵃ

She turns back round.

“Betty is not *going* anywhere,” she says. “Betty has a great deal of work to do, I suppose.” ᴮᵉᵃ

* * *

Frieren, in the doorway, turns round and goes away down the corridor without a word.

Mau follows her.

He doesn't need to be told. He goes out of the library and closes the door of the room with no name behind him, quietly, the way Echidna asked, the first night.

Through it, very faintly, he hears Beatrice say something.

He hears Echidna not answer, for a long time.

And then he hears something he's never heard from that room before, from either of them, in all the weeks since the night Beatrice found it.

He hears them both laughing.

He walks away down the corridor, and doesn't listen to the rest.
