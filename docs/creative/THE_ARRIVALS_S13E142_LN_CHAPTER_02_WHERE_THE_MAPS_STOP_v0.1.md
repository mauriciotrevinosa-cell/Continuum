# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 85
## Chapter 12 — Where the Maps Stop

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 85
**Reading order:** 12
**Source episode:** S13E142
**Continuity state:** B+469 afternoon into the night of B+470 ("18" → "17"). Past the ruins, down into the basin. Ori stops at the black scorch in the middle where Kamish's fire landed and asks what happened here. Mau tells her the real one, at her size. Then on, past the far rim, into country nobody in the house has walked: a long falling valley with a stream, and at the bottom of it a stand of old pear trees gone wild, heavy with small hard fruit. Ori names it on her map. They camp under the trees. Yuka keeps her word and sleeps in the tent, with Ori on one side and Mau on the other. *When: B+469–470. "18", then "17". The notebook went in the satchel. Ori wrote both numbers by torchlight.*

The basin is still black in the middle.

Ori sees it from the rim. She stops so suddenly that Beru nearly walks into her.

It's a wide flat scorch on the old stone, right in the centre of the bowl, where nothing grows. A month of wind and rain hasn't moved it. A great dark ring with ragged edges, like a coin someone burned into the ground.

Ori looks at it for a long time.

Then she looks at Mau.

“Here,” she says. ᴼ

It isn't a question.

“Here,” says Mau. ᴹ

* * *

She walks down into the basin.

He lets her. He doesn't go first. He walks a step behind her, with Yuka beside him and Beru beside Ori, and lets her lead the way down the slope and across the cracked stone to the edge of the black.

She stops at the edge. She doesn't step onto it.

“Tell me,” she says. ᴼ

He tells her.

The real one. Her size. Not the whole of it. Not the hungry things pouring over the rim, or what they looked like, or what they wanted. But the shape of it.

That things came that wanted to hurt the house. That they were small fears, mostly, frightened of something new. That he came here, with Makima, so they'd come to him and not to the river or the road. That he put everything he had into one thing, and couldn't move, and Makima said *stop* and they stopped. That the thing he made went through the basin and they weren't there anymore. That a few were left, and the soldiers made a ring so they couldn't run toward the house, and Kamish came down out of the sky.

“And that's his fire,” he says. “There. That's where it landed.” ᴹ

Ori looks at the black ring.

“Did they hurt?” she says. ᴼ

He thinks about it. The real answer.

“I don't think so,” he says. “It was very fast. And Makima says they'll be born again somewhere far away, small and new. They won't remember any of it.” ᴹ

Ori nods slowly.

“Then they won't be sad,” she says. “Like she said.” ᴼ

“Like she said.” ᴹ

* * *

Ori crouches down at the edge of the black.

She takes a pencil out of her satchel and a fresh page of her map, and draws it. The basin. The rim. The black ring in the middle. Very carefully. With the ragged edges.

Under it she writes: *KAMISH'S FIRE.*

And under that, smaller: *Dad and Makima. (Not sad.)*

Overhead, very high, a shape the size of a cloud tilts its wings once, as if it can read.

* * *

Past the far rim, the maps stop.

Not Ori's map. Ori's map goes wherever Ori goes. But every other map in the house, Rimuru's and Fern's and the old survey Senku copied, stops at the basin. Past it, on all of them, there's nothing. White paper.

Ori climbs out over the far rim of the basin and stands at the top and looks.

A long valley. Falling away in front of them, full of colour for Yuka and grey for Mau and whatever it is for Ori, going down and down between two long hills. A stream at the bottom, catching the light. Trees along the stream, thicker as it goes down. And at the very bottom, where the valley opens out, a stand of trees older and bigger than any of the others, spreading, crowding together.

Nobody has been here.

Not anyone from the house. Mau can feel it. There's no path. No mark. Just the grass and the slope and the long fall of the valley to the old trees at the bottom.

Ori takes a breath so big her whole satchel lifts.

“*Nobody*,” she says. ᴼ

“Nobody,” says Mau. ᴹ

Ori looks at her compass. Still pointing back, south, over the basin, toward the house. She looks at the valley.

She grins so wide her hat slips.

“Come *on*,” she says, and starts down.

* * *

The trees at the bottom are pears.

Old ones. Wild ones. Gone back to whatever pears were before anyone grew them. Their branches twisted and low and tangled together, and heavy, so heavy they bend to the grass, with small hard fruit, half the size of the orchard pears at home.

Ori stands under the first one with her mouth open.

She reaches up and picks one. She looks at it. She bites it.

She makes the face. The pickle face.

“It's *terrible*,” she says, delighted. ᴼ

Yuka picks one. Bites it. Makes the same face.

“It's terrible,” she agrees. ʸ

Beru picks one. He doesn't bite it. He holds it very carefully in one claw and looks at it, and then at Ori, and then puts it somewhere about his person for safekeeping, as if it were treasure.

Ori sits down under the tree with her map.

She draws the valley. The stream. The trees.

And at the bottom, where the pears are, she writes, very carefully, in her best handwriting:

*ORI'S PEARS.*

Then she looks at it. Then she crosses out *ORI'S* and writes *THE FORT'S*.

Then she looks at that. Then she crosses it out too, and writes, very small:

*EVERYONE'S PEARS (TERRIBLE)*

* * *

They camp under the trees.

Mau takes the tent out of the shadow and puts it up, with Ori giving instructions and Beru holding the ropes, both ropes, in case one rope. Yuka gathers wood. Kamish comes down out of the sky at dusk and lies down along the far side of the meadow, and the meadow is suddenly much smaller. His body runs the whole length of it, a long dark ridge between the pear trees and the night, with his tail curled round the far end very carefully so as not to flatten anything. He puts his head down by the stream and closes his eyes.

Mau lights the fire with a small spell. The first one Frieren ever taught him that wasn't flowers.

They eat the good pears from home, and Shuna's rolls, and the honey cake, and a pot of something Yuka makes over the fire that is mostly rice and mostly burnt and which Ori declares the best thing she's ever eaten.

The stars come out.

* * *

Yuka keeps her word.

She sleeps in the tent. Not on him. In the tent. On the thick blanket, with Ori in the middle and Mau on the other side, the three of them in a row under the canvas with Beru folded up across the doorway and Kamish's great slow breathing outside like the sea.

Ori isn't asleep.

She's lying on her back between them, looking up at the canvas. The tent flap is tied open, and through it, past Beru, you can see a slice of sky.

“Yuka,” she says. ᴼ

“Mm.” ʸ

“What's a satellite?” ᴼ

Yuka turns her head.

“Umaru won't stop talking about them,” says Ori. “Since the roof. Since Rem. She says they found one. She says it's *official*. But she won't tell me what it *is*. She just says *you'll see*.” ᴼ

Yuka is quiet for a moment.

Then she tells her.

Mau lies on the other side of Ori and listens. He doesn't know much about satellites. He doesn't remember enough about the world they come from. But Yuka does. She tells Ori about people, in another world, a long way away, who built small machines and sent them up past the sky, so high they never came down, so high they went round and round the whole world forever. How they catch the sun. How at night, if you know where to look, you can see them moving. Very slowly. Very small. A star that isn't a star, going across the sky in a straight line.

Ori listens to all of it.

“Are there any here?” she says. ᴼ

“No,” says Yuka. “Nobody's ever built one here. Nobody's ever sent one up.” ʸ

Ori is quiet for a long time.

“But Umaru looks anyway,” she says. ᴼ

“Umaru looks anyway,” says Yuka. ʸ

“Because one day there might be one.” ᴼ

“Because one day there might be one.” ʸ

Ori turns her head and looks out through the tent flap at the slice of sky.

“I'm going to look too,” she says. ᴼ

* * *

She looks for a long time.

Mau feels her looking. Lying very still between them, with her eyes wide open, watching the slice of sky through the tent flap. Every star. Checking each one. Waiting for one to move.

None of them do.

After a while, she stops looking for satellites. He feels the moment it happens. Something in her goes soft and wide. She stops checking. She just looks.

At the stars. All of them. More than anyone could ever count, crowding the slice of sky, so thick and bright in the dark valley where nobody has ever been that they look like something spilled.

“They're enough,” says Ori. Very quietly. Almost asleep. ᴼ

“What are?” says Mau. ᴹ

“The stars,” says Ori. “Even without satellites. They're enough.” ᴼ

And she's asleep.

Just like that. All at once. The way Yuka goes.

Yuka, on the other side of her, reaches across her in the dark and finds Mau's hand, over the top of Ori, and holds it.

Neither of them says anything.

Outside, Kamish breathes like the sea. Beru shifts in the doorway. The fire ticks down to embers.

Somewhere far behind them, over the basin and the ridge and the hills, the needle of a small old compass hanging round a sleeping child's neck points steadily home.
