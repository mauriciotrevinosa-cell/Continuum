*When: B+472. "15." Under it, in Yuka's hand: "Tsurugi (finally)".*

Tsurugi has been strange with her since the gate.

Not bad strange. Loud strange. Yuka noticed it the day he arrived and hasn't stopped noticing. He hugged her in the yard too long, that first day, and then let go and was immediately, enormously, cheerfully *Tsurugi*. Telling stories. Telling everyone about Mau, at the long table, until Mau wanted to sink through the floor. Laughing at everything. Arguing with Dabura about weight classes. Teaching Milim a game from their world. Being everywhere, all the time, with everyone.

Except her.

He's never once been alone with her. Not in nearly eight weeks. Every time she's come into a room where he is, he's found a reason to be talking to someone else. Every time she's gone looking for him, he's been somewhere else.

She'd let him be after the quarry. He had apologized to Mau for talking Dabura up into a challenge, and helped Maru with the people coming down from the north. Every time she thought she could ask him about the rest he was doing something she knew somebody needed help with.

She has room now.

* * *

She corners him on the training ground in the morning.

He's alone, for once, doing the drills their grandmother taught them both, a long time ago, in another world, with a wooden staff. The same drills. The same order. Yuka could do them in her sleep.

She walks onto the training ground and picks up the other staff from the rack, and stands in front of him.

He stops.

He looks at her. His face does the thing it's been doing for nearly eight weeks: opens up into the big cheerful Tsurugi grin, ready to say something loud and funny.

“Don't,” says Yuka. ʸ

The grin stops halfway.

“Why won't you be alone with me?” she says. ʸ

* * *

He doesn't answer straight away.

He stands there with the staff in his hands and the grin gone and his face, underneath it, looking very young. Younger than she's seen it since they were children. Since before she got sick.

“I've been busy,” he says. ᵀˢ

“No you haven't.” ʸ

“There's a lot going on. The Simurians. Dabura. The—” ᵀˢ

“Tsurugi.” ʸ

He stops.

He looks at the staff in his hands. He turns it round. He doesn't look at her.

“I thought you were getting one person back.” He rubs his thumb along the worn end of the staff. “I knew Cross had looked for you. I didn't ask myself what he wanted when he came here. And after the fight I kept thinking I was the one who brought all of it to your door.” ᵀˢ

Yuka puts her staff down. “You brought people home, Tsurugi. Cross decided what to do when he got here. So did I.” ʸ

He looks up long enough to see she means it. Then his eyes drop to her hand.

“The ring,” he says. ᵀˢ

* * *

Yuka goes very still.

“When you—” He stops. Starts again. “After Grandpa died. They gave me the ring. His ring. The one he gave you on the beach when you were six. The one you wore on the cord. They took it off you and gave it to me.” ᵀˢ

“I know,” says Yuka. ʸ

“They said I deserved it more,” says Tsurugi. “They said I was more suited. Stronger. Better technique. A real sorcerer. And I—” ᵀˢ

His hands tighten on the staff.

“I let them,” he says. “I was a kid and I let them. I didn't say *it's hers*. I didn't say *give it back*. I just took it. Because part of me thought they were right. Part of me *wanted* it.” ᵀˢ

He looks up at her finally.

“And then you got sick,” he says. “And then you were gone. You just—stopped being anywhere. And I had his ring on my finger and you were gone, and I never said sorry. I never gave it back. I never said anything.” ᵀˢ

His voice cracks.

“And then I came here,” he says, “and you were *alive*. And happy. And married. And you had his ring again. *Continuum brought it.* Maki told me. It was across your room, off the cord, the night the coin found you, and it came with you.” ᵀˢ

He looks at her hand. At the ring on it.

“And I couldn't look at you,” he says. “Every time I looked at you I saw it. On your hand. Where it should always have been. And I thought, she knows. She knows I took it. And I couldn't—I didn't know how to—” ᵀˢ

He stops.

“So I was loud,” he says. “And I told stories. And I made sure I was never alone with you. Because if I was alone with you, I'd have to say it.” ᵀˢ

* * *

Yuka puts the staff down.

She walks across the training ground to her brother.

He flinches, a little, as if he thinks she's going to hit him. She doesn't. She stands in front of him and looks up at him, because he got tall, somewhere in the years she wasn't there, taller than her, taller than Grandpa ever was.

“I knew,” she says. ʸ

He stares at her.

“I knew you let them,” says Yuka. “I watched you let them. I was there. And I was so angry. For years. I hated you a bit, I think. Every time I saw it on your hand.” ʸ

He doesn't say anything. He looks like someone waiting for the rest of a blow.

“And then I got sick,” says Yuka. “And it stopped mattering. And then I came here and it came with me. And I stopped being angry somewhere along the way and I didn't notice.” ʸ

She looks at the ring on her own hand.

“I'm not angry now,” she says. “I haven't been for a long time. You were a kid. They were the grown-ups. They told you that you deserved it, and you were a kid and you wanted to believe them. That's all. That's all it was.” ʸ

She looks back up at him.

“You don't have to hide from me,” she says. “You never did. You're my brother. I missed you.” ʸ

* * *

Tsurugi's face crumples.

All at once. The way it used to when they were very small and he'd fallen off something and was trying not to cry in front of her.

He doesn't try now.

She puts her arms round him. He's so tall that her face only comes to his chest. He puts his arms round her and holds on, very hard, and shakes, and doesn't say anything, and doesn't need to.

They stand like that on the training ground for a long time.

* * *

Maru finds them like that.

He's crossing the yard toward the river path, alone, and he sees them, and stops. He doesn't come closer. He stands at the edge of the training ground with his hands at his sides, the way his people stand, and waits until they let go of each other.

Then he says, quietly: “I'm sorry. I didn't mean to—” ᴹᵃʳᵘ

“It's fine,” says Tsurugi, wiping his face with his sleeve. Then he stops. Glances at Yuka. “I mean. It's not *fine*. It's good. It's good, actually.” ᵀˢ

Yuka laughs.

Maru almost smiles. Then his face goes thoughtful.

“Brothers,” he says. ᴹᵃʳᵘ

They look at him.

“I have one too,” says Maru. ᴹᵃʳᵘ

* * *

He doesn't say much. Maru never does. But he says enough.

That Cross is quiet these days. Very quiet. Since the workshop. Since Mau told him not to say his name. Since the orchard and Ori and Beru's cry.

That Cross has been helpful. Very helpful. Helping Shuna carry things. Helping Mai sweep the workshop. Helping Senku with the boiler. Fixing the split training post without being asked. Smiling at everyone. Saying thank you for everything.

“Too helpful,” says Maru. ᴹᵃʳᵘ

Yuka looks at him.

“I don't know if it's real,” says Maru. “He's my brother. I want it to be real. I want him to be sorry. Maybe he is. He's never been very good at being sorry. Maybe this is what it looks like when he tries.” ᴹᵃʳᵘ

He's quiet for a moment.

“Or maybe he's waiting,” he says. “For everyone to stop watching.” ᴹᵃʳᵘ

He looks at Yuka.

“I'm watching,” he says. “I wanted you to know. I'm keeping him away from Ori, like I promised. And I'm watching.” ᴹᵃʳᵘ

“Thank you,” says Yuka. ʸ

Maru nods. And then, unexpectedly, he looks at Tsurugi, and at Yuka, and at the place where they were holding each other a minute ago.

“I hope mine says it one day,” he says. “The real thing. Like yours did.” ᴹᵃʳᵘ

And he goes on toward the river.

* * *

Yuka picks the staff back up.

She looks at Tsurugi.

Tsurugi looks at the staff. Then at her. And something comes into his face that she hasn't seen since they were children, in their grandmother's yard, in another world, with two wooden staffs and nobody watching.

“You'll lose,” he says. ᵀˢ

“I won't,” says Yuka. ʸ

They spar.

The old drills, and then not the drills. The way they used to, before any of it. Before the ring. Before the hospital. She's faster than she was. He's stronger than he was. Neither of them is holding anything back.

She wins.

She knocks the staff out of his hands and puts the end of hers against his chest, and he stands there with his hands up, laughing.

“I let you,” he says. ᵀˢ

“You did *not*,” says Yuka. ʸ

“I absolutely let you.” ᵀˢ

He didn't. She knows he didn't. She saw his face when the staff left his hands.

She lets him think she thinks he let her.

That's what sisters are for.
