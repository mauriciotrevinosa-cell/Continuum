## Chapter 20 — Himmel's Hour

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 85
**Reading order:** 20

*When: B+477. "10." Ori has drawn two small figures on a step, side by side, under a lot of stars.*

Himmel's hour is at dusk.

He's kept it since long before Mau can remember. Every evening, when the light goes long, he goes out onto the porch and sits on the top step, alone, and watches the yard go dark. He doesn't do anything. He doesn't talk to anyone. When people ask him why, he smiles the hero's smile and says *it's my hour*, and they leave him to it.

Mau has never asked.

These last few weeks, Frieren has started sitting with him.

Not every evening. Most. She comes out with her tea a few minutes after he does and sits down on the step beside him, not close, near, and drinks her tea, and watches the yard with him, and neither of them says anything.

Mau has noticed. Everyone has noticed. Nobody has said anything.

* * *

Tonight he comes out onto the porch at dusk to fetch Ori, who is supposed to be inside, and finds the two of them on the top step.

He stops in the doorway.

“Sorry,” he says. “I didn't mean to—” ᴹ

“Stay,” says Frieren. ᶠ

He looks at her.

She doesn't usually say that. She doesn't usually say much of anything during Himmel's hour. But she's turned her head to look at him over her shoulder, with her teacup in both hands, and she means it.

“Sit,” she says. “I'd like you here. I'm going to say something and I'd like someone there who knows what it is.” ᶠ

Himmel, beside her, looks at her. Then at Mau. And moves along the step a little, to make room.

Mau sits down on the step below them.

* * *

Frieren doesn't say it straight away.

She drinks her tea. She watches the yard. The training posts going dark. The orchard going dark. Kamish along the bottom of the orchard with his head at the old pear, a long dark ridge longer than the house. The lamps coming on in the kitchen window behind them.

“I said something to Mau,” she says at last. “On the day before the quarry. The Day Between. When he was about to go and fight Dabura, and Yuka was in the orchard with Cross, and everything was wrong.” ᶠ

Himmel waits.

“I told him,” says Frieren, “that I thought there would be time. With you. That I thought I could understand you later. When it was convenient. So I didn't ask. And you were kind, and I was kind back, and neither of us said anything.” ᶠ

She turns her cup in her hands.

“And I told him it took me ten years and a funeral,” she says, “to find out what I'd missed.” ᶠ

* * *

Himmel doesn't say anything for a long time.

He's looking at the yard. His face, in the last light, isn't doing the hero's smile. It isn't doing anything. It's just his face. Older than it looks, somehow. Younger than it should be.

“I know,” he says. ᴴⁱᵐ

Frieren looks at him.

“I always knew,” says Himmel. “That you'd get there. Eventually. That you'd understand.” ᴴⁱᵐ

He smiles. Not the hero's smile. A smaller one.

“I just didn't think I'd be there to see it,” he says. “In your world, I wasn't. I was a statue, by then. A lot of statues. You understood me in front of statues.” ᴴⁱᵐ

Frieren's hands go very still on her cup.

“And then I woke up here,” says Himmel. “And you were here. And I thought, well. Maybe this time. Maybe this time I get to be there when she does.” ᴴⁱᵐ

He looks at her.

“So I kept my hour,” he says. “Every evening. On the step. In case.” ᴴⁱᵐ

* * *

Mau, on the step below, doesn't move.

He understands, suddenly, what the hour is. What it's always been. Every evening for longer than he's known Himmel. Sitting on the top step at dusk, alone, watching the yard.

Waiting.

Not for anything. For *someone*. To come out and sit down beside him. To understand. In case, this time, she did.

And these last few weeks, she has been.

* * *

“I'm not going to wait this time,” says Frieren. ᶠ

Himmel goes very still.

“That's what I wanted to say,” says Frieren. “That's why I wanted Mau here. Because he heard the first part, and I wanted someone to hear this part.” ᶠ

She puts her cup down on the step beside her.

“I'm learning,” she says. “In this house. From all of them. From Yuka, who sat in a doorway. From Rem, who went up on a roof. From DIO, who couldn't say it, so he said it for her.” She glances at Mau. “From him. All of them say the thing now. The plain thing. Out loud. So nobody has to guess.” ᶠ

She looks at Himmel.

“I'm a thousand years old,” she says. “I'm very good at waiting. I'm the best at it of anyone who has ever lived. I could wait another ten years. Another hundred. And I'd tell myself there was time.” ᶠ

She shakes her head.

“I'm not going to,” she says. “I don't know what it is yet. What I feel. I'm still slow. I'm always slow. But I'm going to find out *with* you. Here. Now. Not after. Not in front of a statue.” ᶠ

She glances down at Mau on the step below.

“He offered something once,” she says. “A long time ago, near the start of all this. The elf route. Whether any of it could ever be shared with you. If we both wanted it.” ᶠ

Himmel goes very still.

“I told him not to hurry,” says Frieren. “I told him first I wanted to understand you. This you. What you want. What being here has meant to you. I knew so many things about you that I'd forgotten to ask you anything new.” ᶠ

She turns her cup in her hands.

“I'm still doing that,” she says. “I want the time to do it properly. Not to put it off. To use it. I'd like to know you before I know what I feel. Is that all right?” ᶠ

Himmel looks at her for a long moment.

“That's all I've ever wanted,” he says. “For you to want to know me.” ᴴⁱᵐ


* * *

Himmel doesn't say anything.

He looks at her for a long, long time, in the last of the light.

Then he smiles. The small one again.

“Then I'll keep my hour,” he says. “Every evening. You know where I'll be.” ᴴⁱᵐ

Frieren picks her tea back up.

“I know,” she says. ᶠ

She doesn't move away. She doesn't move closer either. She sits where she is, on the top step, near, and drinks her tea, and looks at the yard with him, the way she has every evening for weeks.

But something is different. Mau can feel it. The quiet between them isn't the old quiet anymore. It isn't waiting. It's two people sitting on a step, starting something, slowly, the way Frieren does everything.

* * *

Mau gets up very quietly.

Neither of them notices. They're sitting on the top step, side by side, not looking at each other, looking at the yard going dark. Neither of them is saying anything.

They don't need to.

He goes back inside. He doesn't close the door.

* * *

Ori is at the kitchen window.

She's been there the whole time. He knows it the moment he comes in. She's standing on a chair with her nose to the glass and her notebook in her hand, looking out at the porch.

She doesn't turn round.

“Ori,” he says. “Bed.” ᴹ

“In a minute,” says Ori. ᴼ

He comes and stands beside the chair and looks out too.

The porch. The step. Two figures on it, side by side, against the dark yard and the first stars.

Ori looks at them for a long time.

Then she opens her notebook. She doesn't write anything. She draws. Two small figures, on a step, side by side. And above them, lots of stars. More than she usually draws. More than she has ever drawn.

She closes the notebook.

“That's Mama,” she says. Very quietly. ᴼ

“Yes,” says Mau. ᴹ

“She's not waiting anymore,” says Ori. ᴼ

Mau looks down at her.

“How do you know?” ᴹ

Ori looks up at him with Frieren's eyes.

“Because she went out and sat down first,” she says. “Before he asked. You don't do that if you're still waiting.” ᴼ

She climbs down off the chair.

She goes to bed.

Mau stands at the window a while longer.
