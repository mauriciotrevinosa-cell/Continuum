## Chapter 21 — Date Night

**Continuity state:** B+481 ("6"). Mau asks Yuka out. Properly. For the first time since before the island he plans something himself and doesn't ask anyone how. He takes her back to the valley of everyone's pears, by Exchange to Kamish's shadow on the far rim, at sunset, with a blanket and a basket Shuna packed without asking questions. *When: B+481. "6." Under it, in Mau's hand, which still almost never appears: "date". Yuka has underlined it three times and drawn a pear.*

He asks her at breakfast.

He doesn't plan how. That's the thing. He planned everything else: the where and the when and the basket and the blanket and the way there. He spent two nights lying awake with her face in his shoulder working it out. But he didn't plan how to ask, because he's learned that much this month. You don't plan the plain thing. You just say it.

So at breakfast, while she's stealing his pickles, he says:

“Will you come out with me tonight?” ᴹ

She stops with a pickle halfway to her mouth.

“Out where?” ʸ

“It's a surprise.” ᴹ

She looks at him.

“You planned something,” she says. ʸ

“Yes.” ᴹ

“By yourself.” ʸ

“Yes.” ᴹ

“You didn't ask anyone how.” ʸ

“No,” he says. “Not even Ciel.” ᴹ

*He didn't*, says Ciel, inside, sounding faintly put out. *I've been extremely curious for two days.* ᶜ

Yuka eats the pickle. She makes the face.

“Yes,” she says. ʸ

* * *

Shuna packs the basket without asking questions.

He goes to her after breakfast and says *something for two, for tonight, to take somewhere*, and Shuna looks at him for a long moment and then nods and turns to the cupboard, and by the afternoon there's a basket on the kitchen table with a cloth over it, and a blanket folded on top. The thick one. Not the thin one.

He lifts the cloth.

Bread. The house loaf, the one he made. Cold fish, the good way, skin on. The honey and pear cake. Plum paste. A bottle of something. And in a small jar on its own, wrapped in a napkin folded into what is unmistakably a dragon: pickles.

He looks at Shuna.

Shuna is at the sink with her back to him.

“Ori folded the napkin,” she says, without turning round. ˢʰᵘ

* * *

He takes her by Exchange.

They stand in the yard at sunset, with the basket, and he holds out his hand, and she takes it, and the world folds round them like a coat.

Kamish is already there. He flew out an hour ago and settled himself on the far rim of the basin above the long valley, and folded his wings, and waited. His shadow is the anchor.

They come through into Kamish's shadow on the rim.

Yuka looks round.

And stops.

The valley goes down in front of them, long and falling, full of colour in the last of the sun for her and grey for him. The stream at the bottom catching the light. The old wild pear trees at the very bottom, heavy with small hard fruit.

Everyone's pears. Terrible.

She looks at him.

“Here,” she says. ʸ

“Here,” says Mau. ᴹ

* * *

They walk down.

Slowly, in the last of the light, with the basket and the blanket. Kamish stays on the rim. He puts his head down on his forelegs and watches them go, and doesn't follow. Mau told him he didn't need to. Kamish seemed to understand.

At the bottom, under the oldest pear tree, Mau spreads the blanket.

Yuka sits down on it. She looks up at the branches, heavy and low and tangled over her head. She reaches up and picks one of the small hard pears and looks at it.

“They're terrible,” she says. ʸ

“I know,” says Mau. ᴹ

She bites it. She makes the face.

“*So* terrible,” she says, delighted, and eats the whole thing. ʸ

* * *

They eat Shuna's basket in the dusk.

The bread. The fish. The cake. The bottle, which turns out to be something sweet and fizzy that Yuka likes and Mau doesn't and which she therefore drinks most of. The pickles, out of the jar with the dragon napkin, which Mau sets aside on her side of the blanket without thinking and she steals without thinking.

The stars come out.

One by one, and then all at once, the way they did the night of the expedition. Thick and bright over the valley where nobody has ever been. More than anyone could count.

Yuka lies back on the blanket and looks up.

Mau lies down beside her.

* * *

“I want to tell you something,” he says. ᴹ

She turns her head.

He doesn't look at her. He looks at the stars. It's easier.

“For two weeks,” he says, “I was afraid you'd go.” ᴹ

She goes still beside him.

“Not just afraid,” he says. “Sure. I was sure you'd go. I was getting out of the way so it wouldn't hurt when you did. I was practising for it. Like the Trial.” ᴹ

He breathes out.

“And I'm not afraid now,” he says. ᴹ

He turns his head and looks at her.

“And I've been trying to work out what the difference is,” he says. “Between then and now. What changed. Because you're the same. I'm the same. The house is the same.” ᴹ

She waits.

“It's that you told me the truth,” he says. “When it was ugly. In the clinic. You sat in Ori's chair and you said *I said it twice, I went for the worst thing I could find, I used you staying as a reason it was safe to hurt you.* You didn't make it nice. You didn't make it smaller. You said the ugly thing, all of it, plainly.” ᴹ

He looks at her face in the starlight. The only colour in the world, even in the dark.

“That's why I'm not afraid,” he says. “Because now I know you'll tell me. When it's ugly. You'll tell me. I don't have to guess anymore. I don't have to practise for it. If you ever wanted to go, you'd say so. To my face. And you haven't. So you're not going.” ᴹ

He stops.

“That's all,” he says. “That's what I wanted to tell you.” ᴹ

* * *

Yuka doesn't say anything for a long time.

She lies on the blanket under the terrible pear trees and looks at him, and he feels it down the line: everything going through her, very fast, very warm, too much to name.

Then she rolls over and puts her face in his shoulder.

“That's the most you've ever said at once,” she says, muffled. ʸ

“I know.” ᴹ

“Without me making you.” ʸ

“I know.” ᴹ

“I'm going to cry.” ʸ

“I know,” he says. “It's all right.” ᴹ

She cries. A little. Into his shoulder. The good kind.

Her sleeve has gone damp where she's been trying to wipe her face with it. Mau turns the cuff back for her. Underneath, the skin on her wrist is cold. He puts his mouth there, just where the cuff was, then holds her hand against the warm part of his throat.

“Come here,” she says. ʸ

He lifts his head. “I'm here.” ᴹ

“You're on that bit of blanket. This is the good bit.” ʸ

She rolls onto her back to show him the room she's made, about the width of a finger. He looks at it for a second, then sets his elbow past her shoulder and lets his head come down against her. She gets an arm round him before he's finished getting settled. He smells of the stream, where he rinsed their cups, and the last of the bread crust.

“Can I?” he asks, his nose brushing hers. ᴹ

She answers with her hand at his neck, drawing him down. His elbow shifts in the grass. The blanket wrinkles under her heel and she pushes it away, trying to get closer without breaking the kiss. He's smiling by the time they have to breathe. She kisses the smile. He chases one more when she starts to turn her head, and his lips land halfway to her ear.

“That's my ear,” she says. ʸ

“I got that.” ᴹ

He starts to take his weight onto his arm. She slips both hands under the edge of his cloak, palms flat on his back, and turns her cheek into his. He settles against her again. From here the stream sounds closer. He can feel the little hitch in her breathing taper off as she lets her fingers relax against his shirt.

Down in the grass beside the blanket, something drops out of a tree with a soft thud.

“One of them just fell,” says Mau. ᴹ

“It can stay there.” ʸ

“I was going to save it for you.” ᴹ

She pulls him back up to kiss him properly. By the time she lets him look for it, it's too dark to see it in the grass.

* * *

Much later, she lifts her head.

“When I asked you to stay on the porch, I was going to ask you a different thing.” She moves up against him before it occurs to her to explain. “I wanted to know if you were still thinking about what we were going to do together in a year.” ʸ

“Were you?” he asks. ᴹ

“I was thinking about next week at first. Then the other day you said Ori was going to need a bigger coat before it got cold, and I started thinking farther than that again.” She reaches far enough to take the edge of his sleeve. “I want tomorrow with you. And the rest of them.” ʸ

“I was thinking about the island,” he says. “One of these days we should get over there before the first bird wakes up.” ᴹ

“The bird's up before you, every time.” ʸ

“I'm going to ask you to get me up at a terrible hour for it.” ᴹ

She rests her chin against him. “That's one tomorrow.” ʸ

“Yeah. I'll come up with more.” ᴹ

She looks at him for a long, long time in the starlight.

Then she puts her face back in his shoulder.

“I'm keeping you,” she says. ʸ

“I know,” says Mau. ᴹ

* * *

They don't go home until very late.

When they finally climb back up the valley to the rim, with the empty basket and the blanket over Yuka's shoulders, Kamish is still there. Exactly where they left him. His head on his forelegs. His eyes half closed.

He opens one eye when they come up.

“There's a pear in your hood,” he says. ᴷ

Mau reaches behind his own neck. Nothing. Yuka touches the back of hers, and her hand comes away with a little hard pear in it.

“How long has that been there?” she asks. ʸ

“I was going to tell you at the bottom,” says Kamish. He closes his eye again. “You looked busy.” ᴷ

They step into Kamish's shadow, and the world folds round them like a coat, and they're home. In the yard. Under the stars. With the house dark and asleep and one lamp still lit in the kitchen window, where somebody left it for them.

Shuna. Always Shuna.
