# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 77
## Chapter 19 — A Shell for Ori

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 77
**Reading order:** 19
**Source episode:** S13E19
**Continuity state:** B+303, island day eleven, clear after the rain. A day in the village by the water with Elmesia's card: stalls, a straw hat Mau buys that is far too big and Yuka wears all day on principle, small gifts chosen for people at home, and a long search on the wet sand for Ori's shell. The house is thought of fondly, not missed yet. No danger, no new topic. Claude, by Mauri's request; bracketed notes are for Work.

*When: The morning after the rain, when the whole island smells of wet stone and the boats are already out.*

“We promised Ori a shell,” Yuka says at breakfast. ʸ

“A good one,” Mau agrees. ᴹ

“We haven't found one.” ʸ

“We haven't looked.” ᴹ

She puts her cup down with the air of someone who has just been reminded of a duty.

“Today,” she says. “We find the shell today.” ʸ

* * *

The village is a short walk along the shore from the hotel.

They have seen it from the air and from the boat, low roofs and a little harbor and stalls along the water, but they have never gone into it. Every day there has been something else. Now there isn't, and the rain has washed the sand flat and smooth, and Yuka has the card in her pocket and the bad sandals on her feet.

The stalls are just opening when they get there.

Fruit, piled in pyramids. Fish laid out on wet boards. Bread. A man frying something small and round in a great black pan, and the smell of it reaches them from three stalls away. Cloth, hanging in long strips that move in the wind. Baskets. Little carved things, birds and fish and boats, set out in rows on a blanket by an old woman who watches them come with sharp, interested eyes.

Yuka stops at every single stall.

Mau lets her. He walks a step behind her with his hands in his pockets and watches her pick things up and turn them over and put them down again, and ask questions, and laugh at the answers. She shows the card to the man with the frying pan and he gives her two of the round things in a paper twist and won't take anything for them. She gives one to Mau without looking, the way she gives him things at home, and keeps walking.

It's hot and sweet and burns his tongue. He eats it anyway.

* * *

He buys her a hat.

He doesn't plan to. They pass a stall hung with straw hats, wide ones, the kind the fishermen wear against the sun, and he sees one with a brim so enormous it could shade a small table, and he thinks of her on the balcony squinting into the light, and before he can think better of it he has paid for it and put it on her head.

It comes down over her eyes.

All the way down. To her nose. She stands in the middle of the market with the hat resting on her face, and doesn't move.

“Mau,” she says, from under it. ʸ

“It's a bit big.” ᴹ

“I can't see.” ʸ

“I'll lead you.” ᴹ

She pushes the brim up with one finger and looks at him from underneath it. The woman at the hat stall is laughing so hard she has to hold onto the post.

“I'm wearing it,” Yuka says. ʸ

“You don't have to—” ᴹ

“You bought it for me. I'm wearing it. All day.” ʸ

And she does. She tilts it back so she can see out from under it and wears it the whole length of the market, through the whole morning, with the brim flapping in the wind and knocking into things, and every time somebody looks at it she says *my husband bought it for me* with such dignity that nobody dares to laugh until she has gone past.

Mau has never loved her more.

* * *

They buy things for the house.

It happens without either of them deciding it. Yuka picks up a little carved bird from the old woman's blanket and turns it over and says *Frieren*, because it's the same bird that steals from Mau's plate, and Frieren will want to hear about the bird. So they buy it.

Then there is a jar of the sharp sweets, and Yuka says *Umaru*, and they buy that too. A wooden spoon with a long handle, for Rem. A tin of the coffee from the little stall, a second one, for Gojo, who will drink the first one too fast. A strip of the cloth with a pattern Yuka says Mai will want to take apart to see how it was woven. A little carved boat for Shizue. A knife with a bone handle for Maki, which the man at the stall assures them is very sharp, and which Yuka tests on her thumb, and then has to suck the thumb.

“Is it sharp?” Mau asks. ᴹ

“It's sharp,” she says, around her thumb. ʸ

They buy it.

By midday his inventory has a whole shelf of things in it, each one with a name. He finds that he likes carrying them. It's like carrying the house around in his pocket.

* * *

They still haven't found the shell.

There are shells on the stalls. Plenty of them. Polished and arranged, in baskets, some of them very beautiful. Yuka looks at all of them and shakes her head at all of them.

“Not these,” she says. ʸ

“Why not?” ᴹ

“Because we have to find it. Ori said *from here*. She meant found here. Not bought.” ʸ

Mau looks at her under the enormous hat.

He doesn't know whether Ori meant that or not. He has a feeling that Ori would be perfectly happy with a polished shell from a basket. But he can see that it matters to Yuka, and that it matters to her that it matters to Ori, and so it matters to him.

“Then we find it,” he says. ᴹ

* * *

They look for the rest of the afternoon.

They walk the long beach on the far side of the village, where the rain has left the sand flat and wet and full of things the sea brought in during the night. Weed. Driftwood. Bits of glass worn smooth. Shells, hundreds of them, broken and whole.

Yuka is very particular.

She picks up shells and looks at them and puts them back. Too small. Too broken. Too ordinary. One is perfect except for a chip on the edge, and she holds it a long time before she puts it back too. Mau carries the ones she can't decide about in his hands until his hands are full, and then she makes him put them all back.

“None of these?” he asks. ᴹ

“None of these,” she says. ʸ

The sun moves across the sky. The hat flaps in the wind. Mau starts to think they might be here until dark.

Then she stops.

* * *

It's half buried in the wet sand at the edge of the water, where the waves are just reaching.

She kneels and digs it out with her fingers. It's a spiral shell, the size of her palm, smooth and whole, curling round into a point at one end and opening wide at the other. When she holds it up, the light goes through the thin parts of it.

Mau can't see what color it is.

He can see that it's beautiful. The shape of it, the way it turns in on itself, the way the light comes through. He can see her face, looking at it, lit up with the kind of quiet happiness she gets when she has found exactly the thing she was looking for.

“This one,” she says. ʸ

“What color is it?” he asks. ᴹ

She looks at it, then at him.

“Pink on the inside,” she says. “Pale, like the inside of your ear. And the outside has lines, brown and white, going round.” ʸ

She turns it in her fingers.

“Like your hair,” she says. ʸ

He laughs.

“Ori will like that,” he says. ᴹ

“Ori will love that,” Yuka says. “She'll put it next to the window so she can see it from the bed.” ʸ

She holds it out to him. He puts it in his inventory carefully, on its own, not with the other gifts.

* * *

They walk back along the beach as the sun goes low.

Yuka has taken off the bad sandals and is carrying them in one hand, swinging them, and holding his hand with the other. The hat is still on her head. The brim has gone soft and floppy in the wind and comes down over one eye, and she doesn't fix it.

“Do you think they're all right?” she says, after a while. ʸ

“At home?” ᴹ

“Mm.” ʸ

He thinks about the shelf of gifts in his pocket. A bird for Frieren. Sweets for Umaru. A knife for Maki. A shell for Ori.

“They're fine,” he says. “They're probably having a very nice time without us.” ᴹ

“Without us,” Yuka repeats, as though she doesn't quite believe it. ʸ

She doesn't say anything else about it. But she squeezes his hand a little harder, and they walk the rest of the way back to the hotel without talking, and when they get to the steps she stops and looks back at the beach for a long moment before she goes up.
