# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 77
## Chapter 20 — The Rock in the Afternoon

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 77
**Reading order:** 20
**Source episode:** S13E19
**Continuity state:** B+304, island day twelve. Yuka finally goes back to the rock in afternoon light, as she wanted on the second day. Snorkeling; a turtle; a playful contest over which of them a small fish prefers, which Mau tries to win with the gentlest Authority and Yuka declares cheating. Long afternoon lying on warm sand. DIO and Elmesia pass with more sea glass. No danger, no new topic.

*When: The afternoon after the village, when the sun has come round to the far side of the rock.*

“You said it might look completely different,” Mau says. ᴹ

They're standing on the wet sand at the far end of the beach, by the rock where they swam on the second day. Yuka has her mask pushed up on her forehead and the hat, the enormous hat, left on the sand behind them with a stone on the brim to keep the wind from taking it.

“I did say that,” she says. ʸ

“Does it?” ᴹ

She looks at the water behind the rock for a long time.

“Yes,” she says. ʸ

He waits for her to tell him how. She doesn't, at first. She just looks. He has learned that this is part of it, the looking, that she needs to have it for herself first before she can give any of it to him.

“In the morning it was all one color,” she says at last. “Clear, all the way down. Now there's light on the bottom. Moving. Like a net.” ʸ

He looks. He can see it. Not the color, but the light itself, shifting on the sand below the surface, bright lines breaking and joining and breaking again as the water moves over them.

“I can see that,” he says. ᴹ

She turns and looks at him, pleased, the way she always is when they find something they can both see the same.

“Then come and look at it closer,” she says. ʸ

* * *

The water is warmer than it was in the morning.

They wade out past the little drop and put their faces under, and the world goes quiet. Just the sound of their own breathing through the tubes, and the soft knock of water against the rock.

The light is everywhere. On the sand, on the rock, on the fish. It slides over Yuka's back as she floats beside him, over her arms, over the hand she holds out to him under the water. He takes it.

The three small fish are still there.

They come out of their gap in the rock the way they did on the second day, one and then the other two, and hang in the water in front of the rock as though they are waiting for something. Yuka points at them with her free hand. Mau nods, which is difficult to do underwater, and makes her laugh into her snorkel.

They watch the fish for a long time.

* * *

Then the turtle comes.

Mau sees it first, a long way off, a shape moving slowly along the bottom at the edge of the clear water. He doesn't know what it is until it comes closer. Then he squeezes Yuka's hand so hard that she turns her whole body to look.

It's enormous. Bigger than both of them together. It swims past the rock at its own pace, its flippers moving slow and steady, its old head turning a little as it goes, as though it is looking at them as much as they are looking at it.

Yuka goes absolutely still.

So does Mau.

The turtle passes so close that he could reach out and touch its shell, and he doesn't. He just floats there with Yuka's hand in his and watches it go by, unhurried, the light from the surface sliding over its back. It reaches the edge of the clear water and goes on into the darker water beyond, and gets smaller, and is gone.

They come up at the same moment.

Yuka pulls her mask off and stares at him with her mouth open, water running down her face.

“Did you see—” ʸ

“I saw.” ᴹ

“It was *right there*.” ʸ

“It was.” ᴹ

“It looked at me.” ʸ

“It looked at both of us.” ᴹ

“It looked at *me*,” Yuka says, and her face is so bright that he can't argue. ʸ

* * *

The argument about the fish starts half an hour later.

They've gone back under. The three small fish have come out again. One of them, the smallest, keeps swimming up close to Yuka's mask and hanging there, looking at her through the glass, before darting back to the rock. It does this four times.

When they surface, Yuka says:

“That fish likes me.” ʸ

“Which fish?” ᴹ

“The small one. It keeps coming to look at me. It doesn't come to look at you.” ʸ

Mau considers this.

“It's probably looking at itself in your mask,” he says. ᴹ

Yuka looks at him with great disdain.

“It likes me,” she says. ʸ

They go back under. The small fish comes up to Yuka's mask again. Mau watches it hang there, looking at her.

He shouldn't do it. He knows he shouldn't. But it's very small, and very close, and he can feel exactly how little it would take.

He reaches out with the faintest touch of Authority, the smallest grip he has ever used, so light it wouldn't move a feather, and turns the fish round in the water. Gently. Just enough that it's facing him instead of her.

The fish looks at him.

It hangs there for a moment, startled, in front of his mask. Then it darts back to its rock.

Yuka comes up out of the water so fast she nearly hits him.

* * *

“*You cheated.*” ʸ

“I didn't—” ᴹ

“You turned it round. I saw you. You used your thing.” ʸ

“I barely touched it.” ᴹ

“You used Authority on a *fish*. To make it look at you.” ʸ

“It was a very small amount of Authority.” ᴹ

Yuka stands in the waist-deep water with her mask pushed up and her hands on her hips, staring at him with an expression of such enormous betrayal that he starts laughing and can't stop.

“That doesn't count,” she says. ʸ

“It looked at me.” ᴹ

“Because you *made* it.” ʸ

“It still looked.” ᴹ

She splashes him. A whole armful of water, right in the face. He comes up spluttering, and she does it again, and he grabs her round the waist and pulls her under with him, and they come up together, both laughing, her arms round his neck.

“The fish likes me,” she says, very close to his face. ʸ

“The fish likes you,” he agrees. ᴹ

“Say it properly.” ʸ

“The fish likes you better than me, and I am a cheat, and I'm sorry.” ᴹ

She looks at him for a long moment.

“You're forgiven,” she says. ʸ

He braces his feet when a swell comes up behind her. Yuka's heels lift off the sand and her arms tighten around his neck. He keeps both hands at her waist until the water goes down again.

“Still forgiven?” he asks. ᴹ

She nods. She has to turn her head a little to get around the mask on his forehead. The first thing he tastes is salt. The next thing he feels is her laugh when the edge of his mask comes down across his eyes.

Mau pushes it up. Yuka lifts it the rest of the way off him, loops the strap around her wrist and kisses him again before he can do anything about the state of his hair. He lets one hand slide to the small of her back. This time when the water lifts her, he feels her trust him with her weight before she even notices her feet have left the bottom.

By the time they break apart there's a ripple at his elbow, just enough to make him look down. The little fish hangs there for a moment and darts past him to Yuka's side.

Mau opens his mouth.

“You already said it properly,” she says. ʸ

She gives him his mask back. He lets her get a whole step toward the beach before splashing her.

* * *

Afterwards, they lie on the sand.

It's warm from the afternoon sun, and the hat is still where they left it with the stone on the brim. Yuka lies on her back with her eyes closed and her arms spread out. Mau lies beside her. Neither of them says anything for a long time.

Then Yuka rolls over and lies on top of him.

She does it the way she does everything when she wants to be close: completely, without warning, all her weight. She settles with her stomach on his and her arms folded on his chest and her chin on her arms, so she can look at his face.

“Hello,” she says. ʸ

“Hello.” ᴹ

“You're warm.” ʸ

“So are you.” ᴹ

She lies there and looks at him. He lies there and lets her. The sand is warm under his back and she is warm on top of him and the sea is going in and out a little way off, and there is nothing in the world either of them has to do.

“What are you thinking about?” he asks, after a while. ᴹ

“Nothing,” she says. ʸ

“Really nothing?” ᴹ

She thinks about it.

“The turtle,” she says. “And that I'm hungry. And that I like your face.” ʸ

“That's three things.” ᴹ

“They're all very small,” she says, and puts her cheek down on her arms and closes her eyes. ʸ

* * *

DIO and Elmesia come along the beach a little later.

They're walking slowly at the water's edge within reach of the shelter, heads down, looking at the sand. DIO has her hands cupped together in front of her. When they get close, she stops and looks at Mau and Yuka lying on the sand, one on top of the other, and a small smile crosses her face.

“Don't let us disturb you,” Elmesia says. ᴱˡ

“You're not,” Yuka says, without opening her eyes. ʸ

DIO opens her hands. There are three pieces of glass in them, worn smooth by the sea, with the light coming through them.

“We found more,” she says. ᴰⁱᵒ

Yuka lifts her head to look. She tells Mau what colors they are without being asked: one pale, one darker, one almost clear. DIO listens to her describe them as though she's hearing something new about her own glass.

“They're for the house,” DIO says. “For the windowsills. I thought Ori might like one.” ᴰⁱᵒ

“She will,” Yuka says. “She'll put it next to her shell.” ʸ

DIO looks pleased. She closes her hands round the glass again and goes on along the beach with Elmesia, the two of them stopping every few steps to look at something.

Yuka puts her head back down on Mau's chest.

“Everybody's bringing Ori something,” she says. ʸ

“She's going to have a very full windowsill,” Mau says. ᴹ

“Good,” Yuka says. ʸ

They lie there until the sun goes low and the sand starts to cool, and then they get up, and Yuka puts on the enormous hat, and they walk back along the beach to the hotel with the turtle still swimming in both their heads.
