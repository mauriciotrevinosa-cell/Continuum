# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 78
## Chapter 3 — Something in Everyone's Shadow

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 78
**Reading order:** 3
**Source episode:** S13E23
**Continuity state:** B+308, morning. Mau proposes extending the emergency-anchor arrangement already used for Makima (V73#20), Hinata and Elmesia (V75) to everyone in the house who wants one: one ordinary soldier from the existing legion in each person's shadow, revocable, alerting only if the person asks or the soldier detects immediate danger; no routine reports; an alert does not guarantee an Exchange. Each placement is tested (call and withdrawal) in front of the person. Ori already has Lantern, whose bond with Mau stands. No new units; 6.617 bonds.

*When: The morning after the first full day home. Mau has been thinking about it since he woke up, which Yuka says she could tell, because he kept frowning at the ceiling.*

He brings it up at breakfast, because breakfast is the only time everyone is in one room without anyone having to be fetched.

He waits until Shuna has sat down. That's a rule nobody wrote: you don't start anything at the table while Shuna is still standing, because she'll stand for the whole of it. Then he puts down his cup and says, “I want to ask everybody something,” ᴹ and the noise around the table drops away in the way it does when Mau asks for something, which isn't often.

Yuka already knows. He told her last night. She puts her hand on his knee under the table and leaves it there.

“During the war,” he says, “the relay got marked complete when it wasn't. Yuka was holding the line on her own. Mahoraga was holding with her. I got there because Muster was close enough to use. If Muster hadn't been there, I'd have been a lot slower.” ᴹ

Nobody says anything. Most of them were there. Yuka's hand tightens on his knee, very slightly, and then relaxes.

“Makima has one of my soldiers in her shadow,” he goes on. “So does Hinata. So does Elmesia. You all know the terms. It doesn't watch. It doesn't report. It only wakes up if she asks for help or if something is about to hurt her badly, and then it tells me, and if I can get there, I can get there.” ᴹ

“And if you can't?” Maki asks. ᴹᵃᵏ

“Then I can't. It isn't a promise. It's a door. Sometimes a door's locked.” ᴹ

Maki nods slowly. That's the answer she wanted. Maki trusts things more when they come with the part where they fail.

“I'd like to offer the same thing to anybody here who wants it,” Mau says. “Not everybody. Anybody. You can say no. You can say yes and change your mind tomorrow, or in a year, and it comes out and nobody asks why. I'll show each of you how it goes in and how it comes out before it stays.” ᴹ

There's a pause.

Then Ori says, “I already have Lantern,” ᴼ in the tone of someone pointing out that she's ahead of the class.

“You do,” Mau agrees. “Lantern counts. If you want Lantern to be your door, he can be. You'd have to ask him.” ᴹ

Ori looks down at her shadow, where nothing at all is visible. “Lantern,” she says, “will you tell Dad if something bad happens?” ᴼ

The shadow under her chair moves. Just slightly, like water when a fish turns under it. Then it's still.

“He says yes,” Ori reports. ᴼ

“He didn't say anything,” Milim objects. ᴹⁱ

“He doesn't *talk*. He said yes with his shadow.” ᴼ

Milim considers this, decides it's fair, and goes back to her eggs.

* * *

Yuka goes first, because she said she would.

They do it in the yard, after breakfast, with everyone who wants to watch standing around on the porch. Bellion brings a soldier from the reserve: one of the ordinary ones, faceless, a shape that holds still when it's told and moves when it's needed, nothing about it to tell it apart from a hundred others. Mau has never named any of them. He knows every one.

“This one's quiet,” he says. “It's been on the north wall since the war. It doesn't like to be noticed.” ᴹ

“That sounds like you,” Yuka says. ʸ

“That's why I picked it.” ᴹ

She laughs. Then she stands very straight in the middle of the yard, the way she does when she's about to have something done to her that she agreed to, and holds out her hand.

The soldier goes into her shadow like a person stepping into water. There's no sound. For a second her shadow looks deeper than it ought to, darker at the edges, and then it settles and looks like a shadow again.

“Call it,” Mau says. ᴹ

“How?” ʸ

“You don't have to say anything. Just want me to come.” ᴹ

Yuka closes her eyes.

He feels it immediately: a tug, small and clear, somewhere under his breastbone, like a hand on his sleeve. It comes with a sense of direction, and a sense of *her*, and nothing else. No picture. No words. Just: *here*.

“Got it,” he says. ᴹ

She opens her eyes. “That's all?” ʸ

“That's all. If you'd been in trouble, it'd have come louder.” ᴹ

“Now take it out.” ʸ

He holds out his hand. The soldier rises out of her shadow and stands beside her, faceless and patient, and her shadow is only a shadow.

“Put it back,” Yuka says at once. ʸ

“You don't want to think about it?” ᴹ

“I thought about it last night. While you were frowning at the ceiling.” ʸ

He puts it back.

* * *

After that, it goes faster.

Shoko wants one. She asks practical questions first — what happens if she's in the middle of surgery and it decides she's in danger because there's blood, whether it can tell the difference between her hands being in someone else's chest and someone else's hands being in hers — and Mau answers them, and Ciel answers the ones he can't, and when Shoko is satisfied she holds out her hand without ceremony and says, “Fine. Do it.” ˢʰ

Gojo laughs at the whole idea.

“I'm the strongest,” he says, spreading his arms. “What is it going to warn you about? *Gojo is about to be mildly inconvenienced*?” ᴳ

Shoko looks at him.

She doesn't say anything. She just looks.

Gojo holds out his hand.

Maki says yes and Yuta says yes, and they do it together, side by side, because they do most things together. Mai says yes after asking whether the soldier would mind sitting very still for long hours in the workshop, and Mau tells her it would prefer that to anything else in the world, which is true. Senku asks if he can study the mechanism. Mau tells him he can look at it, but he can't take it apart. Senku says that's fine, he'll just look at it very closely. Fern says yes and writes it in the register. Shuna says yes. Rem looks at Umaru, and Umaru says, “I'll do it if you do,” ᵁ and Rem says, “Then we'll both do it,” ᴿᵉ and they hold hands while it happens, which nobody comments on.

Milim says no.

She says it very cheerfully, with no hesitation at all. “I don't need one,” she says. “If something's going to hurt me badly, I'll hurt it first. You can come later and help clean up.” ᴹⁱ

“That's fair,” Mau says. ᴹ

“But you can put one in Ori's room,” Milim adds. “In case Lantern's asleep.” ᴹⁱ

“Lantern doesn't sleep,” Ori says. ᴼ

“Everybody sleeps.” ᴹⁱ

“Dragons don't.” ᴼ

“*I* sleep, and I'm much bigger than a dragon.” ᴹⁱ

This argument goes on for some time.

Frieren is the last one on the porch. She has been watching the whole thing with her chin on her hand, and when Mau looks at her she doesn't say anything for a moment.

“You don't have to,” he says. ᴹ

“I know.” ᶠ

“You've been alone on roads for a thousand years. You don't need—” ᴹ

“I know what I've been,” Frieren says. ᶠ

She stands up. Comes down the porch steps. Holds out her hand to him, palm up, the way she holds it out when she wants him to take something from her and doesn't want to make a speech about it.

“Put it in,” she says. “If something happens to me, I'd like somebody to know.” ᶠ

He can't say anything for a second. He puts it in.

* * *

By noon there are thirty-one soldiers in thirty-one shadows, not counting Lantern, not counting Muster's forty on the walls and Bellion's reserve, not counting the ones that went with the injured man's family to see them safely home.

Mau sits on the porch steps afterward and feels them.

Not as voices. They don't speak. Not as eyes; they don't watch. It's more like knowing where all the doors in a house are, in the dark. He doesn't think about them, and doesn't have to. They're just there, at the edge of him: thirty-one small places where, if something went wrong, a hand would tug at his sleeve.

“Heavy?” Ciel asks quietly, inside. ᶜ

“No,” he says. Then, honestly: “A little. Not the way you mean.” ᴹ

“How, then?” ᶜ

He thinks about it.

“I keep thinking,” he says, “that I should have done this before. Before the war. Before—” ᴹ

He doesn't finish. Ciel doesn't make him.

“You did it now,” she says. ᶜ

“I did it now.” ᴹ

Yuka comes out of the house with two cups of something warm and sits down on the step beside him. She doesn't ask what he's thinking. She puts one cup in his hand and leans against him and drinks from the other, and after a while he leans back.

“Thirty-one,” she says. ʸ

“Thirty-one.” ᴹ

“Who's left?” ʸ

He doesn't have to count. He's already been thinking about it all morning.

“Jin-Woo,” he says. ᴹ
