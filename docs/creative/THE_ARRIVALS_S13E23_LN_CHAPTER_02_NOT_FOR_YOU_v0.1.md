The Sungs' house is the one past the orchard path, where the ground starts to slope down toward the stream.

Mau can hear Suho before he sees any of them. Suho is at the age where everything he does is at full volume, including standing still, and he is not standing still. He's running in circles in the yard with something held over his head, shouting, and behind him, at a dignified walk that somehow keeps up with him perfectly, comes one of Jin-Woo's shadows.

It's Igris. Or it isn't, exactly. Jin-Woo has his own Igris, the way Mau has his own; they're the same knight, from different sides of something neither of them fully understands. Jin-Woo's Igris is currently carrying a small wooden sword in one gauntleted hand and holding it very carefully out of Suho's reach, and Suho is jumping for it, and Igris is raising it exactly as high as he needs to, each time, and no higher.

Jin-Woo is sitting on the porch watching this with a cup of tea and an expression of complete peace.

“He's been doing that for an hour,” Jin-Woo says, when Mau comes up the path. ᴶʷ

“Igris or Suho?” ᴹ

“Both.” ᴶʷ

Mau sits down on the step below him. Suho sees him and shouts his name and then immediately forgets about him again, because Igris has lowered the sword by about a finger's width and that's much more important.

* * *

He doesn't know how to start, so he just says it.

“I put soldiers in the house's shadows this morning,” he says. “For emergencies. Same as Makima and Elmesia.” ᴹ

“I heard. Hae-In was there for some of it.” ᴶʷ

“I want to offer you one.” ᴹ

Jin-Woo looks at him.

Then he laughs. Not unkindly. It's a short, surprised sound, the laugh of someone who's been offered an umbrella while standing next to a lake.

“Mau,” he says. “I have an *army*.” ᴶʷ

“I know.” ᴹ

“I have a hundred of my own right here. More where they came from. I *am* the—” ᴶʷ

“It's not for you,” Mau says. ᴹ

Jin-Woo stops.

Mau looks out at the yard, where Suho has finally caught the sword, mostly because Igris has let him, and is now waving it around and declaring himself the winner of something.

“It's for them,” Mau says. “When you're here, they don't need anything from me. When you're not — when you're at a gate, or at the house, or somewhere I've dragged you to train — I'd like to know.” ᴹ

He waits.

“That's all,” he says. “If something happens to them, I'd like to know. I don't need to be the one who gets there first. I'd just like to know.” ᴹ

Jin-Woo doesn't say anything for a long time.

He's looking at Suho too. Suho has started teaching Igris how to hold the sword properly, which involves a great deal of correction, and Igris is accepting the correction with enormous patience. Somewhere inside, Mau can hear Jin-Ah arguing with her mother about something to do with dinner, and Hae-In laughing at both of them.

“You know what the worst part of the last war was,” Jin-Woo says finally. It isn't a question. ᴶʷ

Mau doesn't know which last war he means. Jin-Woo has had more of them than Mau has. He waits.

“Being somewhere else,” Jin-Woo says. “Fighting something that mattered, and knowing it mattered, and being somewhere else when something happened at home.” ᴶʷ

“Yeah,” Mau says. ᴹ

“You know that one.” ᴶʷ

“Yeah.” ᴹ

Jin-Woo turns his cup around in his hands.

“Ask them,” he says. “Not me. It's their shadows.” ᴶʷ

* * *

Hae-In says yes before he's finished explaining.

She's a hunter herself, a good one, and she understands immediately what it is and isn't. She asks one question, which is whether it would interfere with anything Jin-Woo has already put in place, and Jin-Woo says no, and she holds out her hand.

Jin-Ah wants to understand it first. She asks the same questions Shoko asked, almost word for word, and Mau answers them, and halfway through she starts writing the answers down in a small notebook she takes out of her pocket, and he has a sudden strong sense that she's going to get along with Fern frighteningly well. When she's satisfied, she says, “Okay,” ᴶᵃ and puts the notebook away and holds out her hand.

Their mother is harder.

She doesn't refuse. She listens to the whole explanation with her hands folded in her lap, very politely, and then she looks at her son, and then at Mau, and then she says, “Does it hurt?” in a voice that makes it clear she's not asking about herself.

“No,” Mau says. “You won't feel anything.” ᴹ

“Not me,” she says. “*You*. Does it hurt you, having all these things in all these people?” ᴶᵐ

He doesn't know what to say.

Nobody has asked him that. Ciel asked if it was heavy. That's not the same question.

“No,” he says at last. “It doesn't hurt.” ᴹ

She studies his face for a long moment, the way mothers do, and he has the distinct sense that she knows exactly how much of that is true. Then she holds out her hand anyway.

* * *

Suho wants one very badly, and wants to name it.

“You can't name it,” Jin-Ah tells him. “It's not a pet.” ᴶᵃ

“*Igris* has a name.” ˢᵘ

“Igris is a knight.” ᴶᵃ

“So is this one!” ˢᵘ

He points at the soldier Bellion has brought, which is standing in the yard looking exactly like every other ordinary soldier in Mau's legion: tall, faceless, unremarkable, a shape made of the absence of light.

“It's not a knight,” Jin-Ah says. ᴶᵃ

“It could be,” Suho says. ˢᵘ

Mau looks at Hae-In. Hae-In looks at Jin-Woo. Jin-Woo shrugs, very slightly, the shrug of a father who has already lost this argument several times and has made his peace with it.

“If your mother says yes,” Mau tells Suho, “you can give it a name. But it won't answer to it. It doesn't answer to anything except me.” ᴹ

“That's okay,” Suho says. “It'll know.” ˢᵘ

Hae-In nods. Suho stands very still in the middle of the yard with his whole body vibrating with the effort of standing still, and holds out his hand, and the soldier steps into his shadow, and he looks down at it with wonder.

“Sir Shadow,” he announces. ˢᵘ

Igris, behind him, makes a very small sound that might be a cough, if Igris could cough.

“That's a terrible name,” Jin-Ah says. ᴶᵃ

“It's a *good* name.” ˢᵘ

“It's literally just what it is.” ᴶᵃ

“That's why it's good.” ˢᵘ

Mau, privately, agrees with Suho.

* * *

When it's done, and Bellion has gone back to the house with the reserve, and Suho has gone inside to tell his grandmother about Sir Shadow for the fourth time, Mau sits on the porch step again and Jin-Woo sits above him.

“You didn't put one in me,” Jin-Woo says. ᴶʷ

“You said no.” ᴹ

“I said ask them.” ᴶʷ

Mau turns around.

Jin-Woo isn't looking at him. He's looking out at the yard, where the light is getting long and low across the grass, and his face has the stillness it gets sometimes when he's thinking about something very far away.

“Put one by the door,” he says. “Not in me. By the door. If I'm not here and something comes for them, and it gets through what I've left, I'd like you to know too.” ᴶʷ

Mau puts one by the door. It settles into the shadow under the porch roof, where the light never quite reaches, and goes still.

“Thank you,” Jin-Woo says, very quietly. ᴶʷ

“You'd have done it for me.” ᴹ

“I'd have done it for you,” Jin-Woo agrees. “And you'd have told me you had an army.” ᴶʷ

* * *

That night, in their room, he tells Yuka about the gate.

He's sitting on the edge of the bed. She's lying behind him with her head propped on her hand, drawing shapes on his back with one finger while he talks: the posts in the yard, the itch, Gojo's advice, the thirty-one soldiers and the one under Jin-Woo's porch.

“Tomorrow,” he says. “If it's all right. I'll ask Fern which gates nobody wants. I'll find one. I'll be back for dinner.” ᴹ

Her finger stops.

He waits for it. For the small silence before she says something careful. She's had reasons, before, to be careful when he says he'll be back.

The silence doesn't come. Her finger starts moving again, a slow circle between his shoulder blades.

“Go,” she says. “You've been vibrating since we got off the boat. It's like sleeping next to a bee.” ʸ

“I have not been vibrating.” ᴹ

“You jiggle your leg in your *sleep*, Mau.” ʸ

He didn't know that. He thinks about it. It's probably true.

“Come back for dinner,” she says. ʸ

“I'll come back for dinner.” ᴹ

“And tonight you're mine.” ʸ

She hooks her arm around his middle from behind and pulls, and he lets himself fall backward onto the bed, and she rolls half on top of him and settles there with her chin on his chest and looks at him with enormous satisfaction, as though she has just won something.

“That's not how you claim an evening,” he says. ᴹ

“It's how I claim one.” ʸ

He doesn't argue with that either.
