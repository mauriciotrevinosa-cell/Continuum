# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 78
## Chapter 6 — The Pillow

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 78
**Reading order:** 6
**Source episode:** S13E24
**Continuity state:** Inside the red gate, local days 1–6. The broken towers hold long-limbed frost creatures that hunt in packs. Mau kills what attacks him and eats what he can: one kind of tower lizard is edible roasted with a small fire spell from Frieren's lessons; ice melted for water. A huge shaggy herbivore follows him for three days; he thinks it is hunting him until it lies down on him to get warm. He names it the Pillow. Ciel measures the clock: roughly a day inside for every forty seconds outside. No new units; he does not Arise anything.

*When: The first week inside. Ciel keeps the count, because Mau has asked her to, and because the last time nobody kept it he lost track somewhere past two hundred.*

The things in the towers come for him on the first night.

He'd have called it night, anyway. Nothing changes in the ceiling, but the air gets colder in a way he can feel in his teeth, and the plain goes quiet in a different way than before, and Ciel tells him that by the measure she's using, about eighteen hours have passed since he came through. He's reached the first of the broken towers by then. It's huge, bigger than it looked from the plain, a stump of pale stone as wide as the house and four times as tall, its top sheared away at an angle like something bit it. There are openings in the side. Something has been living in them.

He finds out what when they come out.

They're long. That's the first impression: long arms, long legs, long bodies, thin as winter branches and moving with a strange loose jointed grace that the eyes can't quite follow. Their skin, or whatever covers them, is a texture like frost on a window, and where it catches the flat light it has a faint dry shine. They have no faces he can see. They come out of the tower in a rush of limbs and a sound like ice cracking on a pond, nine or ten of them, and they don't stop to look at him.

He kills the first three before he has decided to.

That's Jin-Woo's teaching, sitting in his hands. The daggers are out and moving before the thought is finished, low and fast, the grip Jin-Woo spent weeks correcting on his bad fingers. The first one comes in with its arms wide and he goes under them and opens it from hip to shoulder. The second is behind the first and he uses the first one's falling body as a step. The third tries to wrap him in all four limbs at once, and he lets Infinity take the touch, and while it's hanging there confused in the space it can't close, he puts a dagger through the place a heart would be on anything that had one.

The others hesitate.

Then they come anyway, all at once, from every side, and he stops thinking about Jin-Woo and starts thinking about the eyes.

* * *

It takes about a minute.

When it's done, he's standing in the middle of a ring of long pale bodies, breathing hard, and the cold is biting at the sweat on his face, and he's grinning. He can feel it. He tries to stop and can't.

“You're enjoying this,” Ciel says. ᶜ

“A little.” ᴹ

“Your heart rate says more than a little.” ᶜ

“Then a lot,” he admits. ᴹ

He hasn't moved like that in — he tries to think how long. Since before the war, anyway. Since before the recovery clears, where Bellion counted his steps. His body has been waiting for permission for a long time and now it's got it, and it's like opening a window in a room that's been shut all winter.

He looks down at the bodies.

“Can I eat those?” he asks. ᴹ

“No,” Noise says immediately. ᴺ

“You didn't even check.” ᴹ

“I checked. No.” ᴺ

* * *

Food turns out to be the problem he expected.

He eats half the bread on the first day, telling himself it's to keep his strength up for running, and then has to sit down on a fallen stone in the lee of the tower and have a serious conversation with himself about arithmetic. One day of food. Unknown number of days inside. Ciel is still working on the clock, but whatever it is, it's more than one.

He's done this before. He knows how to do this. It still makes his stomach go tight, sitting there in the cold, looking at what's left.

“The lizards,” Ciel says on the second day. ᶜ

He's been seeing them: small quick things that live in the cracks of the towers, about the length of his forearm, flat and armoured and fast. They eat something that grows on the inner walls, a kind of crust. Noise has been watching them for a day and a half.

“Edible?” Mau asks. ᴹ

“Edible,” Noise confirms. “Cooked. Don't eat the back plates. Or the tail. Or the part behind the head.” ᴺ

“What does that leave?” ᴹ

“Not much,” Noise says. “But some.” ᴺ

Catching them is easy. Cooking them is harder, because there's nothing to burn. There are no trees on the plain, and the towers are stone, and whatever grows on the walls is too thin to hold a flame. In the end he does what Frieren taught him to do in the first year, sitting in a field outside a town whose name he can't remember now, with her correcting his hand every time he got the shape wrong: a small fire spell, the kind that sits in the air and burns without fuel for as long as you feed it mana. It's a children's spell. Frieren made him practise it until he could hold it for an hour without thinking. He never understood why.

He holds it now, over the lizard on the end of a dagger, and thinks about her.

The lizard tastes like nothing. Like cold chicken that's been in a drawer. He eats all of it that Noise says he can, and then he eats the edge of the part Noise says he shouldn't, and Noise says nothing, which is how he knows it's fine.

Water is ice. There's plenty of that.

* * *

On the third day, something starts following him.

He notices it in the afternoon, or what he's calling afternoon. Something large, behind him on the plain, just at the edge of the haze. When he stops, it stops. When he turns to look, it's a shape in the grey, big and low and round, too far away for the eyes to resolve. When he starts walking again, it starts walking again.

“Hunting,” he says. ᴹ

“Possibly,” Ciel says. ᶜ

“It's big.” ᴹ

“It's very big.” ᶜ

He keeps an eye on it. It keeps its distance. When he reaches the next tower and clears it — six of the long things this time, faster than before, now that he knows how they move — the big shape waits out on the plain until he's done, and then comes a little closer.

That night he doesn't sleep. He sits with his back against the tower wall and the daggers across his knees and watches the shape in the dark, and the shape lies down about a hundred paces off and doesn't move, and at some point toward morning he realises he can hear it breathing. Long, slow, heavy breaths, like wind through a doorway.

“It's asleep,” Noise says. ᴺ

“Or pretending.” ᴹ

“It's asleep, Mau.” ᴺ

He doesn't believe it.

* * *

On the fourth day it's closer.

He can see it now. It's enormous: taller than he is at the shoulder, and three times as long, and covered from end to end in a thick shaggy coat of something that looks, to him, like a heap of old grey wool. Its legs are short and thick. Its head is broad and low and has two small dark eyes buried in the fur, and in front of them, a long flexible nose like a thick rope, which it uses to pull the crust off the tower walls and stuff it into its mouth.

It looks at him while it eats. It doesn't look like it's hunting. It looks like it's waiting.

“Herbivore,” Noise says. ᴺ

“It's been following me for two days.” ᴹ

“Herbivore,” Noise says again, patiently. ᴺ

“Then why is it following me?” ᴹ

Nobody knows.

* * *

On the fifth day he finds out.

He's in the lee of the fourth tower, at the end of a long day of running and fighting, so tired his hands are shaking. He has the little fire spell going because he can't feel his fingers otherwise, and he's sitting as close to it as he can get without burning his knees. The big shaggy thing is where it always is, out on the plain, watching.

Then it gets up and walks toward him.

He's on his feet before it's covered half the distance, daggers out, the eyes wide open and reading everything. It keeps coming, slowly, steadily, its long nose swaying, until it's close enough that he could touch it. Then it stops.

It looks at the fire.

It looks at him.

Then it lies down — right there, right next to the fire, so close that it's practically on top of him — and lets out a long, deep, contented groan like a door that needs oiling, and closes its eyes.

Mau stands there with the daggers in his hands.

“It wanted the fire,” he says. ᴹ

“It wanted the fire,” Ciel agrees. ᶜ

“For three days.” ᴹ

“For three days.” ᶜ

“It's been following me for three *days* because I'm the only warm thing on this whole plain.” ᴹ

The big shaggy thing opens one small eye and looks at him, and then closes it again.

He starts to laugh. He can't help it. It comes up from somewhere deep and doesn't stop, and he has to sit down, and he sits down against the thing's side because that's where there's room, and it's *warm*. It's warm the way a stove is warm. It's the warmest thing he's touched in five days.

He laughs until he can't breathe. The thing doesn't seem to mind.

* * *

He sleeps against it that night, and the night after.

He names it the Pillow. Noise objects to the name on the grounds that it's undignified. Mau points out that Suho named a shadow soldier Sir Shadow and nobody objected, and Noise says that several people objected, actually, and Mau says that's not the point.

The Pillow doesn't care what it's called. It follows him to the next tower and waits outside while he clears it. When he lights the fire, it lies down beside him. When he wakes up in the morning it's still there, its huge slow breathing going in and out under his back like the sea.

“Are you going to keep it?” Ciel asks on the sixth day. ᶜ

He thinks about that. He could. He knows how. A word, and it would stand up again in his shadow, shaggy and huge and warm, and he could take it home, and Ori would lose her mind, and Milim would try to ride it.

“No,” he says. ᴹ

“No?” ᶜ

“It's not mine. It lives here. It eats the walls.” ᴹ

He pats the Pillow's flank. It groans contentedly.

“Besides,” he says, “what would I tell Beru?” ᴹ

* * *

That night, Ciel finishes the clock.

“Six days in here,” she says. “By the outside, about four minutes.” ᶜ

He does the sum in his head and doesn't like it.

“About forty seconds a day,” he says. ᴹ

“About that.” ᶜ

“So if I take a month in here—” ᴹ

“About twenty minutes out there.” ᶜ

He lies against the Pillow in the dark with the little fire burning in front of him and thinks about twenty minutes. Fern looking at the clock. Yuka glancing at the door. Twenty minutes is nothing. Twenty minutes is a cup of tea.

A month in here, on a day's food, is not nothing.

He puts his hand in his pocket where the chocolate isn't — it's in the inventory, safe, untouched — and thinks *for later*, and closes his eyes.

“Tomorrow,” he says, “we go faster.” ᴹ
