# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 78
## Chapter 18 — Ori Tries

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 78
**Reading order:** 18
**Source episode:** S13E30
**Continuity state:** B+312. Second day of the gap. Morning: Yuka comes up behind Mau at the window and puts her arms round him; he goes stiff, takes a moment, then covers her hands with his. Ori tries twice: first with the shell (he says it's very good and doesn't know why she gave it to him), then with Lantern and the stone shaped like his thumb. He's kind and doesn't know her. Beru keeps calling him my king; Mau doesn't know what he's king of. Gojo sits with him in the evening. Some small things come back (the stairs, Shuna's bread), not people.

*When: The second morning. Mau is allowed out of bed as far as the window. Shoko says that's the limit, and means it.*

He's standing at the window when she comes in.

He's been there a while. The yard is down below: posts, a wall, an orchard beyond it, a porch. He knows all of it. He knows which of the posts leans. He knows that the third tree from the left in the orchard has a branch that's good for sitting on. He doesn't know how he knows. He's stopped asking himself. It's easier to just let the knowing be there, like furniture.

He hears the door open behind him. He hears her step into the room. He knows it's her without turning around; he can hear it in the way she walks, light and quick and a little uneven, and he doesn't know how he knows that either.

He doesn't turn around.

Then her arms come round his waist from behind.

* * *

He goes stiff.

He doesn't mean to. It just happens. Something in him that doesn't know her, that only knows a stranger has just put her arms around him, goes rigid all at once, shoulders up, breath held. His hands come up off the windowsill as if to push something away.

She feels it. He feels her feel it. Her arms loosen; she starts to pull back.

And then something else in him, something underneath the stiffness, says *no*.

He doesn't know what it is. It isn't a memory. It's lower than that, older. It's the same thing that made his hand find hers yesterday. It knows these arms. It knows the exact weight of her chin settling on his shoulder from behind, the warmth of her chest against his back. It knows that this is a thing she does. It knows that this is a thing she does *to him*.

His hands come down.

They come down over hers, where they're locked in front of him. He covers them. He holds them there. He doesn't know why. He just does.

She stops pulling back.

They stand like that at the window for a long time. He can feel her breathing against his back, a little too fast and then slower. He can feel her chin on his shoulder. Neither of them says anything. Down below, in the yard, someone is carrying a basket across to the kitchen.

“You used to do this,” he says at last. It isn't a question. ᴹ

“I still do this,” she says. ʸ

He thinks about that.

“Okay,” he says. ᴹ

She doesn't let go for a long time. He doesn't ask her to.

* * *

Ori comes in the middle of the morning.

He knows her. Of course he knows her. Small and serious, with a face that's working very hard at being brave. He knows her the way he knows the posts in the yard: completely, without anywhere to put it.

She's carrying something carefully in both hands.

“Hi,” she says. ᴼ

“Hi.” ᴹ

“I'm Ori.” ᴼ

“Ori,” he repeats. ᴹ

That one fits. A little. Not all the way. But there's something in it, the shape of it in his mouth, that feels like a word he's said a great many times.

She comes over to the bed, where he's sitting now because Shoko came in and caught him at the window, and holds out what's in her hands.

It's a shell.

A spiral, about the size of his palm. Smooth. He can see that the inside is a different shade than the outside, lighter, but he can't tell what it is. He takes it carefully.

“You found it,” Ori says. “On the island. With Yuka. You brought it back for me. It's for my windowsill.” ᴼ

He turns it over in his hands. He waits for it to mean something. It's a beautiful shell. He can see that. He can see the care in how she's holding herself, waiting for him to say the right thing.

“It's very good,” he says. ᴹ

Ori's face does something.

“It's a very good shell,” he says again, because he can tell that was close to right but not right. ᴹ

“You wrote that,” Ori says. Her voice is very small. “In the letter. *The shell is very good.* I asked if it was good and you said very good.” ᴼ

He doesn't remember a letter.

“I'm sorry,” he says. ᴹ

“That's okay,” Ori says. ᴼ

She takes the shell back, very carefully, and holds it against her chest, and goes away. He hears her on the stairs. The third one creaks. He knows that it will before it does.

* * *

She comes back in the afternoon with her shadow.

Something rises out of it when she's standing by the bed. Not much of it. A long, curved shape, scaled, the head of something big, lifting out of the darkness under her feet just far enough to look at him with an eye like a lamp.

“This is Lantern,” Ori says. “He's yours. Sort of. He lives in my shadow now. He chose. But you made him. Sort of. You can talk to him.” ᴼ

He looks at the dragon's head. The dragon's head looks at him.

He feels something, then. A thread. Thin and faint, running from somewhere inside him to the thing in Ori's shadow. He knows it. He knows a great many threads like it; he can feel them now that he's paying attention, a vast web of them, thousands, running out from him in every direction like roots. He doesn't know what any of them are.

“Hi, Lantern,” he says. ᴹ

The dragon blinks, slowly, and sinks back into Ori's shadow.

Then she gives him the stone.

It's a small grey stone, smooth, flattened, about the size of the end of his thumb. Ori puts it in his palm and looks at him with enormous intensity.

“It's the same shape as your thumb,” she says. “I found it in the creek. I showed you. You said—” ᴼ

She stops.

He looks at the stone. He looks at his thumb. He doesn't see the resemblance. He has a strange, distant sense that he's thought exactly this before, in exactly these words, and decided not to say it.

“It is the same shape,” he says. ᴹ

Ori looks at him for a long time.

“You never said that before,” she says. “You always just looked at it.” ᴼ

He doesn't know what to say.

She takes the stone back. She doesn't cry. He watches her not cry, very hard, with her jaw set and her eyes very bright. Then she says, “I'll come back tomorrow,” ᴼ in the voice of someone making a promise to herself, and goes.

* * *

The big ant is outside his door.

He noticed it yesterday. It's hard not to notice. It's taller than a man, armoured, many-eyed, and it's wearing a small cloth hat with holes cut for its antennae, and it has been standing outside his door, as far as he can tell, since he woke up.

He knows it. He knows it the way he knows the dragon. There's a thread to it, thicker than the others, much thicker. He doesn't know what that means.

“My king,” it says, whenever he passes the door. ᴮᵉ

“Why do you call me that?” he asks finally, on the second afternoon. ᴹ

The ant considers this.

“Because you are,” it says. ᴮᵉ

“King of what?” ᴹ

Another pause.

“Of me,” the ant says. “And of many others. And of this house, a little, though you would deny it. And the queen is your queen.” ᴮᵉ

“The queen.” ᴹ

The ant's many eyes look past him, toward the stairs, where Yuka has just come up with a tray.

“My queen,” it says to her, very gently. ᴮᵉ

He looks at Yuka. Yuka looks at the floor.

He doesn't ask any more questions.

* * *

Some things come back that day. Small things.

The stairs: he knows, suddenly, while Yuka is helping him down them in the evening, that the third one creaks and that it's Ori who always steps on it and that someone keeps offering to fix it. He doesn't know who. He just knows that it's not supposed to be fixed.

The bread: Rem brings it up — he knows her name now; she told him, and it fit — and he tastes it and knows that it's Shuna's, that Shuna gets up before dawn to make it, that you can hear her on the stairs if you're awake. He says so. Rem's eyes fill up, and she says, “Yes,” ᴿᵉ and goes away very quickly.

A song: somebody is playing something downstairs on a guitar, and he knows the next four notes before they come.

Not people. Not her.

* * *

Gojo sits with him in the evening.

He just comes in and sits down in Shoko's chair by the window and stretches his long legs out, and doesn't say anything for a while. The blindfold is on. Mau can feel him looking underneath it anyway.

“I know you,” Mau says. ᴹ

“Yeah.” ᴳ

“I don't know how.” ᴹ

“That's okay.” ᴳ

“Everyone keeps saying that.” ᴹ

Gojo laughs, a little. “Because it is. You'll get there.” ᴳ

“How do you know?” ᴹ

Gojo turns his head toward him. For a moment he doesn't say anything.

“Because I can see you,” he says. “In there. All of it. It's all still there. You're just in a room with the lights off, feeling your way around the furniture.” ᴳ

Mau thinks about that.

“She's in there?” he asks. “Yuka?” ᴹ

Gojo is quiet for a long moment.

“She's the biggest thing in there,” he says. “By a long way. You just can't find the light switch.” ᴳ

He gets up. He stops at the door.

“She's going to come up in a minute,” Gojo says, “and sit on the edge of the bed, and not ask you for anything. Hold her hand when she does. You don't have to know why.” ᴳ

He goes.

A minute later, Yuka comes up. She sits on the edge of the bed. She doesn't ask him for anything.

He holds her hand.
