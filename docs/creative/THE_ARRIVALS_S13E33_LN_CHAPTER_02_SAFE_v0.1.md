# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 78
## Chapter 24 — Safe

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 78
**Reading order:** 24
**Source episode:** S13E33
**Continuity state:** B+318, morning, Mai's workshop. Yuka's point of view. Yuka's conversation alone with Mai that the handoff asks for: she feels safe with Mau; he takes even her whims seriously; it makes her happy that he feels safe resting with her too. She wants him near almost all day, and she also misses Mai. They agree on a standing morning in the workshop. Mai cuts the island cloth.

*When: The morning after chocolate day. Mau is asleep again; Shoko says he will be, on and off, for a while. Yuka goes to the workshop without him for the first time since they came home.*

The workshop is cold in the mornings.

Yuka had forgotten that. Mai never lights the stove until her hands are already moving, because she says warm hands get lazy. So when Yuka comes in, a little after breakfast, with two cups of Shuna's hot chocolate, Mai is sitting at the bench in her coat with the island cloth spread out in front of her and a piece of chalk in her fingers, breathing little clouds.

She looks up.

“You came,” Mai says. ᴹᵃ

“I said I would.” ʸ

“You said you would four times. You kept getting distracted.” ᴹᵃ

Yuka puts one of the cups in front of her. Mai wraps both hands around it and closes her eyes.

“Distracted,” Mai says again, without opening them. “By a tall boring man with grey hair.” ᴹᵃ

“His hair's not grey. It's brown and white.” ʸ

“Grey is what you get when you mix those.” ᴹᵃ

“That's *paint*.” ʸ

“Everything's paint if you look at it long enough.” ᴹᵃ

Yuka laughs, and sits down on the stool beside her, and feels something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched.

* * *

They don't talk at first.

That's the thing about Mai. You don't have to. Mai picks up the chalk and starts marking the cloth, little lines and dots that mean something only to her, and Yuka sits beside her with her own cup and watches, and the stove stays cold, and the light comes in grey and soft through the high window.

It's so quiet. Yuka hadn't realised how loud everything had been.

“You want to say something,” Mai says, after a while. She doesn't look up from the chalk. ᴹᵃ

“Do I?” ʸ

“You've been holding your cup with both hands and not drinking from it for five minutes. That's what you do.” ᴹᵃ

Yuka looks down. It's true. She drinks some, and it's gone lukewarm.

“I don't know how to say it,” she says. ʸ

“Then say it badly.” ᴹᵃ

* * *

She tries.

“I feel safe,” Yuka says. ʸ

Mai's chalk keeps moving.

“With him. I feel—” Yuka stops. Tries again. “You know how I was. Before. When I came here. I didn't have a tomorrow. I didn't think about tomorrow. There wasn't any point. I just grabbed everything I could right now, all the time, as hard as I could, because right now was all there was.” ʸ

“I know,” Mai says. ᴹᵃ

“And I still do that. A bit. I still grab. I still want everything right now.” ʸ

“I know.” ᴹᵃ

“But with him it's not—” Yuka puts the cup down. “It's not grabbing any more. It's like—when I'm with him, I'm not scared there won't be a tomorrow. I'm not scared at all. Even when he's the one who's hurt. Even when he's lying there with blood coming out of his eyes. I'm scared *for* him. But I'm not scared like *that*. Like I used to be. Like the floor's going to go.” ʸ

Mai stops chalking.

“He's the floor,” Mai says. ᴹᵃ

Yuka looks at her.

“Yeah,” she says. “Yeah. He's the floor.” ʸ

* * *

“And the stupid thing,” Yuka says, “the really stupid thing, is that he takes it all seriously.” ʸ

“Takes what seriously?” ᴹᵃ

“*Everything*. Everything I want. Even the dumb things. Even the things I only want because I want them right now and I'll forget about them in an hour.” ʸ

She's smiling. She can't help it.

“On the island I wanted a hat,” she says. “A giant stupid hat from a stall. I didn't need it. I just saw it and wanted it. And he got it for me with the pass, and I wore it all day on principle, and he never once said it was silly. And then Ori drew it in her note, and he looked at the drawing for about five minutes like it was the most important thing anyone had ever made.” ʸ

Mai is smiling too now.

“Yesterday,” Yuka says, “I said *chocolate day* and he bought one of every bar in the Shop. Then he bought two of the fish because I looked at the fish. He didn't ask why. He just did it. He does that with *everything*. If I say I want to sit somewhere, he makes room. If I say I'm cold, he's already got a blanket. If I say I want something stupid, he doesn't ask if it's stupid, he just — he just goes and gets it, and then he watches me have it like that's the thing *he* wanted.” ʸ

She has to stop. Her eyes have gone hot.

“Nobody ever did that,” she says. “Before. Nobody took me seriously. I was the sick girl. Everybody was very nice to the sick girl. Nobody took her *seriously*.” ʸ

Mai puts down the chalk. She doesn't say anything. She just reaches over and puts her cold hand on top of Yuka's on the bench and leaves it there.

* * *

“And he feels safe too,” Yuka says, when she can. ʸ

“With you?” ᴹᵃ

“With me.” ʸ

She tells Mai about the day of the red gate. How he came home thinner. How she climbed into his lap and put his head against her and he just *let go*. Fell, all at once, all of him, like she was the floor.

“He never did that before,” Yuka says. “Not like that. He always held something back. Even asleep. Even after the dungeon, when he could barely move. Part of him was always awake, watching the door. And that day he just—stopped watching.” ʸ

She looks at her hands.

“I can't explain how happy that makes me,” she says. “That he can rest. That he can rest *with me*. That I'm somewhere he doesn't have to watch the door.” ʸ

Mai is quiet for a moment.

“You're the floor too,” she says. ᴹᵃ

Yuka looks up.

“That's what that means,” Mai says. “You're his floor. He's yours. That's what you've got.” ᴹᵃ

Yuka doesn't say anything. She can't.

* * *

“I want him near me all the time,” she admits, after a long while. ʸ

Mai snorts. “I know. The whole house knows. Gojo's started a tally.” ᴹᵃ

“He *what*?” ʸ

“Every time you go to find him within five minutes of leaving him. He's up to about forty.” ᴹᵃ

Yuka puts her face in her hands.

“I can't help it,” she says through her fingers. “I go to the kitchen and I come back and I've missed him. I go to the bathroom and I've missed him. It's *embarrassing*.” ʸ

“It's very embarrassing,” Mai agrees. ᴹᵃ

“And then—” ʸ

Yuka takes her hands away from her face. She looks at Mai. At Mai's cold red-knuckled hands, at the chalk on her fingers, at the island cloth spread out on the bench, at the stove she hasn't lit.

“And then I miss *this*,” she says. ʸ

Mai goes still.

“I miss you,” Yuka says. “I miss the workshop. I miss sitting here in the cold while you mark things and not talking. I miss you telling me my hair looks like a bird's nest. I miss telling you what colour things are. Since the island I've been with him every minute and I love it and I don't want to stop, and I still miss *you*, and I don't know how to have both.” ʸ

* * *

Mai doesn't say anything for a while.

Then she picks up the chalk again, and turns back to the cloth, and makes a mark.

“Mornings,” she says. ᴹᵃ

“What?” ʸ

“Mornings. Here. Before I light the stove. He sleeps late now anyway. Shoko said.” ᴹᵃ

Another mark.

“You come down after breakfast,” Mai says. “Every day you want to. You bring Shuna's hot chocolate. You sit there and tell me what colour things are, and I tell you your hair looks like a bird's nest, and we don't talk about him unless you want to. And then you go back up and climb on him and Gojo adds one to his tally.” ᴹᵃ

Yuka stares at her.

“And on the days you don't come,” Mai says, “I'll know he needed you more, and I won't mind.” ᴹᵃ

Another mark. Her voice is perfectly dry.

“Much,” she adds. ᴹᵃ

Yuka laughs. It comes out wet and surprised and much too loud for the quiet workshop. She leans over and puts her arms round Mai's shoulders from the side, coat and chalk and all, and holds on.

“You're squashing me,” Mai says. ᴹᵃ

“I know.” ʸ

“I'm going to make a wrong mark.” ᴹᵃ

“Make it.” ʸ

Mai sighs, very deeply, and doesn't push her away.

* * *

Later — after the stove is finally lit, and the workshop is warm, and Mai has cut the first length of the island cloth with Senku's good scissors, which she has borrowed without asking and will return before he notices — Yuka holds the piece up to the window.

“What colour?” Mai asks. ᴹᵃ

“The lagoon at noon,” Yuka says. “That's what the woman at the stall called it. It's blue, but there's green in it. Like you're looking down at sand through clear water. And when you move it, it goes lighter. Like the sun's moving on it.” ʸ

Mai looks at the cloth for a long time, as if she can see every word.

“I'll make him something out of the edge,” she says. “The bit I cut off. Something small. Something he can keep in his pocket.” ᴹᵃ

“He can't see it,” Yuka says. ʸ

“No,” Mai agrees. “But you can tell him what it is. And he'll know.” ᴹᵃ

Yuka folds the cloth over her arm. She looks at the door, toward the stairs, toward the room where he's sleeping.

“Go on,” Mai says, without looking up. “It's been twenty minutes. Gojo's waiting.” ᴹᵃ

Yuka goes.

She does not, on the stairs, run. She walks very calmly, as befits a grown woman and the reigning champion of the household racing tournament.

She runs the last bit.
