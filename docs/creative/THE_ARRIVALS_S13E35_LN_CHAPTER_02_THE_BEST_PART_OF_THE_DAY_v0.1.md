# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 78
## Chapter 29 — The Best Part of the Day

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 78
**Reading order:** 29
**Source episode:** S13E35
**Continuity state:** B+323. The hour after supper, when the house goes quiet and Mau and Yuka find each other, has become the part of the day both of them wait for; neither has said so. Through the day each keeps checking the light. Tonight it's a walk in the orchard. Mau tells her that Shoko said to ask Yuka about taking Shoko's surname. Yuka laughs, then takes it seriously; they don't decide. She says she wants something both of them chose. Sitting on the third tree's branch, she leans on him until the lamps come on.

*When: All day, and then the hour after supper. Mau notices around noon that he has looked at the window three times to see how far the sun has gone.*

He notices it at noon.

He's on the porch with Senku, who wants to know whether the shard from the red gate is colder at the tip or at the base, and Mau is trying to answer, and he realises he's just looked at the sky for the third time since breakfast. Not at anything. At where the sun is. How far it's got.

He makes himself look at the shard instead.

“The tip,” he says. ᴹ

“You're sure?” ˢᵉ

“The tip.” ᴹ

Senku writes it down. Mau looks at the sky.

* * *

It started after the gate with Yuta. Or before it. He can't say exactly when.

There's an hour, after supper, when the house goes quiet. Not silent; the house is never silent. But the dishes are done, and Shuna has gone to sit with her feet up, and the children are being sent to bed and arguing about it, and the people who are going to play games are playing games, and the people who are going to read are reading. And in that hour, without anyone deciding it, he finds her, or she finds him.

Some nights it's the porch. Some nights it's their room. Once it was the roof, because she wanted to see if the stars were in the same places as on the island, and they weren't, and she was outraged about it for ten minutes.

It's never planned. He doesn't say *meet me after supper*. She doesn't say it either. It just happens: he looks up from whatever he's doing and she's in the doorway, or he goes looking for her and finds she's already on her way to look for him, and they meet in the hall and both stop and laugh.

He's started waiting for it.

That's what the looking at the sky is. He knows that now, on the porch with Senku. He's been waiting for it since breakfast.

* * *

He catches her doing it in the afternoon.

She's in the kitchen with Rem, helping with something that involves a great deal of flour, and he goes in for a glass of water, and she doesn't see him at first. She's looking out of the window. At the light on the orchard. At how long the shadows have got.

Then she sees him in the doorway, and goes faintly pink, and looks very hard at the flour.

“What,” she says. ʸ

“Nothing.” ᴹ

“You're smiling.” ʸ

“You were looking at the shadows.” ᴹ

“I was looking at the *orchard*.” ʸ

“You were checking how long until supper.” ᴹ

She throws a handful of flour at him. Rem pretends very hard not to have noticed.

He doesn't say *so was I*. He doesn't have to. She can see it on his face.

* * *

Supper is long. It's always long when you're waiting for it to be over.

Gojo tells a story about a curse that turned out to be a very large cat. Milim wants to know if the cat was all right. Ori wants to know if she can have a very large cat. Umaru announces the date of the next tournament and that Authority remains banned. Mau eats and laughs at the right places and does not look at Yuka more than about once a minute.

She's across the table, between Maki and Mai. She isn't looking at him either. She's looking at him exactly as often as he's looking at her, which means that about once a minute their eyes meet over the bread, and both of them look away.

Gojo makes a mark in his notebook.

“That's not a tally thing,” Yuka says. ʸ

“Everything's a tally thing,” Gojo says. ᴳ

* * *

Then the dishes, and Shuna sitting down, and Ori being sent to bed and arguing about it, and the house going quiet.

He goes out to the porch.

She's already there.

She's leaning on the rail with her back to the door, and she doesn't turn round when he comes out. She just holds out her hand behind her, without looking, and he takes it.

“Orchard?” she says. ʸ

“Orchard.” ᴹ

* * *

They walk.

The orchard at this hour is cool and quiet and smells of the grass under the trees. The light is going. He can see it going: the way it lies along the ground and thins and lifts, the shadows of the trunks stretching out long and soft across the grass. Grey on grey. And her beside him, in the middle of all that grey, in colour, the last of the light caught in her hair.

They don't talk for a while. They don't need to. She swings their joined hands a little as they walk, the way Ori does.

“I have to tell you something,” he says. ᴹ

She looks up at him.

“It's not bad,” he says quickly. “It's—Shoko told me to ask you something. Before the clinic. Before everything. I forgot, and then I remembered, and then there was the gate, and the tournament, and the chocolate, and—” ᴹ

“Ask me what?” ʸ

“About the name.” ᴹ

* * *

He tells her.

Sitting with Shoko in the clinic. Talking about the island, and who to bring, and then about surnames, the ones they tried on the island and didn't like. And then him saying, without really meaning to, *I could take yours*. Shoko's.

“*Ieiri*,” Yuka says. ʸ

“Mau Ieiri.” ᴹ

She stops walking.

He waits for it. He doesn't know what he's waiting for. Then her face does something, and she puts her free hand over her mouth, and she starts to laugh.

“What?” ᴹ

“You *asked Shoko* if you could take her *name*?” ʸ

“I didn't ask. I just said it.” ᴹ

“And what did she say?” ʸ

“She said it was stupid.” ᴹ

Yuka laughs harder.

“And then she looked out the window for a long time,” Mau says, “and said *ask Yuka*.” ᴹ

Yuka stops laughing.

* * *

She doesn't say anything for a while. They start walking again, slower.

“She meant it,” Yuka says finally. ʸ

“I think so.” ᴹ

“She looked out the window?” ʸ

“For a long time.” ᴹ

Yuka nods slowly. She's quiet. He can see her thinking about it, properly, the way she thinks about things that matter: with her head a little on one side and her lower lip caught in her teeth.

“It's a good name,” she says. ʸ

“It's a good name,” he agrees. ᴹ

“She's put you back together more times than I can count.” ʸ

“More times than *I* can count.” ᴹ

“And she'd hate it,” Yuka says. “She'd pretend to hate it for the rest of her life. And she'd love it.” ʸ

He laughs.

“But,” Yuka says. ʸ

He waits.

“I want—” She stops. Starts again. “I think I want it to be something we both picked. Not something you said in the clinic without meaning to. Even if it's a good one. Even if we end up picking it anyway.” ʸ

“Is that stupid?” ʸ

“So it's on the list,” Yuka says. ʸ

“It's on the list.” ᴹ

“Near the top.” ʸ

“Near the top.” ᴹ

“And we keep looking.” ʸ

* * *

The third tree from the left has a branch that's good for sitting on.

He's known that for years. He doesn't remember who told him; maybe nobody did; maybe he just found it, one evening, a long time ago. It's low and thick and grows out sideways almost level before it turns up, and there's a place on it, near the trunk, worn smooth by sitting.

He lifts her onto it. She lets him. Then he sits beside her, and the branch takes both of them, and they sit there with their legs hanging down and their shoulders touching and look back through the trees at the house.

The lamps are coming on. One window, then another. The kitchen. The common room, where someone is playing a game and Umaru is shouting. Upstairs, Ori's window, where Ori is supposed to be asleep and isn't, because there's a small shape at the glass looking out at the orchard.

Yuka sees it too. She lifts her hand and waves.

The small shape at the window waves back, frantically, and then disappears very fast, as if someone has come in.

Yuka laughs and leans her head on his shoulder.

“This is my favourite part,” she says. ʸ

He doesn't ask of what. He knows.

“Mine too,” he says. ᴹ

“I look at the window all afternoon,” she admits. “Waiting for it. Like an idiot.” ʸ

“I looked at the sky three times before lunch.” ᴹ

She lifts her head and looks at him.

“*Three*?” ʸ

“Senku noticed.” ᴹ

“Senku *noticed*?” ʸ

“Senku notices everything. He wrote it down, I think.” ᴹ

She puts her face in his shoulder and laughs until the branch shakes.

* * *

They stay until the lamps are all lit and the orchard has gone dark around them, and the only light is the house, warm and soft through the trees.

Then she slides down off the branch and holds her arms up, and he comes down after her, and she catches him, sort of, the way she caught him on the couch on the first night home when he came back from being a slime. They stand there in the dark under the tree for a moment with her arms round his neck.

For the first few seconds he's still got his hand on the branch behind him. She feels him check his footing, then takes one step into the space between his shoes. His hand comes down to her waist.

“You managed to spend a whole evening with me without giving me homework,” she says. ʸ

“We have a list.” ᴹ

“We can lose it whenever we want.” ʸ

He smiles. “I'd like to keep it.” ᴹ

That is what gets her. He says it with a little crease between his eyes, as if she might actually throw it out and he'd have to go looking for it in the dark. She pulls him within reach and kisses him. She can feel the laugh leave him before he kisses her back. His hand moves from her waist to the middle of her back. She leans into it and stays long enough to forget that there are lights on in the house at all.

“Tomorrow,” Yuka says, against his collar. ʸ

“Tomorrow,” he agrees. ᴹ

“Same time.” ʸ

“Same time.” ᴹ

They walk back to the house holding hands, slowly, the long way round, and when they get to the porch, Gojo is sitting on the steps with his notebook open on his knee, and doesn't say anything, and makes a mark.
