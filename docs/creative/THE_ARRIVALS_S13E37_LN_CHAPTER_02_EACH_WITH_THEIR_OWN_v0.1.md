# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 78
## Chapter 33 — Each with Their Own

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1

Mau goes with Gojo, Yuta and Jin-Woo to the stream to fish; nobody catches anything; Gojo talks about Shoko and the island trip; Jin-Woo teaches Suho to skip stones. *When: Two days after the gate. Gojo announces at breakfast that the two of them are being separated "for the good of the house and the integrity of my tally".*

“You're being separated,” Gojo announces. ᴳ

The table goes quiet.

“For one day,” Gojo says. “For the good of the house. For the integrity of my tally, which has become statistically meaningless. And because Maki says Yuka hasn't spent a whole day with her friends since before the island, and I say Mau hasn't spent a whole day with *his* friends since before the island, and Shoko says if I don't stop counting things she'll take my notebook.” ᴳ

“I said I'd *burn* it,” Shoko says. ˢʰ

“She said she'd burn it,” Gojo agrees. ᴳ

Mau looks across the table at Yuka.

Yuka looks at Maki. Maki looks back with her arms folded, unmoved.

“One day,” Maki says. “Town. Mai needs thread. Umaru needs snacks. Rem needs a new pan because Milim used the old one as a helmet. You're coming.” ᴹᵃᵏ

“And you,” Gojo says to Mau, “are coming fishing.” ᴳ

“I don't fish.” ᴹ

“Nobody fishes. That's what fishing is.” ᴳ

* * *

They go to the stream.

Gojo, Yuta, Jin-Woo and Mau, with four rods that Senku made out of something that bends in ways rods probably shouldn't, and a basket of food Shuna packed, and Suho, who heard the word *fishing* and attached himself to his father's leg until Jin-Woo gave up and brought him.

The stream is wide and slow below the Sungs' house, where it bends round a big flat rock. They sit on the rock. They put their lines in the water. Nothing happens.

Nothing happens for a long time.

It's the strangest morning he's had in months. Nobody needs anything. Nobody's hurt. Nothing is coming. The water goes past, slow and grey and glinting, and the sun is warm on the rock, and Gojo is lying on his back with his rod propped between his feet and his blindfold pushed up on his forehead, and Yuta is very carefully watching his line as if it might do something, and Jin-Woo is showing Suho how to choose a flat stone.

“Like this,” Jin-Woo says. “Flat. Thin. Smooth.” ᴶʷ

Suho finds a stone that is none of those things.

“Not that one,” Jin-Woo says. ᴶʷ

“Why not?” ˢᵘ

“It's round.” ᴶʷ

“It's a *good* stone.” ˢᵘ

“It's a good stone,” Jin-Woo agrees gravely. “It's not a skipping stone.” ᴶʷ

Mau watches them. He feels something in his chest he doesn't have a name for.

* * *

“So,” Gojo says, eventually, to the sky. ᴳ

“So?” ᴹ

“The island.” ᴳ

Mau looks at him. Gojo is still lying on his back, eyes closed.

“Shoko told me,” Gojo says. “That she's coming. And Anko. And Hinata.” ᴳ

He pauses.

“And me,” he says. “*If I behave*.” ᴳ

“She said perfectly,” Mau says. ᴹ

“I know what she said. She said it to me in the exact same voice. *Perfectly*.” ᴳ

He's quiet for a moment.

“She's never asked me to go anywhere,” Gojo says. Very quietly. Not to Mau. To the sky. ᴳ

Mau doesn't say anything. Beside him, Yuta has stopped watching his line.

“I'm going to behave,” Gojo says. “I'm going to behave so *perfectly*. I'm going to be the most well-behaved man who ever sat in a little pool.” ᴳ

“You're going to say something about the little pool in the voice,” Yuta says mildly. ʸᵗ

“I *know*,” Gojo says, anguished. “I can feel it already. It's going to come out of me and I won't be able to stop it.” ᴳ

Mau laughs so hard he nearly drops his rod.

* * *

Nobody catches anything.

Suho skips a stone, once, three times across the water before it sinks, and is so overjoyed that he has to run up and down the rock twice and tell everyone individually. Jin-Woo watches him do it with the same expression Mau has seen on him only once before, when Hae-In laughed at something across the dinner table.

They eat Shuna's food. They lie on the rock in the sun. Yuta falls asleep. Gojo tells a long story about a curse that turned out to be a very large fish, which is clearly the same story as the cat one with the animal changed, and nobody points this out.

It's good. It's really good.

And he keeps looking at the sky.

* * *

“You're doing it,” Jin-Woo says, in the afternoon. ᴶʷ

They're sitting at the edge of the rock with their feet in the water. Suho is asleep against his father's side.

“Doing what?” ᴹ

“Checking the sun.” ᴶʷ

Mau stops.

“Hae-In does it too,” Jin-Woo says. “When I'm at a gate. She says she doesn't. Jin-Ah told me.” ᴶʷ

He looks down at Suho, asleep.

“It's a good thing,” he says. “Wanting to get home. It's a good thing to have somewhere you're checking the sun for.” ᴶʷ

Mau looks at the light on the water. It's getting long.

“Yeah,” he says. ᴹ

* * *

Yuka's day, he hears about later. In pieces. From her, and from Maki, and from Umaru, who has opinions.

They went into town. Mai bought thread, too much of it, in colours Yuka described to her one by one in the shop until the shopkeeper started listening too. Umaru bought snacks, too many of them, and argued about the price of one bag for a quarter of an hour and won. Rem found a pan, a good heavy one, and carried it home herself and wouldn't let anyone help. They had lunch at a place with tables outside. Maki made Yuka laugh so hard she got tea up her nose.

“She kept looking at the sky,” Maki tells him, at supper, without being asked. “All afternoon. Every ten minutes.” ᴹᵃᵏ

“So did he,” Yuta says. ʸᵗ

“*Every* ten minutes?” ᴹᵃᵏ

“About every five,” Jin-Woo says. ᴶʷ

* * *

At supper, he finds her eyes.

She's at the other end of the table. Between Maki and Umaru, where she's been all day. She's laughing at something Umaru said. And then she looks up, down the long table, past the bread and the dishes and the twenty people talking, and finds him.

They look at each other.

She's been gone one day. Not even a whole day: breakfast to supper. She was in town. He was at the stream. Both of them had a good day. He knows she had a good day; he can see it on her face, the leftover brightness of an afternoon with her friends.

And still.

She puts down her spoon. He puts down his.

Shuna is bringing out dessert. It's the tart Rem made with the fruit from the orchard, the one everyone likes.

Yuka stands up.

So does he.

Gojo looks from one of them to the other. He opens his notebook. Then he looks at Shoko, who is looking at the notebook, and closes it again.

“Go,” Shoko says. ˢʰ

They go.

They meet in the hall. Halfway. Both stop. Both laugh.

“I had a good day,” she says. ʸ

“So did I.” ᴹ

“I missed you anyway.” ʸ

“So did I.” ᴹ

* * *

The next day, it rains.

It starts before dawn, heavy and steady, the kind of rain that settles in for the day. It drums on the roof. It runs down the windows. The orchard is a blur of grey through the glass.

Nobody goes anywhere. There's nowhere to go.

He wakes up late. She's already awake, lying beside him with her chin on her hand, watching the rain on the window.

“Rain,” she says. ʸ

“Rain.” ᴹ

“Nothing to do.” ʸ

“Nothing.” ᴹ

She looks at him. He looks at her.

* * *

They do nothing.

It turns out to be the best day of the week.

They get up eventually. Late. They go down to the kitchen, where Shuna has made porridge because it's a porridge kind of day, and eat it at the end of the table with Ori, who wants to know if the rain will make the stream big enough for a boat. They go back up. They don't go back to bed; they sit in the window seat in the hall at the top of the stairs, the deep one with the cushions, where you can see the whole orchard and the path down to the Sungs' house and the hills beyond.

He sits in the corner of the seat. She sits between his knees, leaning back against his chest. He wraps the blanket from their bed round both of them.

They watch the rain.

People go up and down the stairs past them all day. Milim, chasing Ori. Ori, chasing Milim. Fern, with the register. Makima, once, who stops at the top of the stairs and looks at them in the window seat and then goes back down without a word. Gojo, who doesn't take out his notebook. Beru, with his hat, who takes up a post at the bottom of the stairs and stays there.

Nobody asks them to do anything.

* * *

They talk, a little.

About nothing. About the ship in the movie, which she has decided was a metaphor for love, and which he still doesn't remember. About the Pillow. About whether Suho is old enough for a knife, which is apparently a question every household in the valley is asking about every child. About the surname, which is on the list, near the top. About the island, and whether Gojo will behave, and Shoko's face when she said *ask Yuka*.

Mostly they don't talk. Mostly they just sit there. Her weight against his chest. His arms round her under the blanket. The rain on the glass.

“What are you thinking?” she asks, once, in the afternoon. ʸ

He thinks about it.

“Nothing,” he says. ᴹ

She turns her head to look up at him.

“Really nothing?” ʸ

“Really nothing.” ᴹ

He means it. That's the strange part. For the first time in he doesn't know how long, there's nothing in his head. No gate. No sea. No door he's holding shut. No count. Just the rain, and her, and the window, and the orchard going grey and greyer in the falling light.

She looks at him a moment longer. Then she smiles, and turns back to the window, and settles her head against his chest.

“Good,” she says. ʸ

* * *

The light goes.

It doesn't really set; it just fades, the grey getting deeper and softer, until the orchard is gone and there's only the rain on the dark glass and their own reflections in it, faint, two shapes in a window seat under a blanket. Hers in colour. His in grey.

Downstairs, Shuna is calling people to supper.

Neither of them moves.

“The best part of the day,” Yuka says quietly. ʸ

“It's been the best part all day,” he says. ᴹ

She laughs, very softly.

They stay in the window seat until Ori comes up the stairs, stepping on the third one, to tell them that supper is getting cold and that Shuna says if they don't come down she's sending Beru.

They go down.

Behind them, the rain keeps falling on the orchard, and on the path to the Sungs' house, and on the hills beyond, and on the long valley, and on whatever is out there past the valley, waiting for its turn.

It isn't their turn yet.
