# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 79
## Chapter 3 — The Arithmetic

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 79
**Reading order:** 3
**Source episode:** S13E41
**Continuity state:** B+333. Yuka, marking the household calendar for the island letter, counts back to before the island (her last period was just before the trip) and realises she's about two weeks late. She tells Mau at once, in the workshop corridor. "What do you mean, late?" She explains. She asks if he can see. He has been trying not to look since the moment she said it; he doesn't look. They decide to tell no one yet. Night: they lie awake making careful, quiet plans, and he tells her about the boy in the shallow water he imagined on the beach (V77 #13).

*When: Mid-morning. Yuka has the household calendar open on Fern's desk because she wants to count how many days until a date they could offer Elmesia. Mau is in Mai's workshop next door, holding something still for Mai.*

She counts the days for the letter.

That's all she's doing. Fern keeps the household calendar on the desk in the little room off the hall: a big book with a page for every month and a square for every day and Fern's small straight handwriting filling the squares. *Shuna day off. Pillow home. Red gate, M, 21/14.* Yuka has it open because she and Mau have decided, without quite saying it to each other, that they want to write to Elmesia about the island again, and she wants to be able to say *in two weeks* or *in a month* and mean a real day.

So she counts forward.

And then, because her finger is already on the page, she counts back.

* * *

It isn't a decision. It's a habit. She's done it every month since the hand, since they first decided to try. Count back to the last one. Count forward to the next. It's become something she does without thinking, the way she checks the stove is out before she leaves the kitchen.

She counts back to the island.

To before the island. To the night they packed, after Ori's birthday, when she'd already had it and didn't say so yet.

She counts forward.

Her finger stops on today.

She counts again.

She takes her finger off the page, and puts it back on the square from before the island, and counts again, very slowly, moving her lips, the way Ori counts when she's afraid of getting it wrong.

Fourteen days. Give or take. Fourteen days past when it should have come.

She sits there with her hand flat on Fern's calendar and her heart doing something she doesn't have a name for.

* * *

She doesn't wait.

That's the first thing she decides, and she decides it before she's stood up. She doesn't wait until tonight. She doesn't wait until she's sure. She doesn't wait until she's worked out how to say it or whether it's worth saying or what she'll do if it's nothing. She waited, last time; she held it on her own for days because she didn't want to get his hopes up, and it didn't make anything easier, and she promised herself after the island that she wouldn't do that again.

She gets up. She goes out of Fern's little room and down the hall.

The workshop door is open. She can see him from the hall: standing by Mai's bench with both hands flat on a piece of the frost-iron from the red gate, holding it perfectly still while Mai works on its edge with something that whines. His sleeves are rolled up. He's concentrating. He looks up when she stops in the doorway, the way he always looks up when she stops in a doorway.

His face changes.

She doesn't know what hers is doing. Something. Enough.

“Mai,” he says, without taking his eyes off Yuka, “can you hold this?” ᴹ

Mai looks at Yuka. Then at Mau. She puts down the thing that whines and puts her own hands on the frost-iron without a word.

He comes out into the hall.

* * *

“What's wrong?” ᴹ

“Nothing.” ʸ

He looks at her.

“Nothing's wrong,” she says again. “It's not wrong. It's—” ʸ

She doesn't know how to say it. She's been practising for the length of the hallway and she still doesn't know how to say it. So she just says it.

“I'm late.” ʸ

He blinks.

“Late for what?” ᴹ

She stares at him.

He stares back. She can actually see him trying to work it out: what she could be late for, at ten in the morning, on a day with nothing in it. Breakfast? A lesson with Maki? Something with Mai? His face goes through all of them, one after another, completely sincere, completely lost.

“What do you mean, late?” he asks. ᴹ

And she starts to laugh. She can't help it. She puts both hands over her face in the middle of the hallway and laughs, and it comes out half a sob, and he takes her by the shoulders looking frightened.

“Yuka—” ᴹ

“*Late*,” she says through her fingers. “I'm *late*, Mau. My—it should have come. Before the island it came, and it should have come again by now, and I counted, and it's two weeks. Two weeks late.” ʸ

She takes her hands away from her face.

She watches him understand.

* * *

She'll remember his face for the rest of her life.

It doesn't change all at once. It goes very still first, the way it goes still in a fight when something has happened that he needs to think about. Then his hands tighten on her shoulders. Then his eyes go to her face, and down, and back up to her face very quickly, as if he'd caught himself doing something.

And then he just looks at her. Wide-eyed. Lips parted. Like someone who's been handed something very small and very breakable and doesn't know yet where to put his hands.

“Two weeks,” he says. ᴹ

“About.” ʸ

“That's—is that—” ᴹ

“It could be nothing,” she says quickly. “It's happened before. Not two weeks, but—it could be nothing. Stress. The red gate, the clinic, all of it. Shoko said once that it can just—” ʸ

“It could be something,” he says. ᴹ

She stops.

“It could be something,” she agrees, very quietly. ʸ

* * *

She sees his eyes go down again. To her stomach. And then she sees him stop himself, hard, and drag them back up to her face. And she understands what he's doing.

“Can you see?” she asks. ʸ

He doesn't answer right away.

“With your eyes,” she says. “Can you see if it's—can you *tell*?” ʸ

“I don't know,” he says. Then, honestly, because he doesn't hide things from her any more: “Probably. If I looked. I think I could.” ᴹ

“Have you looked?” ʸ

“No.” ᴹ

“You were about to.” ʸ

“I've been trying not to,” he says, “since the second you said *late*.” ᴹ

She can see it. She can see what it's costing him. His eyes keep wanting to go down and he keeps not letting them. His jaw is tight with it. She's seen him hold himself back from things before: from a fight, from a door, from telling her something that would hurt. She's never seen him hold himself back from *looking at her*.

She thinks about it.

She thinks about knowing. Right now, here, in the hall, with Mai's whining tool starting up again behind the workshop door. One look and he'd know, and then she'd know, and it would be over, one way or the other.

She thinks about not knowing. About waiting. About finding out the way everybody finds out, with her body, in her own time.

“Don't,” she says. ʸ

He nods immediately.

“Don't look,” she says. “Please. I want to—I want to find out like everyone else. I don't want it to come out of your eyes. I want it to come out of *me*.” ʸ

“I won't look,” he says. ᴹ

“Even if you really want to.” ʸ

“Even if I really want to.” ᴹ

“You really want to.” ʸ

“I really, *really* want to,” he says, and his voice cracks a little, and she starts laughing again and puts her face against his chest so he can't see it, and he puts his arms round her in the middle of the hallway and holds on.

* * *

They decide not to tell anyone.

Not yet. Not until it's something. She doesn't want to watch the house hope and then watch the house be sad for her. He doesn't argue. He just nods, and holds her, and when Mai puts her head out of the workshop to ask if he's coming back for the frost-iron, he says yes in a voice so normal that Yuka has to bite the inside of her cheek.

He goes back in. She hears him say something to Mai about the edge. She hears Mai answer.

She stands in the hallway with both hands pressed flat over her stomach and doesn't let herself think anything at all.

* * *

That night they lie awake.

So close that when he tries to turn onto his back she follows him without waking all the way, her leg hooked over his and her head on his shoulder. His hand rests on her hip; her fingers stay over his as though that can keep their careful hopes from getting ahead of them. They look at the ceiling together. The wind is in the orchard. Through the wall, nothing, because Senku put something in it.

“If it's something,” she says, very quietly. ʸ

“If it's something,” he agrees. ᴹ

“Where would we put it? A bed. A crib. Whatever it's called.” ʸ

He thinks about it. “By the window. Your side.” ᴹ

“Why my side?” ʸ

“Because you'd want to see it first. In the morning.” ᴹ

She doesn't say anything for a moment. Her hand finds his on her hip and holds it.

“On the island,” he says. “On the beach. That day. I told you I'd thought about—” ᴹ

“The water,” she says. ʸ

“The shallow part. Where the waves come in small.” ᴹ

He's quiet for a while.

“I keep seeing it,” he says. “Someone sitting on my shoulders in the shallow water. Holding onto my hair. Screaming every time a wave comes. And you on the sand telling us to come in before we drown.” ᴹ

She laughs, very softly, into his shoulder.

“I wouldn't tell you to come in,” she says. “I'd be in the water too.” ʸ

“You hate when the waves hit your face.” ᴹ

“I'd get used to it,” she says. ʸ

They lie there a long time. Not saying much. Making careful, quiet plans that both of them know they shouldn't be making yet, and making them anyway, in low voices, in the dark, as if saying them too loud would break them.

“Don't look,” she says, much later, half asleep. ʸ

“I'm not looking,” he says. ᴹ

He isn't. She can tell without opening her own eyes: the small tightness at the hinge of his jaw against her forehead, the way he's holding himself very still, like someone keeping a door shut with his whole weight. The last thing she knows before she falls asleep is that he's still keeping them closed, because he promised.
