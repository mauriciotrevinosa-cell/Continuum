# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 79
## Chapter 9 — Go, But Hurry

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 79
**Reading order:** 9
**Source episode:** S13E44
**Continuity state:** B+338, morning. In bed, Yuka brings up the cancer herself: the treatments before she came, whether they left something. Mau offers to bring Shoko; Yuka refuses. She doesn't want him to go, but she wants to know if it's a chance, so she tells him to go and hurry. A short conversation in the clinic: Mau worried, Shoko telling him these things take time, the truth about what treatments like hers can and can't do, and how he could look for it with the eyes. Shoko never sees Yuka. Mau goes back and asks Yuka's permission himself before he looks. She gives it. He looks, and finds nothing of the kind. No intimate scene.

*When: B+338. The third morning. She's been awake for a while again. He knows by the way she's breathing, careful and even, like someone who's been thinking about how to say something.*

“The hospital,” she says. ʸ

He opens his eyes.

She's lying on her back looking at the ceiling. Her hand is on her stomach, flat, the way it's been every time she's been awake since the bathroom door.

“Before I came here,” she says. “The treatments. For the tumour. They gave me things. A lot of things. For a long time.” ʸ

He doesn't say anything. He waits.

“What if it was that,” she says. ʸ

She doesn't look at him. She says it to the ceiling, very quietly, as though if she says it quietly enough it won't be real.

“What if it broke something. In there. What if I came here with it already broken and I didn't know, and I've been—we've been trying, all this time, and it was never going to—” ʸ

She stops.

He turns on his side to face her. He doesn't touch her yet. He just looks at her profile against the grey light from the window, and the hand flat on her stomach, and the line of her jaw where she's holding something back.

“We could ask Shoko,” he says. ᴹ

* * *

“No.” ʸ

It comes out fast. She turns her head to look at him for the first time.

“I don't want her to see me,” she says. “Like this. I don't want anyone to see me like this. I don't want her coming in here with her bag and her face and asking me questions while I'm—” ʸ

She gestures at herself. At the bed, the two days, all of it.

“Okay,” he says. ᴹ

“I don't want—” ʸ

“Okay,” he says again. “She doesn't come in.” ᴹ

She breathes out.

Then she's quiet for a long time, looking at him. He can see her working something out.

“Could *you* go?” she says. ʸ

He blinks.

“To her. Could you go and ask her. Without me. Just—ask her if it's possible. If it could be that. If there's any way to *know*.” ʸ

He looks at her.

“I don't want you to go,” she says, and her voice cracks. “I don't want you to leave. Not even for a minute. But I want to know. I want to know if it's a chance or if it's—if it's just *me*. I want to know so much I can't breathe.” ʸ

He reaches over and puts his hand on top of hers, on her stomach.

“I'll go,” he says. ᴹ

“Hurry,” she says. ʸ

“I'll hurry.” ᴹ

“I mean it. *Hurry*.” ʸ

“I'll run,” he says. ᴹ

She almost laughs. Almost.

* * *

He does run.

Down the stairs, past Beru in the hall, past the kitchen where Shuna looks up from the bread with flour on her hands and opens her mouth and closes it again. Across the yard. Into the clinic, where Shoko is sitting at her desk with a cup of tea she hasn't touched and a book she isn't reading, and looks up the second the door opens.

She's been waiting. He can see it. She's been sitting there for two days waiting for someone to come through that door.

“Is she hurt?” Shoko asks. ˢʰ

“No.” ᴹ

“Rimuru said not physical—” ˢʰ

“She's not hurt,” he says. “She—we—” ᴹ

He doesn't know how to say it. He hasn't said it to anyone. It's been inside the room for two days, inside the two of them, and now he has to take it out and put it in the air, and he can't find the shape of it.

Shoko looks at his face.

“Sit down,” she says. ˢʰ

He sits. On the edge of the bed where she's patched him up a hundred times.

“We were trying,” he says. “For a baby. You know. Since the hand. And she was late. Two weeks. We thought—we thought it might be—and then two days ago it came.” ᴹ

Shoko doesn't say anything. She just puts her tea down, very slowly.

“She's not okay,” Mau says. “She's been in bed for two days. She thinks it's her fault. She thinks her body—she thinks the treatments. From before. The tumour. She thinks they broke something and she's been trying all this time with something broken and didn't know.” ᴹ

* * *

“Is she right?” he asks. ᴹ

Shoko is quiet for a long moment.

“I don't know,” she says. ˢʰ

He doesn't move.

“I won't lie to you,” she says. “Some treatments do that. Not all. Not always. It depends what they gave her and how much and for how long, and I don't know any of that because it happened in a world I've never been to, in a hospital I've never seen, when she was still a girl.” ˢʰ

“So it could be.” ᴹ

“It could be. And it could be nothing to do with it. Two months is nothing, Mau. Two months is *nothing*. I've known couples who tried for two years before anything happened, and they were perfectly well, both of them, and one day it just—happened. Bodies keep their own time. They don't explain themselves.” ˢʰ

He looks at his hands.

“It takes time,” she says, more gently. “This. All of it. The trying, and the waiting, and the days like these ones. It takes time, and there's no shortcut, and I know that's the worst thing I could possibly tell you.” ˢʰ

“It is,” he says. ᴹ

“I know.” ˢʰ

* * *

“Could I see it?” ᴹ

She looks at him.

“With the eyes,” he says. “If it was the treatments. If something's damaged. Would I be able to see it? Would I know what I was looking for?” ᴹ

Shoko thinks about it. She thinks about it properly, the way she thinks about everything medical, with her eyes narrowed and her fingers drumming once on the desk.

“Probably,” she says. “If it's there. Scarring. Damage to the tissue. The way something's built that shouldn't be built that way. Your eyes see more than any instrument I've ever had. If there's something wrong in there that you can see, you'd see it.” ˢʰ

She tells him what to look for. Briefly. Clearly. The shapes of things. What healthy looks like and what it doesn't. He listens to every word.

“And if I don't see anything?” he asks. ᴹ

“Then there's nothing to see,” Shoko says. “Which doesn't mean nothing's wrong. But it means it isn't *that*.” ˢʰ

He nods.

He stands up.

“Mau,” Shoko says. ˢʰ

He stops at the door.

“Ask her,” she says. “Before you look. Don't just look.” ˢʰ

“I was going to,” he says. ᴹ

She looks at him a moment longer. Then she nods, and picks her tea back up, and he goes.

* * *

He runs back.

She's sitting up in bed when he comes in. She's been watching the door. She looks at his face and tries to read it and can't, because there's nothing on it yet to read.

He sits down on the bed beside her.

“She doesn't know,” he says. “Not for sure. She says some treatments can. Not all of them. And it might be nothing to do with it. She says two months is nothing. She says some people try for years and they're fine and one day it just happens.” ᴹ

Yuka looks at him. Her eyes are very bright.

“But I could look,” he says. ᴹ

She goes still.

“With the eyes,” he says. “She told me what to look for. If something's damaged from the treatments, I'd see it. If there's nothing to see, then it isn't that.” ᴹ

He doesn't move. He doesn't let his eyes go anywhere but her face.

“You asked me not to look,” he says. “Before. When you were late. And I didn't. So I'm asking now. Can I?” ᴹ

* * *

She doesn't answer for a long time.

He watches her think about it. He watches her want to know and be afraid to know, both at once, so badly that her hands shake on the blanket. He doesn't say anything else. He doesn't push. He just waits, with his eyes on her face, the way he's kept them for three days.

“Yes,” she says. ʸ

Very small.

“Yes. Look. Please.” ʸ

* * *

He looks.

He lets the filter open. All the way. He lets the eyes do what they do, what they've been begging to do for three days. They go through the blanket and the shirt and the skin like light through water, and the world opens up underneath, layer on layer, the slow invisible weather of her: her blood moving, her breath, the small bright flicker of cursed energy running through her the way it runs through everyone who's ever trained.

He goes slowly. He goes carefully. He looks for every shape Shoko told him about. The scarring. The damage. The way things are built that shouldn't be built that way.

He looks for a long time.

He doesn't find anything.

* * *

He looks again, to be sure. He goes through all of it a second time, every place Shoko said, every shape. He makes himself be thorough. He makes himself be honest.

Nothing.

Everything is where it should be. Everything is built the way it should be built. There's no scarring, no damage, nothing broken, nothing wrong. Just her. Healthy. Whole. Exactly as she should be.

He lets the filter close.

He looks at her face.

* * *

“There's nothing,” he says. ᴹ

She stares at him.

“There's nothing,” he says again. “I looked everywhere she told me. Twice. There's nothing there, Yuka. It's not the treatments. Nothing's broken. You're—everything's how it's supposed to be.” ᴹ

She doesn't move.

“It's not that,” he says. “Whatever it is, it isn't that. It isn't you being broken. You're not broken.” ᴹ

For a long moment her face doesn't do anything at all.

Then she puts both hands over it and bends forward until her forehead is on her knees, and makes a sound he's never heard her make before. Not crying. Not quite. Something that has crying in it, and something else, something that's been held so tight for three days that it doesn't know how to come out.

He puts his arms round her.

She doesn't say anything. She just stays there, folded over her knees with her hands over her face and him wrapped round her, shaking, letting go of something he couldn't see even with the eyes.

He holds on until she's done.
