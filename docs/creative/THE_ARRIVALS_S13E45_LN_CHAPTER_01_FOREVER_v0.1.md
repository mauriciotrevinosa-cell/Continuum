# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 79
## Chapter 11 — Forever

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 79
**Reading order:** 11
**Source episode:** S13E45
**Continuity state:** B+339–341. The low days go on, more slowly. Yuka gets up and walks to the window and back, then the hall, then the porch at night. She won't let go of Mau; she asks him not to leave her. One night she tells him he should divorce her, that he could have a child with someone whose body works. He tells her he will never divorce her, that he loves her more than she has any idea of. "Forever?" "Forever." She just holds on. On the third evening Mau tells Frieren that Yuka seems a little better. Mau's own grief has a place too: alone for a minute on the third tree's branch. No intimate scene.

*When: B+339–341. The fourth, fifth and sixth days. The house has started to move normally again downstairs, carefully, the way you walk around someone sleeping.*

She gets up on the fourth day.

Not far. To the window. She stands there with the blanket round her shoulders and her forehead against the glass, looking at the orchard, and he stands behind her and doesn't touch her, because she didn't ask him to.

After a while she says, “Don't go anywhere.” ʸ

“I'm not going anywhere.” ᴹ

“I mean it.” ʸ

“I know.” ᴹ

She reaches back without turning round and finds his hand and pulls it forward round her waist, and leans back into him, and they stand at the window together until her legs get tired.

* * *

On the fifth day she goes as far as the hall.

She sits in the window seat at the top of the stairs, the deep one with the cushions where they watched the rain, wrapped in the blanket, with her knees up and her back against his chest. People go up and down the stairs. Nobody stops. Rimuru, on his way past, puts his hand on the top of her head for exactly one second and keeps walking. Shuna leaves a cup of hot chocolate on the sill without a word. Ori comes up the stairs with the Pillow, stops at the top, looks at Yuka for a long moment, and is taken gently away by Frieren before she can say anything.

“Ori,” Yuka says, very quietly, after they've gone. ʸ

“Frieren's been keeping her away,” Mau says. “She didn't want her to be scared. After the clinic. After—everything with me.” ᴹ

Yuka is quiet for a while.

“I don't want her to be scared either,” she says. ʸ

“I know.” ᴹ

“I'm not ready.” ʸ

“I know,” he says. “She can wait. Frieren's good at waiting.” ᴹ

* * *

She won't let go of him.

Not in a way that makes it hard. In a way that makes it obvious. If he gets up from the window seat to stretch his legs, her hand is on his sleeve before he's straightened. If he goes to the washroom, she's waiting at the door when he comes out, wrapped in the blanket, like Ori waiting outside a door at night. Every time he moves she moves with him, half a step behind, close enough to touch.

He doesn't mind. He doesn't say anything about it. He just makes sure she's never more than an arm's length away, because he can see in her face that she needs to know where he is, all the time, every second.

“Don't leave me,” she says, on the fifth night, half asleep, holding his shirt. ʸ

“I won't.” ᴹ

“Promise.” ʸ

“I promise.” ᴹ

“You'd tell me. If you were going to.” ʸ

“I'm not going to,” he says. ᴹ

She doesn't answer. Her fingers tighten in his shirt. He lies awake until they loosen.

* * *

On the sixth night, she says it.

They're lying in the dark. She's been quiet all evening; quieter than the other nights, in a way that's different, that has something underneath it. He's been waiting for whatever it is.

“You should divorce me,” she says. ʸ

He doesn't move.

“I mean it,” she says, to the ceiling. Her voice is very steady. Too steady. “You should. You could find someone else. Someone whose body *works*. You could have a child with someone who could actually—you'd be such a good father, Mau, you'd be the *best*, and I'm—what if it never happens, what if it's always going to be this, every month, the counting and the hoping and then the *bathroom*—you shouldn't have to—” ʸ

“No,” he says. ᴹ

She stops.

* * *

He turns on his side to face her. In the dark he can see her only as a shape, the outline of her profile against the faint grey of the window, but he doesn't need to see. He's never needed to see her.

“I will never divorce you,” he says. ᴹ

She doesn't answer.

“Never,” he says. “Not if it never happens. Not if it's always this. Not if we count every month for a hundred years and it never once comes out the way we want. Never.” ᴹ

“You don't know that—” ʸ

“I know it,” he says. “I know it the way I know you're the only thing in the world with colour. I don't decide it. It's just true.” ᴹ

He puts his hand on her face, very gently. It's wet.

“I didn't marry you for a child,” he says. “I married you because you sat outside the door of a dungeon every day for a year when I told you not to. Because you climb on me like a cat and tell me I'm badly arranged. Because you eat my share and you say it's ours. Because you're you.” ᴹ

He can feel her trembling under his hand.

“I love you,” he says, “more than you have any idea of. More than I know how to say. I don't have the words. I didn't have them before and I don't have them now. I just have *you*. And I'm not giving that back. Not ever. Not for anything.” ᴹ

* * *

She doesn't say anything for a long time.

Then, very small, in the dark: “Forever?” ʸ

“Forever,” he says. ᴹ

She doesn't say anything else.

She just holds on.

She turns on her side and puts both arms round him and her face in his chest and holds on so hard he can feel her fingernails through his shirt, and she doesn't cry, and she doesn't speak, and she doesn't let go. And he holds her back, all the way round, and puts his chin on top of her head, and they lie like that in the dark until her breathing changes.

When she finally falls asleep, he moves only enough to press a kiss to her forehead. She makes a small sound and curls closer without waking. He stays where he is, one hand spread across her back, because there is nothing he needs to solve before morning and nowhere he needs to be except here.

* * *

He lets himself have it the next morning.

Just for a minute. He doesn't mean to. She's asleep, deeply, for the first time in days, and he slides out from under her arm and goes downstairs and out into the cool grey before dawn, because he can't breathe in the room for some reason he doesn't understand, and he ends up in the orchard.

The third tree from the left.

He climbs onto the branch. The low thick one that grows out sideways, the place near the trunk worn smooth by sitting. He sits there in the half-light with his feet hanging down and his back against the trunk, and looks at the house through the trees.

Her window. Her side. Where the bed would have gone.

He doesn't cry. He still doesn't cry. He just sits there and lets it be in him, finally, all of it, without anyone to hold up or be strong for or find a solution for. The bed by the window. The shallow water. The list Noise has put away somewhere safe. The weight on his shoulders that isn't there.

He sits with it.

* * *

Someone climbs up beside him.

He doesn't hear her coming. She's very quiet, for someone so small. She just appears at the foot of the tree, in her nightclothes, with a blanket round her shoulders, and puts her hands on the trunk, and climbs, badly, the way someone climbs who's only ever watched other people do it. He reaches down and helps her the last bit. She settles on the branch beside him, close, with her shoulder against his arm.

Ori.

She doesn't say anything. She doesn't ask why he's out here before dawn, or what's wrong with Yuka, or anything at all. She just sits beside him on the branch with her blanket round her, and looks at the house, and leans on him.

After a while, she puts her hand on his.

They sit there until the sky goes pale.

* * *

“Is Yuka better?” Ori asks finally. ᴼ

He thinks about it.

“A little,” he says. “Last night was better.” ᴹ

Ori nods, very seriously.

“Frieren said I can't visit until she's better,” she says. “So I don't scare her. Or so she doesn't scare me. Frieren wasn't sure which.” ᴼ

He almost laughs.

“She's a little better,” he says again. “Maybe tomorrow. Maybe the day after. I'll tell Frieren.” ᴹ

Ori nods again. She doesn't let go of his hand.

When they climb down, she goes first, and he spots her, and at the bottom she turns round and hugs him, hard, around the middle, for a long time. Then she runs back to the house without looking back, and he stands under the third tree with the dawn coming up and feels, for the first time in six days, that something in his chest has moved over to make room.

* * *

He tells Frieren that evening.

She's on the porch with her book. She doesn't look up when he sits down beside her.

“She's a little better,” he says. ᴹ

Frieren turns a page.

“I know,” she says. “Ori told me. She also told me she found you in the orchard at dawn and you looked like a lost dog.” ᶠ

He doesn't argue with that.

Frieren closes her book. She looks at him for a long moment with those old, patient eyes that have watched so many people be sad for so many reasons over so many centuries.

“Tomorrow,” she says. “I'll let her go tomorrow.” ᶠ
