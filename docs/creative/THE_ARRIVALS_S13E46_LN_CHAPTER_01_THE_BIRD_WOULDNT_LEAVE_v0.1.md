# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 79
## Chapter 13 — The Bird Wouldn't Leave

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 79
**Reading order:** 13
**Source episode:** S13E46
**Continuity state:** B+343. Yuka is Yuka again, and clingier than before: she knows Mau never once blamed her, loves him more for it, and will never admit that's why. First breakfast downstairs in a week; the house pretends nothing happened, badly. Yuka notices the letter to Elmesia is gone from the bedside table. Mau admits he sent it on the fourth day, because Elmesia's bird sat on the porch rail for three days and refused to leave without it. Gojo's notebook returns. Mau tells Yuka the truth when she asks how he was: he was bad.

*When: The eighth morning. She wakes up before him and, instead of lying there looking at him with her face already wet, she climbs on top of him.*

He wakes up because something is sitting on his chest.

He opens his eyes. Yuka is sitting on him. Not lying, the way she does; sitting, upright, with her knees on either side of his ribs and her hands on her hips, looking down at him with an expression of enormous seriousness.

“Good morning,” she says. ʸ

“Good morning,” he says, carefully. ᴹ

“I'm hungry.” ʸ

He blinks.

“I'm *very* hungry,” she says. “I've been eating soup for a week. I want bread. Shuna's bread. The real kind. From downstairs. At the *table*.” ʸ

He looks at her. Her hair is a mess. Her eyes are still a little swollen. She's wearing his shirt, which she has been wearing for a week, and which he's fairly sure she's never going to give back. And there's something in her face that hasn't been there for eight days. Something with an edge on it. Something that looks like *her*.

“Okay,” he says. ᴹ

“And you're coming.” ʸ

“I'm coming.” ᴹ

“And you're sitting next to me.” ʸ

“I always sit next to you.” ᴹ

“*Closer*,” she says, and doesn't get off him. ʸ

* * *

The house pretends nothing happened.

It's very bad at it. They come down the stairs together, her hand in his, and every single person in the kitchen goes very still for exactly one second and then goes back to what they were doing with tremendous, unconvincing normality. Rimuru says *good morning* to the eggs. Milim drops a spoon. Umaru, who is holding a bag of chips at breakfast for reasons nobody has ever been able to establish, holds it out toward Yuka without a word as she passes, and Yuka takes one without breaking stride.

Shuna puts a plate in front of her. Bread. The real kind.

Yuka looks at it.

Then she looks up at Shuna, and Shuna's face does something, and she turns away very quickly and goes back to the stove and stands there with her back to the room for a while.

* * *

Yuka sits closer.

He didn't think it was possible. There's never been any space between them on the bench since the island. But she finds some. She sits so close that her whole side is pressed against his, shoulder to hip, and she eats with her left hand so that her right can stay on his knee, and every time he reaches for something she reaches for it first and hands it to him, and when he says *thank you* she says *you're welcome* in a voice that makes it clear she's keeping score.

“You're being strange,” he murmurs. ᴹ

“I'm being *nice*.” ʸ

“You're being very nice.” ᴹ

“I'm allowed to be nice to my husband.” ʸ

“You're never this nice to me.” ᴹ

“Then you should enjoy it while it lasts,” she says, and puts a piece of bread in his mouth before he can answer. ʸ

* * *

He knows why.

He doesn't say so. He knows she'll never say so either. But he can see it in her face every time she looks at him: something that wasn't there before the bathroom door, or was, but quieter. Something that knows he sat on a washroom floor and didn't once tell her it was her fault. That he said *it could be me* and meant it, and didn't take it back when she screamed at him. That he looked, when she asked him to, and only then.

When he reaches for his cup, she puts it in his hand before his fingers get there. When he forgets to take the next bite of bread, she breaks off a piece and waits, stubbornly, until he eats it. He catches her looking at him twice. Both times she turns back to her plate and presses her knee harder against his.

She'll never tell him why she's doing it. He takes the next piece of bread from her fingers without asking.

He doesn't say anything. He just lets her.

* * *

Gojo makes a mark in the notebook.

Yuka sees it. She stops with a piece of bread halfway to Mau's mouth and looks across the table.

“I thought Shoko burned that,” she says. ʸ

“She burned the second one,” Gojo says. ᴳ

“How many are there?” ʸ

Gojo considers. “Several,” he admits. ᴳ

Shoko closes her eyes.

But she's smiling. Just slightly. Just at the corner of her mouth. And when Gojo makes a second mark, a moment later, because Yuka has put her head on Mau's shoulder while she eats, Shoko doesn't say anything at all.

* * *

She notices the letter when they go back upstairs.

He's been waiting for her to notice. She stops beside the bed and looks at the bedside table, where the cold porridge bowl has been replaced by a clean cup and the heat pad is folded neatly on the shelf, and frowns.

“Where's the letter?” ʸ

He sits down on the edge of the bed.

“I sent it,” he says. ᴹ

She turns to look at him.

“On the fourth day,” he says. “Elmesia's bird came. The big one, with the band. It landed on the porch rail on the first morning and it sat there. All day. It wouldn't eat anything. It wouldn't let anyone near it. Shuna tried. Ori tried. Beru tried, and it tried to take his hat.” ᴹ

Something is happening at the corner of her mouth.

“On the second day it was still there,” he says. “On the third day it was *still* there. It was getting rained on. It looked miserable. Frieren said it was going to sit there until it got what it came for, because that's what Elmesia's birds are trained to do, and that it might actually die of stubbornness.” ᴹ

“So you sent it.” ʸ

“On the fourth day,” he says. “You were asleep. I went down and gave it the letter and it took it and looked at me like I was an idiot for making it wait, and flew away.” ᴹ

She's staring at him.

“You didn't tell me,” she says. ʸ

“You were—” He stops. “It was the fourth day.” ᴹ

* * *

She sits down on the bed beside him.

She doesn't say anything for a while. He waits. He doesn't know whether she's going to be angry. He thinks she has a right to be. It was her letter too. Her words, mostly. The *some* people, and the fruit that he didn't say anything about, and the bird that he's forgiven.

“Did you read it again?” she asks. ʸ

“No.” ᴹ

“Not even the part about the fruit?” ʸ

“Especially not the part about the fruit.” ᴹ

She makes a sound. Then another one. Then she puts her face in his shoulder and laughs, quietly, the way she laughed at the sign in the common room yesterday.

“The bird waited three days,” she says, muffled. ʸ

“In the rain.” ᴹ

“Because we couldn't come down.” ʸ

“Because we couldn't come down.” ᴹ

She stops laughing. She stays where she is with her face in his shoulder.

“Was it the right thing,” she says. “Sending it. After.” ʸ

He thinks about it honestly.

“I don't know,” he says. “I didn't think about it very much. I just thought you'd still want to go. Whenever you were ready. And I thought Elmesia should know we were coming. Eventually.” ᴹ

She's quiet for a moment.

“I still want to go,” she says. ʸ

“I know.” ᴹ

“I still want to go with *everyone*.” ʸ

“I know.” ᴹ

She lifts her face. Her eyes are wet but she isn't crying.

“Thank you for sending it,” she says. ʸ

* * *

Later, sitting on the porch steps in the afternoon sun with her in his lap and her head under his chin, she asks him.

“How were you?” ʸ

He goes still.

“This week,” she says. “While I was—how were *you*?” ʸ

He could say *fine*. He used to say *fine*. He used to say it to her about everything, about wounds and dungeons and rooms with beds in them, and she always knew, and she always let him, because that was the shape of them then.

It isn't the shape of them now.

“Bad,” he says. ᴹ

She doesn't move.

“I was bad,” he says. “I didn't cry. I don't know why. I just went through everything I have, over and over, looking for something to fix, because I didn't know what else to do. And I made Noise put the list away. And on the sixth morning I went and sat in the orchard on the branch and Ori found me and sat with me and didn't say anything. And it helped. And it was still bad.” ᴹ

She's quiet for a long time.

“It was ugly,” he says. “All of it. It was really ugly.” ᴹ

She turns in his lap until she can see his face.

She doesn't say *I'm sorry*. She doesn't say *you should have told me*. She just looks at him, for a long time, with her hand flat on his chest. And then she puts her forehead against his, and closes her eyes, and they sit like that on the porch steps in the sun.

“Next time,” she says, “you tell me. While it's happening. Not after.” ʸ

“Even if you're on the bathroom floor?” ᴹ

“*Especially* then,” she says. ʸ

He thinks about that.

“Okay,” he says. ᴹ

She settles back against his chest. Down in the yard, the Pillow is lying in the grass with a small person asleep against its side, and a scaled shape is curled in a patch of shade by the fence, very close, much closer than it used to lie. Pretending, very hard, that it just happened to end up there.
