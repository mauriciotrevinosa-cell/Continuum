# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 80
## Chapter 2 — The Things He Forgot

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1 — opening inventory arc; creator review pending
**Volume:** 80
**Reading order:** 2
**Source episode:** S13E57
**Continuity state:** B+355, afternoon. Shoko permits Mau downstairs for a short supervised visit, with his left arm splinted and his side still healing. Yuka insists on reviewing his inventory together. They find ordinary neglected supplies and old project notes, not free power upgrades. Noise notices a gap in the old inventory index but cannot yet prove that hidden objects exist. A level-200 readout is a historical system record, not an automatic level gain. Mai keeps construction decisions in her workshop. The house has not yet settled the island guest list. No intimate scene.

*When: B+355. Three days after the return. Shoko has agreed that sitting at the common-room table is not, by itself, a combat technique.*

“Show me,” Yuka says. ʸ

Mau looks at her.

“Show you what?”

“The inventory.”

“Which part?”

“All of it.”

“That isn't a part.” ᴹ

“Then start at the beginning.”

He's sitting on the long bench by the common-room table, with his left arm in a splint, a cushion behind his injured side and a blanket over his knees that Shuna brought before he'd managed to refuse it. Yuka is beside him, pressed against his good right side. She has a notebook.

She also has a pencil.

That should worry him more than it does.

“Why do you have a notebook?” he asks. ᴹ

“Because you told me the story of a king you forgot to tell me you owned, and I started wondering what else you forgot.” ʸ

“I didn't forget Rime.”

“You forgot to tell your wife about Rime.” ʸ

“That's different.”

“I know. This notebook is for both kinds.”

* * *

The first thing he takes out is a coil of rope.

Yuka looks at it.

“Why?” ʸ

“Because it's rope.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It is if you need rope.” ᴹ

He puts it on the table. Next come two folded tarpaulins, a small bundle of spare straps and a box of ordinary screws.

“That's not the interesting part,” Yuka says.

“I didn't say it would be interesting.”

“You said you had everything.”

“I said I had a lot of things.”

“Then show me the a lot of things.” ʸ

Mau glances toward the fireplace, where the Pillow is asleep with its nose in Ori's lap. Ori is reading something to it. Lantern is curled on the rug beside them, making a careful show of not listening.

None of them helps.

* * *

He opens the list the way he always does.

A familiar arrangement comes up in the part of his vision that doesn't belong to the room. Inventory. Shop. Supplies. Registered equipment. Entries acquired from trials long enough ago that their labels no longer mean much without the records attached.

The eyes don't give the room a new colour. They still show him the table, the bread, the people, all in the shades he has always known. Only Yuka, leaning against him with her pencil hovering, brings colour into it.

“Okay,” he says. “Basic supplies. Tools. Material. Equipment. Old records.” ᴹ

“Candy.”

“That's under food.”

“Show me the candy.” ʸ

“I haven't got candy in my hand.”

“That's why you're showing me the inventory.”

He opens the Shop instead. A small packet appears in his good hand. The balance changes by exactly the amount it should.

Yuka takes it before he can put it down.

“You used money,” she says, pleased. ʸ

“That's how the Shop works.”

“I know. I just like watching you buy me things.”

“That's a very expensive hobby.”

“You're very good at it.” ʸ

She opens the packet, takes one sweet and holds a second to his mouth. He eats it because he's learned what happens when he tries to refuse.

From the other end of the table, Anko says, “If the inventory is open, I also have a request.” ᴬⁿ

Yuka doesn't turn.

“Get in line.” ʸ

* * *

By the time Mai arrives, there are four piles.

The first is things the house actually uses. Rope, tools, supplies and a few old boxes whose contents are exactly what the labels say.

The second is things they brought back from somewhere and then forgot to distribute.

The third is equipment that needs a proper check before anyone should use it again.

The fourth is a collection of things that nobody at the table can explain.

“What's this?” Yuka asks, holding a small metal fitting by two fingers. ʸ

Mau leans closer.

“I don't know.”

“You bought it?”

“Maybe.”

“From where?”

“I don't know.” ᴹ

She makes a mark.

“That's two.”

“Two what?”

“Things you don't know.”

Mai takes the fitting from her and turns it over, examining the edge.

“It's an ordinary part,” she says. “You don't need a theory. You need to stop putting parts from different jobs in the same place.” ᴹᵃ

“I didn't put it anywhere.”

“You put it in a place where you can find it and then never looked at it again.”

“That sounds like putting it somewhere.” ᴹ

“It sounds like the same thing you do to my scissors.” ᴹᵃ

“I don't take your scissors.”

“You once took a whole bench because you thought it was in the way.”

Yuka writes something in the notebook and won't show him what.

* * *

Mai moves the equipment pile closer to herself.

There are pieces of metal he knows, some from old jobs, some with marks he remembers from the red gate. There are records attached to the katana that he has been meaning to revisit. The three rings have notes against them, not a finished construction.

Mai reads the notes silently.

“Don't tell me you've already made the thing,” she says. ᴹᵃ

“I haven't.”

“Good.”

“Why is that good?” ᴹ

“Because I would like to know what you think the thing does before you give it to me to fix.”

Noise speaks in his head.

“The retained observations are not a constructed result,” Noise says. “No new ring has been built or installed.” ᴺ

“Noise agrees with you,” Mau says. ᴹ

“Noise has excellent moments,” Mai answers. ᴹᵃ

Yuka looks from one to the other.

“So the katana gets a project, not a miracle.” ʸ

“Exactly,” Mai says.

Yuka writes *KATANA — ASK MAI FIRST* in large letters.

Mau reaches for the notebook with his good hand. She lifts it out of range.

“You're supposed to be helping.”

“I am helping.” ʸ

* * *

There is another problem.

Noise notices it while the table is distracted by a debate over whether a tarpaulin that has never been unfolded counts as a useful tarpaulin.

“The index has gaps,” Noise says. ᴺ

Mau stops.

“What sort of gaps?” ᴹ

“Entries whose sequence numbers exist in older records but are not displayed by the current list. That doesn't prove the objects are still available. It could be an indexing fault. It could be an obsolete reference. It could be something the old system withheld.” ᴺ

Yuka feels him go still.

“What?” she asks. ʸ

He tells her.

All of it. The missing positions, what Noise can read, what Noise can't.

“Can you open them?” ʸ

“Not yet.”

“Can you tell whether it's dangerous?”

“Not yet.”

“Then don't open them in the common room.”

“I wasn't going to.” ᴹ

She studies him.

He studies her.

“You were thinking about it.”

“I was thinking about asking Noise.”

“That's a kind of thinking about it.” ʸ

He gives up.

Ciel's voice comes through the other place inside him, calm enough to sound almost amused.

“First compare the old manifest against recorded physical inventory,” she says. “Then determine whether there is anything to recover. No action on unknown entries until the discrepancy is understood.” ᶜ

“That is exactly what I was about to suggest,” Noise says. ᴺ

“After I suggested it,” Ciel says. ᶜ

Yuka closes her eyes.

“You live with those two all day?”

“Yes.” ᴹ

She leans her head against his shoulder.

“I suddenly understand a lot about you.” ʸ

* * *

The next surprise is more embarrassing.

It isn't an object. It's an old status record, buried among the files left from the system that used to own the rules.

Level 200.

He looks at it. Then he looks again.

“That's still there,” he says. ᴹ

Noise is silent for a moment.

“It was recorded under the former system. It doesn't establish that the same leveling mechanism is operational now,” Noise says. ᴺ

“I know.”

“What is it?” Yuka asks. ʸ

He tells her.

She blinks. Once. Twice.

“You forgot about levels?” ʸ

“I didn't forget.”

“You put them in a folder and never looked again.”

“That isn't forgetting.”

“It's exactly forgetting. With extra steps.” ʸ

Anko looks up from the other end of the table.

“What did he forget?” ᴬⁿ

“His level,” Yuka announces. ʸ

Silence.

Then Ori, from beside the Pillow: “You're allowed to forget a level?” ᴼ

Gojo, who has appeared in the doorway at a very unfortunate moment, says, “Some of us have never been allowed to forget that he's on a different level.” ᴳ

“Gojo,” Shoko calls from the hall, “don't make him demonstrate anything.”

“I was going to ask him to stand up.”

“Especially don't do that.” ˢʰ

The room starts laughing. Mau puts his good hand over his eyes.

Yuka takes the opportunity to steal another sweet.

* * *

They keep working after the laughter stops.

They don't discover a secret weapon. They don't recover a missing level or turn a ring into a finished tool. Mai takes a written list back to her workshop. Noise and Ciel agree to compare records before anyone touches the gaps. The old status stays a record, not an invitation to press a button without knowing where it goes.

What they do find is useful.

Three boxes of supplies can go to Shuna. The extra straps can go to the people who maintain the carts. The tarpaulins can go to Bellion's stores, provided Bellion actually wants them.

Ori gets a small empty container for her shells. It is not magical. She is delighted.

And Yuka gets the rest of the sweets.

“That's not fair,” Mau says. ᴹ

“You bought them for me.”

“You told me to.”

“Exactly.” ʸ

She rests her head on his good shoulder again, and turns a page of her notebook.

At the top of the next page she has written *ISLAND*.

“That's not inventory,” he says. ᴹ

“No. It's the next thing we need a list for.”

“Elmesia said everyone.”

“Elmesia said we can bring everyone. That's different from deciding who is actually going.”

“Right.”

“And this time,” Yuka says, putting the pencil down, “we're asking people before we put them on anything. I want the house to come. I also want some time when we're not surrounded by the entire house.” ʸ

He knows what she means.

“I'll help you ask,” he says. ᴹ

“I know you will.”

She reaches across him for the notebook. He catches it with his good hand before it slides off the table, and she gives him a look that makes him think she did it on purpose.

“Don't make Shoko regret letting me come downstairs,” he says. ᴹ

“Then don't go anywhere without me.” ʸ

He isn't going to.

* * *

When Shoko calls an end to the afternoon, Mau doesn't argue.

He can't. His side aches and his arm is heavy, and the last thing he wants is to go back to the room and admit that the whole afternoon of sitting down has tired him out.

Yuka sees it anyway.

She closes the notebook. She puts the remaining sweets in her pocket. She moves to his good side, offers him her arm and waits until he takes it.

He doesn't say *I'm fine*.

“I'm tired,” he says. ᴹ

“I know.”

“It hurts more now.”

“I know that too.” ʸ

She doesn't sound pleased to have been right. She just stays beside him as they walk slowly toward the stairs.

At the first step he looks back at the common room: four smaller piles than before, people taking the things they can actually use, Ori trying to persuade the Pillow that its new shell container is not a toy.

For once, finding something he forgot hasn't meant fighting it.

Yuka puts her hand over his good one on the banister.

“Tomorrow,” she says, “we go through the rest.” ʸ

“Tomorrow Shoko might make me stay in bed.”

“Then I'll bring the notebook.”

He looks at her.

She smiles.

“And more candy.” ʸ

That, he thinks, is going to be the expensive part.
