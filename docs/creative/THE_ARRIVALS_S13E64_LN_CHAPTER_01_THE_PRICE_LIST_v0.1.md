# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 80
## Chapter 15 — The Price List

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 80
**Reading order:** 15
**Source episode:** S13E64
**Continuity state:** Inside the Rulers' trial, day three. The first full day of living in the gate. Yuka takes over the money: she counts the currency the monsters drop, writes prices in the notebook, and discovers Mau's old habit of buying the cheapest of everything and hoarding the rest "in case". She makes him buy a real mattress. Her jacket was eaten through by something's venom on day two; Mau buys her clothes and gets every size wrong but one. Kamish requests a Shop meal aloud, with dry, imperious humor; as a speaking shadow he has personality and agency, not just eyes and silent gestures. The cut above Mau's hip is checked morning and night; he says "turned" when it pulls. In the evening, Beru tells Yuka the first story from the tower. No intimate scene inside the gate.

*When: Inside the trial, day three. Outside, the same B+370 morning; Ori has reached the house and Shoko is already running.*

The money comes in small heaps.

That's what Yuka notices first, on the third morning, while he's still packing up the tent. Every time something dies, it leaves a little pile where it fell: flat grey discs no bigger than a thumbnail, cold, heavier than they look. The system calls them *[Currency]*. He's never given them any other name.

She picks one up. She turns it over. She looks at the heap on the floor, and at the bag on his hip where he's been sweeping them without counting, and at him.

“How much have we got?” she asks. ʸ

He thinks about it.

“Enough,” he says. ᴹ

“That's not a number.” ʸ

“It's enough for what we need.” ᴹ

She holds out her hand.

He looks at it.

“The bag,” she says. ʸ

* * *

She counts it sitting cross-legged on the stone with the notebook open on her knee.

It takes a long time. He finishes the tent. He feeds the little stove. He checks the bandage over his hip, which pulled when he bent to roll the blankets, and says *turned* to nobody in particular, and she says *I heard* without looking up.

Then she turns the notebook round and shows him.

A column of numbers. What they've got. And beside it, in her neat small letters, what everything costs.

*Tent — 40. Blankets (2) — 12. Stove — 30. Pot — 6. Water (2 skins) — 4 each. Bread — 1. Sweets (3) — 2 each.*

And underneath, a total. And under that, what's left.

He looks at it.

“That's a lot left,” he says. ᴹ

“It's a *lot* left,” she says. “Mau. You've got enough in here to buy the whole first page six times. Why is the tent the cheapest one?” ʸ

“It's a tent.” ᴹ

“It's got a hole in it.” ʸ

“It's a small hole.” ᴹ

“Why is the blanket the thin one?” ʸ

He doesn't answer.

She looks at him for a long moment. Then she looks at the page again, at the column of everything he bought, every one of them the cheapest thing on its page, and he watches her understand.

“You're saving it,” she says. ʸ

“In case.” ᴹ

“In case of what?” ʸ

He doesn't know how to say it. *In case the Shop closes. In case the money stops. In case this turns out to be the other kind of gate after all, and tomorrow the page says [Unavailable], and there's nothing, and I have to make what we've got last a year.*

He doesn't have to say it. She's already closed the notebook.

* * *

“Open it,” she says. ʸ

He opens the Shop.

She leans in against his arm and goes through it page by page the way she does on the porch, the way she did the night of the inventory, demanding sweets. Only this time she's not looking at the sweets.

“That one,” she says, and points. ʸ

A mattress. A real one. Thick, rolled, with straps.

“That's eighty,” he says. ᴹ

“We have eleven hundred.” ʸ

“We might need—” ᴹ

“We might need to *sleep*, Mau. On something that isn't stone. You've got a new seam across your ribs and a hole in your back that won't close and you've been sleeping sitting up against a tent pole.” ʸ

He buys the mattress.

“And that,” she says. ʸ

A blanket. The thick one.

He buys the blanket.

“And that. And that. And—no, not that one, that one's ugly. That one.” ʸ

He buys all of it. A second lamp. A proper basin. Soap that isn't the grey bar from the bottom of the page. A pan that isn't the pot. Oil for the pan. Salt. A bag of something dried that she says is for soup.

When she's finished, she looks at the numbers again.

“Seven hundred left,” she says. “And we've only done three floors.” ʸ

She turns to look at him. Her face is very gentle.

“It's not going to stop,” she says. “The money. It's not like there. You don't have to make it last. You just have to *spend* it on us.” ʸ

He looks at the Shop. Every page, still open.

“Okay,” he says. ᴹ

* * *

Then her jacket.

The venom ate it, on the second day. Something on the city's edge spat at her and he got Infinity there in time for her skin but not for the cloth, and the left sleeve came away in strips that smoked. She's been wearing his spare shirt since, with the sleeves rolled up four times.

He buys her clothes while she's washing her face in the new basin.

He gets it wrong.

He knows he's getting it wrong while he's doing it. He's never bought her clothes. He's bought her sweets, and a hat, and a bracelet with a mirrored flower on it from a market a long time ago. But never clothes. And the Shop gives him sizes, and the sizes are numbers, and he doesn't know which number she is.

So he buys three of everything.

She comes back with her face wet. She looks at the pile on the mattress. Three jackets, three shirts, three pairs of trousers, each one a different size.

She picks up the biggest jacket. It would fit Beru.

She picks up the smallest. It would fit Ori.

She picks up the middle one. She puts it on.

It fits.

She looks down at herself. Then at him. Then at the two jackets that don't fit, lying on the mattress.

“You bought three,” she says. ʸ

“I didn't know which.” ᴹ

“So you bought *three*.” ʸ

“You said spend it.” ᴹ

She starts to laugh.

She laughs so hard she has to sit down on the mattress, which is very good, very thick, very worth eighty, and then she lies back on it in her new jacket with her arms flung out and laughs at the dark where the ceiling should be.

“The middle one,” she says, when she can breathe. “I'm always the middle one. Write it down.” ʸ

He writes it down. In her notebook. In his own crooked letters, under the prices.

*Yuka: middle.*

DIO comes out while Yuka is still laughing.

She sits down on the end of the mattress in her own body, bounces once to test it, and picks up the jacket that would fit Ori.

“I could have told you,” she says to Mau. “Middle. I've watched her try things on at the market. You were there. You were looking at her face.” ᴰ

“You didn't say anything.” ᴹ

“You didn't ask,” says DIO. “And this was funnier.” ᴰ

She folds the small jacket neatly and puts it at the bottom of the bag, the way she'd put away something she might want later, and goes back to watching Yuka laugh.

* * *

Kamish asks about lunch at noon.

He's been lying across the tent door every night with one eye open. The shadows don't need ordinary meals to stay alive, but Kamish has never considered the absence of need an argument against a good meal. When Mau puts the pan on the stove and the smell of something frying goes out across the alcove, the dragon raises his head.

“Monarch,” Kamish says, his voice deep enough to make the tent pole tremble, “if that is for the two of you, I request an equal courtesy.” ᴷ

Yuka sits up.

“You could have asked yesterday.” ʸ

“I was being dignified.” ᴷ

DIO, seated on the mattress with the jackets beside her, makes a sound that might be a laugh.

“That's what we're calling it?”

Kamish looks at her.

“You wear a hat fashioned by a child.” ᴷ

“And you want a lunch big enough to feed a village,” DIO replies. “We're all making choices.” ᴰ

Mau opens the Shop. There's a page he's never had a reason to visit: *[Provisions — Large.]* He buys the biggest thing on it: a whole side of something, salted and wrapped in cloth.

Kamish examines it.

“Acceptable,” he says. ᴷ

Then he lowers his great head, takes it in one bite and settles across the threshold again, visibly pleased.

Beru, at the mouth of the alcove, makes a small sound.

“My king,” he says, very carefully. “I would not say no.” ᴮᵉ

Mau buys another one.

“Dignity,” Yuka says, pointing at the dragon. ʸ

“Precisely,” Kamish answers, without opening his eye. ᴷ

* * *

The bandage, morning and night.

It becomes a thing they do. Like the stove. Like the turns. In the morning, before anything else, she kneels behind him on the mattress and lifts his shirt and unwinds the bandage and looks at the cut above his hip. She doesn't make a face. She tells him what she sees.

“Same,” she says, the third morning. “It's not bigger. The edges are red.” ʸ

He can't see red. He never can. But he knows what she means.

“Hot?” ᴹ

She lays her hand near it, not on it.

“A little.” ʸ

“That's normal. It's the RCT fighting it.” ᴹ

“Is it?” ʸ

“Noise?” ᴹ

“Stable,” Noise says, through the link. ᴺ

“The heat is his healing working against the gate's mark,” Ciel explains. “It is not getting worse. He and Beru can maintain the tissue, but the cut will reopen until the Warden dies.” ᶜ

She winds a new bandage round. Tight, the way he showed her.

“You'll tell me if it changes,” she says. ʸ

“I'll tell you.” ᴹ

“Even a little.” ʸ

“Even a little.” ᴹ

At night, the same. Kneeling behind him. The lamp. What she sees. And every time he turns too fast during the day and the cut pulls, he says *turned*, out loud, wherever they are, and she says *I heard*, and writes a short line in the notebook.

It's very strange, he thinks. Telling someone. Every time. Out loud.

It's very strange how much lighter it is.

* * *

That night, Beru tells her a story.

It isn't planned. Yuka can't sleep. She's lying on the new mattress under the new blanket with her head on Mau's chest, and Mau is on watch, sitting up against the tent pole with her half in his lap, and Beru is at the mouth of the alcove the way he is every night.

“Beru,” Yuka says. ʸ

“Yes, my queen.” ᴮᵉ

“Tell me about the tower,” she says. “The climb where you met him. Not the Architect's field later—the tower.” ʸ

Beru turns his head.

He looks at Mau. Asking. Mau doesn't know what his own face is doing.

“Go on,” Mau says. ᴹ

Beru folds his wings.

“It was a tower with a hundred floors,” Beru says. “And my king could leave between ascents. He did leave. Sometimes the others were waiting when he came back with wounds that should have killed him. Shoko repaired him. Sometimes she told him not to climb again so soon.” ᴮᵉ

Yuka's hand finds Mau's under the blanket.

“Did he listen?” she asks. ʸ

Beru looks at Mau. Mau looks back.

“Not enough,” Beru says. “He climbed again. Before I became his soldier, he fought me with every tool he had. It was not a clean duel. And after he raised me, we fought together so often I learned the distance between his steps better than my own wingspan.” ᴮᵉ

“The tunnel with the hundred mouths,” Mau says. ᴹ

“Precisely. My king remembers. I took the passage on the left, he struck through the gap I made, and a beast tried to eat his new boot. He was more furious about the boot than the teeth.” ᴮᵉ

“I was furious that you laughed,” Mau says. ᴹ

“I removed the beast before the boot was lost,” Beru replies, visibly pleased with himself. “But there were many such fights. He nearly died on some floors. When we came out, Shoko and the others repaired what they could. I could heal him too, though never enough to make those climbs harmless.” ᴮᵉ

“Beru,” Mau says. ᴹ

“My king, you asked me not to lie to her.” ᴮᵉ

Mau cannot argue with that.

“He nearly died on more than one floor,” Beru continues. “It was not the year the Architect took him away. I was not with him inside that field. I knew he was still alive from our bond, but Noise was the one who heard him speak there. I will not pretend those are the same memory.” ᴮᵉ

Kamish lifts his great head from the doorway.

“Nor was it only Beru in the tower,” he says. “I remember the floor where you came for me. I remember the cost of that battle.” ᴷ

“I remember it too,” Mau says. ᴹ

“I should hope so,” Kamish replies. “You were there.” ᴷ

Yuka lets out a short laugh, then looks down at their joined hands.

“What about the cheapest things?” she asks. ʸ

“That came from several places,” Mau says. “The Architect's field made it worse. Beru saw what I kept doing afterward; it doesn't mean I forgot why.” ᴹ

Beru inclines his head.

“I watched him do it,” Beru says. “I did not know how to ask him to stop. He asks much less of me now. He lets me live at the house instead of dragging me into every fight, and I enjoy it. Tonight I stand here because I choose to. That part is true too.” ᴮᵉ

Yuka is very still.

“Thank you,” she says, to Beru. ʸ

“It is nothing, my queen,” says Beru. ᴮᵉ

“It's not nothing,” says Yuka. ʸ

She doesn't say anything else. She just holds Mau's hand under the blanket, hard, and after a while she falls asleep like that, on the thick mattress, in the middle jacket, with seven hundred left in the bag.
