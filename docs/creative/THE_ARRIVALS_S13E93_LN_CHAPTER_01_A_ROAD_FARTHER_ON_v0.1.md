# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 82
## Chapter 9 — A Road Farther On

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S13 v0.1
**Volume:** 82
**Reading order:** 9
**Source episode:** S13E93
**Continuity state:** Mau seeks a boundary, door or rule and delays returning because he fears his presence causes loss. Ciel cannot converse through old Trial. No new current powers or definite chronology established.

*When: Selected inn Trial memory; solitary search feels like years, exact elapsed time unverified.*

The first evening, he expects something to stop him.

A wall across the road. A change in the sky. A voice telling him that leaving was another answer the place had prepared for.

Instead there is a cart with a damaged wheel.

The man beside it has taken the load down and is staring at the split wood with the exhausted concentration of somebody who has already tried the only repair he knows. Mau slows as he approaches. The man looks up, sees that he is alone and looks past him for the person who might actually be useful.

“Can you hold it?” Mau asks. ᴹ

The man glances back at him.

“What?” ᵃᵗᵗ

“The axle. If you lift that side, I can reach.” ᴹ

It takes them longer to get the weight where they need it than to mend the damage. The man checks the wheel afterward, suspicious of a repair he did not understand. Mau waits while he rolls it forward and back.

“What do I owe you?” ᵃᵗᵗ

“Do you know where this road ends?” ᴹ

The man points. There is a village, a fork before it, a bridge which may still be flooded. His description is practical and incomplete. Mau asks what lies beyond the village. The man names another place.

“And after that?” ᴹ

The man looks at him more carefully.

“Where are you trying to go?” ᵃᵗᵗ

Mau has no answer he can use here.

He accepts directions to the bridge. The man offers him a place on the cart, but it is travelling the wrong way, and when Mau says no the man does not try to turn it into a lesson.

He climbs up, tests the wheel once more and leaves.

Mau watches the cart disappear around a bend. The world has allowed a small problem to be solved without demanding anything terrible in exchange.

He does not trust it enough to feel relieved.

---

At the bridge, the water has gone down.

He spends the night on the dry side of the bank, listening for the sound which will mean somebody has followed him. There are insects in the grass. Farther upstream, something heavy moves through the shallows and then away.

He calls for Ciel once.

He knows better. He has known better for a long time. The part of him which has learned the rules does not always stop the part which reaches for a familiar voice.

Nothing answers.

He lies back and watches the shapes above the trees until he cannot keep his eyes open.

In the morning he checks the bridge.

It has ordinary damage: worn joints, a place where the recent water dragged branches against the supports. He looks beneath it, along both banks and up the road beyond. There is no sign waiting to be read correctly. No hidden instruction becomes clear when he stands at the angle he failed to try before.

He crosses anyway.

---

For a while, he keeps a record.

The road he followed. The place where he turned. What he asked and what answered. He writes down a ruined arch because it looks like a door, then crosses that out after spending most of a day proving it is only a ruined arch.

He tests distances. He goes farther than he needs to go, doubles back and tries a different direction. He looks for the edge where a world made to contain him might stop spending detail on things nobody expects him to examine.

The detail continues.

There is moss on the far side of stones. There are old repairs in the backs of buildings. A woman in one village tells him that the place he wants is beyond the ridge, then admits she has never been there and is repeating something her brother said. He asks where to find her brother. He has been dead for years.

Mau thanks her for trying to help.

He leaves before she can ask why he looks as though the answer has hurt him.

At night, when there is nowhere sensible left to search, he thinks about the inn.

He tries to imagine the first supper without him. Milim complaining that he left in the middle of a conversation. Fern putting away the cup he should have washed. Frieren opening the book again and finding that he moved the place marker when he sat down.

He lets those things happen in his head.

Then he tries to let another supper happen after them.

If they can get that far without the place finding them, perhaps he has done the right thing.

He cannot prove it from here. He keeps walking as though proof will be waiting one road farther on.

---

The weather changes before he admits he has been gone too long.

He wakes with his hands cold and the ground beneath the shelter damp from rain which fell while he slept. It is not the first rain. It is the first one which makes him think of the wet cloth on the stair rail and understand how much he would give to have that small, stupid problem in front of him again.

He packs immediately.

For most of the morning he travels toward home.

He knows which direction it lies. He has kept enough marks, enough scraps of the route, to make the first turns without looking at the record. The decision almost feels easy. He will go back, see them, find out whether leaving helped. If it did, he can go away again before anything happens.

Then he reaches the place where he left the main road.

He stops.

He has made bargains with himself before. Small exceptions which sounded reasonable until the room changed and somebody died in the space the exception opened. He can remember the exact relief of believing he had finally understood a rule, and what followed it.

He stands there until the rain begins again.

When he moves, he takes the other road.

He tells himself he has not finished looking.

It is true. It is not the whole truth.

---

There is a village where he stays through a stretch of bad weather.

The room has a low ceiling, a window which sticks and a bowl someone has left beneath the place where the roof leaks. He fixes the roof after the second night. The woman who owns the room tells him he could have mentioned it after the first.

He does not know how to explain that he was glad to have something which could be fixed by finding where the water entered.

She feeds him before asking whether he intends to stay. He says he is looking for a place. She asks its name. He says he does not know.

She accepts that less easily than the man with the cart.

“You'll have trouble finding it, then.” ᵃᵗᵗ

“I have.” ᴹ

She pushes the bread closer to him. The gesture is brisk, almost irritated. She has decided he has not eaten enough, and he cannot summon the energy to argue about it.

In the mornings he goes out. In the evenings he returns with one more place he can cross off. The woman begins leaving the bowl on the table at the time he usually comes back, as though he is a person with a place at supper rather than a question she has not been able to answer.

One evening he reaches for the other bowl.

It is empty. Nobody has sat there since he arrived.

For a moment he had expected Frieren to pass it to him.

He puts his hand back in his lap.

The woman asks whether the food is all right. He says it is. She waits, unconvinced, but he has no version of the explanation which can be given to her over a bowl of soup.

He leaves after the weather improves.

She wraps food for the road. He tries to refuse the extra portion and is told, in a tone which reminds him of Fern, that he has already paid for it by mending the roof.

He carries the parcel for a long time before he can bring himself to open it.

---

The record becomes harder to keep.

Some days he writes the same question in different words and does not notice until he reads the pages back. Some roads seem familiar because he has walked them. Others are familiar because the dream in which he finally found a way used the same stretch of trees.

He knows how to measure a day. He stops being certain what the total means.

There are places where the season has turned when he passes through again. There are people who remember him and people who say he must be thinking of somebody else. Once a child he recalls being carried at a woman's hip runs to the gate ahead of her. Mau stands still so abruptly that the woman asks whether something is wrong.

He tells her he thought he knew the road.

He cannot tell how long he has been gone. Long enough for the things he pictured at the inn to become guesses rather than continuations.

He begins imagining changes.

A different arrangement of the tables. The repaired frame painted over. Frieren annoyed because he has stayed away after the reason to stay away became something he could no longer explain even to himself.

In some of those thoughts she is angry when he returns.

He finds himself wanting that version so badly that he has to sit down beside the road.

He wants to be late. He wants to have made an unnecessary, selfish, ridiculous journey which everybody will tell him was ridiculous. He wants Milim to shout at him because she was worried and then continue shouting because he did not find a dragon to describe.

He wants the time he has lost to be the only thing the place has taken.

---

He does not find a door.

There is no rule beyond the ridge. No person waiting in the last village to tell him that he has walked far enough. The farthest place he reaches contains an ordinary road which goes farther still.

He stands on it for a long time.

Then he turns around.

The decision does not arrive with confidence. He cannot prove that going back will be safe. He cannot prove that remaining away has done any good at all.

He has only run out of ways to call not knowing a reason to keep them waiting.

On the first night of the journey back, he takes out the pages and begins sorting them into the order he will need. The old directions are harder to read than he expects. His handwriting looks like the handwriting of somebody he used to know.

He follows it anyway.

When he sleeps, he dreams of the door opening before he has knocked.
