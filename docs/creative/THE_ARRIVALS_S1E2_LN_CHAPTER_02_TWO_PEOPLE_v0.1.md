<!-- scene-id: s1e2-ln02-one-room -->

They did not repair the inn.

They made pieces of it stop being problems.

That was different.

On the first day, the fireplace became usable.

One section of the common room became clean enough to sleep in.

A broken window became a boarded window.

Mau held the board.

"Higher."

He raised it.

"A little."

He lowered it.

Frieren stared at him.

Mau stared back through the gap.

"That was lower."

"Yes."

"You said higher."

"I changed my mind."

He considered this with the same seriousness he gave most things.

Then raised the board again.

"Here?"

Frieren pushed one corner into place.

"Mostly."

Mau's expression changed by almost nothing.

She was beginning to recognize that as amusement.

The nail went in.

Mau stepped back and held his hand near the edge of the frame.

Cold air slipped through.

"Still cold."

"Less cold."

"That keeps being your standard."

"For now."

The next morning, he used the phrase on her before she could use it on him.

That was how some things began.

* * *

<!-- scene-id: s1e2-ln02-days-pass -->

Days accumulated through small evidence.

A pile of broken glass near the wall became a smaller pile.

Firewood appeared beneath the stairs.

The common-room table lost its layer of dust.

A second route to the well became obvious because Mau kept taking the same path.

He learned which floorboard sounded dangerous without actually being dangerous.

Frieren learned that if she asked him to bring back "usable wood," she had to define usable once.

Only once.

The field remained dormant.

The inn remained damaged.

The surrounding houses remained ruins.

Nothing became comfortable quickly.

But each evening they had fewer things to solve before they could eat.

On the third morning, Frieren found Mau at the well before she remembered to check it.

On another day, she returned from the field and found that he had stacked the salvaged boards by length because "it seemed easier."

It was easier.

She did not tell him immediately.

He noticed when she started using the piles.

* * *

<!-- scene-id: s1e2-ln02-what-came-with-him -->

The backpack stopped being mysterious all at once and became mysterious in smaller pieces.

Mau emptied it onto the driest section of floor while his black-and-orange McLaren hoodie hung near the fire to dry.

Frieren sat nearby with a book.

She was reading.

Mostly.

Mau picked up the gray watch he had taken off while washing.

Orange accents crossed the face.

The name came easily.

"TAG Heuer Formula 1."

Frieren looked up.

"You remember that?"

"I know what it is."

"That's different."

Mau looked at the watch.

It was.

He knew it was a watch.

He knew how to set it.

He knew the name printed on it.

He did not know who had bought it.

Did not know when.

Did not know whether he had chosen it himself.

The hands pointed to a time that belonged to a world he could not place.

"What time is it?" Frieren asked.

Mau looked.

Then toward the window.

"I don't know if this time means anything here."

Frieren considered that.

"Probably not."

He set the watch beside him.

The clothes were easier until he looked at them too long.

A navy knitted polo.

"Massimo Dutti."

A red long-sleeved Ferrari team shirt.

A plain white T-shirt.

The white Real Madrid jersey with **MAU T** and **5** on the back.

The McLaren Triple Crown cap.

A bucket hat with **Un Verano Sin Ti Tour** worked into the design.

Mau could name nearly all of it.

That made the absence stranger.

"Ferrari is a racing team," he said.

Frieren turned a page.

"Okay."

"McLaren too."

"Okay."

"Real Madrid plays football."

"What's football?"

Mau opened his mouth.

The answer existed.

Rules.

Field.

Ball.

Goal.

All of it.

A whole sport with no memory attached to learning it.

He laughed once.

Frieren lowered the book.

"What?"

"I know what football is."

"That's good."

"I don't know if I've ever watched it."

Frieren looked at the jersey.

"Maybe you did."

"Maybe."

No revelation came.

Mau folded the shirt again.

The clothes did not return his life.

They did something smaller.

They gave the room evidence that he had once had one.

Over the next days the backpack stopped staying packed.

The navy polo appeared over the back of a chair.

The Ferrari shirt dried near the window after being washed.

The cap ended up beside Frieren's books because Mau had put it down there and forgotten it.

The watch moved from his wrist to the table at night and back again in the morning.

His old life remained inaccessible.

His things began acquiring new locations anyway.

* * *

<!-- scene-id: s1e2-ln02-first-snow -->

Snow came properly one afternoon.

By then Frieren had shifted into her cold-weather clothing rather than treating every day like a continuation of the road. Mau had learned to rotate the few clothes in his backpack instead of wearing the McLaren hoodie until it could stand on its own.

Mau stopped in the lane.

Frieren walked several steps before realizing he was no longer beside her.

She looked back.

He was staring upward.

"Are you coming?"

"In a minute."

A snowflake landed against his fingers and disappeared.

"I know what snow is."

Frieren waited.

"I don't know if I've seen it before."

The sentence had become a category of its own.

Mau knew things.

Then discovered there was no life attached to knowing them.

Frieren had stopped trying to predict where the gaps would be.

He brushed the water from his hand.

"We should move the wood inside."

He went before she answered.

A week earlier he would have waited for instructions.

Frieren watched him disappear around the side of the inn.

Then returned to the window she had been trying to seal.

* * *

<!-- scene-id: s1e2-ln02-night-pattern -->

The nights changed too.

The first few, they slept near the common-room fire because the rest of the inn held cold too well.

Once they had one upstairs room dry enough to trust, changing clothes stopped being a luxury. Frieren stopped sleeping in travel clothes and used her simple sleepwear when the cold allowed it. Mau rotated through the shirts from the backpack and learned which pieces dried fastest near the hearth without scorching.

Frieren read.

Mau asked questions until he became tired enough to stop.

Then, gradually, he asked fewer.

Not because he had run out.

Because silence between them stopped needing to be filled.

One night Frieren woke and found Mau's blanket had shifted closer to the fire.

And to her.

He was still asleep.

Another night, she looked up from her grimoire and realized he had been sitting beside her for several minutes without speaking.

"What?"

Mau looked over.

"Nothing."

He returned to what he was doing.

Frieren understood, eventually, that he had copied the answer from her.

She let it go.

* * *

<!-- scene-id: s1e2-ln02-fern -->

Frieren had been on the same page long enough that she could no longer pretend she was reading it.

The fire was low.

Mau was late.

Not very late.

He had gone to collect usable wood from one of the collapsed houses near the edge of the settlement. The route was short. He knew it. Frieren had already checked the area for danger.

She knew all of that.

The door opened.

Cold air entered first.

Then Mau, carrying several pieces of wood.

Snow melted across his shoulders.

"You took a long time."

"One of the beams was heavier than it looked."

"That happens."

"I know now."

He put the wood beside the hearth.

Normally, that would have ended the conversation.

Tonight he looked at her for several seconds.

The book open but unread.

Her shoulders too still.

The way her hand stayed near Fern's ornament without quite touching it.

He knew that face now.

Not perfectly.

Enough.

So Mau did not ask what was wrong.

He sat near the fire.

Not directly beside her.

Near enough.

Frieren turned another page she had not read.

That made it harder.

She closed the book.

"I was thinking about Fern."

Mau waited.

"She would've complained about this place."

"What part?"

"All of it."

Mau looked around the common room.

"That seems reasonable."

A small sound escaped Frieren.

Not quite a laugh.

It disappeared quickly.

"She would've fixed the windows before I did."

"You fixed them."

"Poorly."

"Mostly."

Frieren looked at him.

He had learned that from her.

For some reason, that hurt.

Her fingers touched the ornament.

"I should've seen it sooner."

Mau's expression changed.

Not surprise.

He had heard pieces of this on other nights.

"Fern would've listened if I'd been faster."

There it was.

The thought she could carry during the day as long as there was wood to move or food to find.

At night it became heavier.

Mau did not tell her she was wrong.

He did not know that.

He shifted closer.

Stopped before touching her.

"I don't know how to fix that."

Frieren looked at him.

"I know."

Mau looked toward the fire.

For a few seconds, she thought that was all.

Then he said, "Everything will be okay."

Frieren said nothing.

"I'm here to help you."

It was almost absurd.

He did not know where he came from.

He did not know whether anyone waited for him.

There were still ordinary things he understood only as facts without memories.

And somehow he was the person promising her that things would be okay.

Mau did not offer proof.

He simply stayed there.

Frieren moved first.

She leaned into him.

Mau caught her awkwardly, an arm settling around her only after he was sure she wanted it.

For several seconds Frieren stayed rigid.

Then her hand closed against his shirt.

"Okay."

That was all she managed.

Mau heard it.

He did not add anything.

He knew by then that after a certain point, words stopped helping.

Frieren's shoulders loosened.

Then her breathing.

When the tears came, they came without a speech.

Mau held her.

The fire continued to burn down.

* * *

<!-- scene-id: s1e2-ln02-after -->

The next morning, neither mentioned it.

Mau handed Frieren a cup.

Frieren took it.

They went outside to work.

That night she sat closer before either of them became tired.

Several nights later, she spoke about Fern again.

Not the fight.

A complaint Fern used to make.

Something ordinary.

Mau listened.

That was different.

The grief had not become smaller.

The space around it had.
