# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 12
## Chapter 6 — Every Place Set

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 12  
**Reading order:** 6  
**Continuity state:** Second disappearance, Day 7 evening to Day 9 | Old inn = home base | The household that stayed | Mau/Ori: missing

Momo set the table for everyone.

Everyone.

She counted out bowls the way she had every night since the inn grew too crowded for anyone to count properly, and when she reached the end of the long table she set two more.

One at the corner where Mau always ended up.

One beside it.

Seiko watched from the kitchen door.

She had been in this house less than a week.

Long enough to learn where the spoons went.

Not long enough to understand why her granddaughter's family had a stranger in the middle of it.

"Those are for the two who left," Seiko said.

"Yes."

"They're not coming to dinner."

"I know."

Momo put a cup at Mau's place anyway.

Seiko waited.

Momo kept her back turned.

"If I don't set them," she said finally, "everybody looks at the table and counts. Then nobody eats."

She straightened a spoon that did not need straightening.

"This way they just see two empty places. That's better than counting."

Seiko said nothing for a while.

Then she went to the cupboard, brought back a second cup, and set it at the place beside Mau's.

For the girl.

Momo looked at it.

Did not say thank you.

Did not need to.

At dinner nobody counted.

Seiko noticed that too.

Okarun noticed something else.

Turbo Granny was sitting on the windowsill above Mau's empty place, where she liked to sit when she wanted to insult him while he ate.

She was not saying anything.

Okarun had never once, in all the time he had known her, seen her sit somewhere and say nothing.

He did not point it out.

He just moved his own plate one seat closer to the window.



Bocchi had been told to help here.

So she had tried.

She had carried water and spilled half of it.

Folded blankets wrong.

Hidden in the pantry for eleven minutes, which Kita had timed and then forgiven.

Then she saw the boy from the north road.

He was sitting on the bedroll Fern had found for him at the edge of the common room, knees pulled up, back to the wall.

He was looking at the room the way a person looks at a room where everyone already knows everyone.

Bocchi knew that look.

She had worn it for most of her life.

She stood very still.

Every part of her said: someone else. Someone better at this. Kita. Momo. Anyone.

Then she remembered someone sitting down beside her once, when she had been the one against the wall, and not asking her to be anything.

She went over.

Sat down.

Not next to him.

Near.

The boy looked at her.

Bocchi looked at the floor.

A minute passed.

"...It's loud here," Bocchi said.

The boy blinked.

"Yes."

"It gets less loud."

"Does it?"

Bocchi considered this honestly.

"No. You just learn which people are the loud ones."

He laughed.

Surprised into it.

Bocchi turned very red and did not leave.

Across the room, Nijika stopped talking in the middle of a sentence.

Ryo followed her eyes.

They watched Bocchi—Bocchi, who had once played her first live from inside a mango box—sit down beside a stranger because he looked the way she used to look.

"When did she learn to do that?" Nijika said quietly.

Kita, beside them, did not take her eyes off her girlfriend.

"Here," she said.



Umaru did not tell anyone what she was doing.

That was the important part.

In the morning she filled the lamp on the stairs before it ran dry.

Brought in wood before anyone noticed the pile was low.

Put the kettle on first, so it was already warm when Fern came down.

Small things.

The things that got done every morning without anyone seeing who did them.

Fern saw.

She stood at the bottom of the stairs with the warm kettle in her hand and looked at the full lamp and the stacked wood and the girl being extremely busy with a cushion.

"Umaru."

"I didn't do anything."

"That's his."

Umaru hugged the cushion.

"He always does it," she said. "Nobody ever has to ask."

Fern was quiet for a moment.

"I know," she said. "I used to wonder who it was. For months."

Umaru looked up.

"Then you found out."

"Then I found out."

Fern poured two cups and gave one to her.

"Don't burn yourself on the lamp."

That was all.

Umaru did it again the next morning.

And the next.



At night, Maki took the porch roof again.

Nobody assigned it this time.

She went up after dinner and the house understood.

Mai found her there an hour later.

She did not ask permission to climb up.

She sat a careful distance away, which was how the two of them had always sat.

For a while neither spoke.

"You used to hate being left behind," Mai said.

"I still hate it."

"But you stay."

Maki watched the dark road.

"Somebody has to be here when they come back."

Mai looked down at the house.

Lit windows.

Voices.

Someone laughing too loudly.

Someone else telling them to be quiet, people were sleeping.

"This is what you chose instead of the clan."

Not quite a question.

Maki was quiet.

"It's what I chose," she said. "Don't make it about them."

Mai almost smiled.

Then a third shape climbed onto the roof without making a sound.

Nazuna.

She had slept the entire day and was, for the first time since sunrise, completely awake.

"Night watch?" she said. "I'm good at nights."

Maki looked at her.

"Anko's still out there," Nazuna added, as if that explained something.

It explained everything.

She settled on Maki's other side, leaned back on her elbows and looked up at the sky like someone who had looked at a great many skies.

"So," she said, to nobody in particular. "Why does everybody keep running off after this guy?"

Maki thought about how to answer.

Mai answered first.

"I'm still working that out," she said.

Nazuna laughed softly.

"Yeah," she said. "Me too."

Below them the last lamp went out.

Three people kept watch until morning.



The second search was not easier than the first.

Only more practiced.

The house knew where the maps went now.

Who would forget to eat.

Which stair creaked when someone was trying not to be heard leaving.

Knowing did not make the waiting smaller.

It made it shared.
