# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 13
## Chapter 19 — The Longest Day

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 13  
**Reading order:** 19  
**Continuity state:** Day 17 afternoon — Day 18 evening | Rimuru in recovery | Household waits | Witches arrive

The first thing Maomao did was try to find a pulse.

She knew it was pointless before she started.

She did it anyway, because her hands needed a procedure, and the only patient in the room was a small blue slime lying very still in Frieren's lap.

She pressed two fingers to it.

It was cool. Slightly yielding. Like touching the surface of very thick water.

No pulse.

No breath.

No temperature she could call a fever or the absence of one.

Maomao sat back on her heels.

"Milim."

Milim jumped.

"What?"

"How do you know when he's alive like this?"

Milim looked at the slime.

She opened her mouth, then closed it again, and for the first time anyone in that house had seen, she thought carefully before she answered.

"He keeps his shape," she said finally. "When he's really hurt, really really, he gets runny. At the edges." She pointed. "He isn't runny."

Maomao looked at the edges.

They were not runny.

She took out her notebook.

PATIENT: SLIME.

BASELINE: UNKNOWN.

INDICATOR OF LIFE: NOT RUNNY. (SOURCE: MILIM.)

She looked at what she had written for a long moment.

Then she put the notebook away and did not take it out again for the rest of the day.



Frieren did not move.

Fern tried, gently, after the first hour.

"There's a bed."

"No."

"You could hold him on the bed."

"No."

Fern looked at her.

Then at the slime.

Then she went and took every pillow and blanket off the empty bed and built a nest on the floor around Frieren, the way she had once built them for an old priest who would not go to bed, and Frieren let her, and did not say thank you, and did not need to.



The house did not know what to do with itself.

That was the most honest description of the afternoon.

People went downstairs because Fern asked them to.

Then they came back up, one at a time, because downstairs was too far away.

Then Fern sent them down again.

By the third time, she stopped.

The corridor outside the room filled up slowly, the way a room fills with water.

Umaru and Coco on the top step.

Bocchi against the wall, knees up, Kita beside her, holding her hand so tightly that both their knuckles were white.

Okarun, standing, because he could not make himself sit.

Momo beside him, not standing, not sitting, leaning on the wall with her eyes closed.

Diablo, in the doorway, exactly where he had been when the light went out.

He had not moved.

He did not move all afternoon.



Anko started a file.

Of course she did.

She sat on the stairs with a board on her knee and wrote, in her tight investigator's hand:

INCIDENT. DAY 17.

Containment began approx. late morning.

Approx. 20 min observation, no anomaly reported.

Then—

She stopped.

She looked at the word then for a long time.

Then she tore the page out of the board, folded it in half, folded it again, and put it in her pocket.

Nazuna, who had been woken by the pressure and had not gone back to sleep, sat down on the step beside her.

"You stopped."

"Yes."

"You never stop."

Anko looked at the closed door.

"For once," she said, "I don't want to know what happened. I just want to know he's okay."

Nazuna considered that.

Then she leaned her head on Anko's shoulder, which she had never done before, and Anko let her, which she had never done before either.



Downstairs, Seiko made tea that nobody drank.

Momo had come down to help her and was now standing at the stove doing nothing, holding a spoon.

"Grandma."

"Mm."

"What was it?"

Seiko poured water into the pot.

She did not answer for a while.

"Not a spirit," she said at last. "I've seen every kind of spirit there is. Hungry ones. Angry ones. Lost ones." She set the kettle down. "This wasn't hungry. It wasn't angry. It just wanted to see." She looked at the ceiling. "Everything. All at once. Like a child who's never been told there's anything it shouldn't look at."

Momo looked at the ceiling too.

From somewhere near the window, Turbo Granny said:

"Whatever's in that kid scared me."

Momo turned.

The old yokai was sitting on the sill with her arms crossed and her face, for once, entirely without mockery.

"And I'm dead," Turbo Granny added. "So."

Nobody had a reply to that.

Seiko poured the tea anyway.



Yuta went outside.

He did not tell anyone.

He walked to the edge of the yard and stood with his back to the inn and his hands at his sides, and they were shaking.

Maki found him there.

She did not ask.

She stood next to him for a while.

Then she took one of his shaking hands in hers, firmly, the way you take hold of a railing on stairs.

"Purple," Yuta said.

"I saw."

"I watched him make it once." His voice was very level. Too level. "On the field. I watched him build it in his hands, and I watched it go off, and I watched it kill him."

Maki held on.

"And today it was trying to happen again," said Yuta. "By itself. Inside Rimuru. And all he could do was hold it apart."

"He held it."

"He shouldn't have had to."

Maki was quiet for a moment.

"No," she agreed. "He shouldn't."

They stood there a long time.

Yuta's hand stopped shaking eventually.

Maki did not let go when it did.



The people who had come later, the ones who had arrived in the weeks of the search and stayed, did not go upstairs.

It was not their corridor.

Not yet.

They sat in the common room instead, in a loose uneasy circle, and tried to understand what they had felt come through the walls.

Jinshi spoke first.

He spoke the way he spoke about everything: carefully, precisely, already three moves ahead.

"I want to say something unpleasant," he said. "And I want to say it once, here, so that nobody has to say it later."

The circle looked at him.

"Everyone in this house has been treating that man as a person who is loved," said Jinshi. "And he is. I have seen it." He paused. "But what we felt this afternoon was not a person who is loved. It was a capability. A very large one. If anyone outside this house ever learns what he can build, they will not see the man. They will only see that."

Nobody answered.

Mai, at the edge of the circle, was looking at the stairs.

"What is he?" she said, to nobody in particular.

Maki had come back in from the yard with Yuta.

She heard.

She stopped in the doorway.

"Ours," she said.

Mai looked at her sister for a long moment.

She did not argue.

Rem had not sat in the circle.

She was upstairs, in the room, with a brush and a pan.

She had been in that room when the light went out, and she had watched the doorframe splinter under Diablo's hand, and she had watched a man become a slime on the floor, and the only thing she had known how to do afterward was what she had always done when the world was too large.

Clean.

She swept the splinters into the pan, one by one, carefully, around Frieren's nest of blankets.

Frieren did not look up.

But when Rem finished, and stood there not knowing what to do with the pan, Frieren said, very quietly:

"Thank you."

Rem nodded.

She did not leave the room after that.

She sat down against the wall near the window and stayed.

Kaneki and Aira came up an hour later and sat on either side of her.

None of them spoke.

They had been in the forest. They had carried Mau out once.

They had all three understood, this afternoon, that they had not understood anything at all.

Downstairs, Nijika sat next to Ryo on the bottom step, listening to the faint sound from the corridor above: Bocchi, playing so quietly through the wall that it was almost not there.

"I didn't get it," Nijika said. "Why she trusts him like that. Why she'd follow him anywhere when she can barely follow anyone to the end of a street."

Ryo did not say anything.

"I think I'm starting to," said Nijika.

Ryo was quiet a while longer.

"She's playing for him," Ryo said finally. "Through the wall. In case he can hear."

"Can he?"

"Doesn't matter," said Ryo.



The witches came at dusk.

They came fast, and together, and without any of the slow formality they had kept to every other time.

Fern met them at the door.

"You're early."

"He went out," said the eldest of them.

Fern stopped.

"What?"

"We have been able to feel the seams in him since we found him by the stream. All of us. Faintly. Like a thread of light you stop noticing." The witch's voice was tight. "This morning the thread went out. Like a candle. Then, at noon, it was everywhere. Then—" She stopped. "Nothing. We have felt nothing for six hours."

"He's alive," said Fern.

"You're certain?"

Fern thought about the slime in Frieren's lap, and Milim's not runny, and Maomao's unopened notebook.

"I'm certain enough to stand here," she said.

Qifrey came down the stairs.

He explained.

Containment. Consent. Twenty minutes of nothing, and then everything.

Under the agreement, he did not have to tell them all of it.

He told them most of it anyway.

When he finished, the witches looked at one another.

"We found him by a stream," said the eldest. "Half dead. We carried him. Whatever our bargain is, we have some right to be frightened."

"Yes," said Qifrey. "You do."

The witches did not leave.

They did not come inside, either.

They waited in the yard all night, in their dark coats, in a small silent group under the inn's one lantern, watching the upstairs window.

Nobody asked them to go.

Around midnight, Himmel went out with a tray.

He did not know them. They had never been kind to him in particular. He brought them tea anyway, and set it down on the edge of the well, and sat on the low wall a little way off and watched the upstairs window with them.

The eldest witch looked at him.

"Why?"

Himmel shrugged.

"You're waiting for the same person I am," he said. "That seemed like enough."



Night came.

Ori had been in the room since the beginning.

She had held herself together all afternoon, carrying water, carrying messages, carrying the things that needed carrying, because if she stopped carrying things she would have to look at the slime in Frieren's lap and think about what was inside it.

At some point after dark, she stopped carrying things.

She sat down in Fern's nest of blankets beside Frieren.

She did not say anything.

She reached out, slowly, and put her hand on the slime too, beside Frieren's.

It was cool.

It did not move.

Frieren looked at Ori's hand.

Then at Ori.

Then, without a word, she shifted the slime so that it rested half in her lap and half in Ori's, and put her arm around Ori's shoulders, and they sat there like that while the lamp burned down.

Ori fell asleep around midnight with her head on Frieren's arm.

Frieren did not.



Milim came in after midnight.

She had been sitting outside the door beside Diablo all evening. Diablo had not spoken. Neither had she. It was the longest Milim had ever been quiet in her life.

Now she came and sat on the floor across from Frieren, cross-legged, and looked at the slime.

"Can I ask you something?"

Frieren looked at her.

"What's he like?" said Milim. "Mau. I mean really. I only know him a little bit. He's Rimuru's brother. Rimuru said so. And Rimuru just—" She gestured at the slime. "Rimuru just did that for him. So I want to know."

Frieren was quiet for a long time.

Then she told her.

Not everything.

Not the important things, the ones that belonged to Frieren.

The small ones.

That he burned food. That he said yes to every ridiculous plan anyone brought him. That he helped people and then looked embarrassed when they noticed. That he had died once, on a field, to keep Frieren alive. That he had come back.

Milim listened to all of it with her chin on her knees.

When Frieren stopped, Milim was quiet for a while.

"Okay," she said. "Then he's my brother too."

Frieren looked at her.

"That's how it works," said Milim firmly. "Rimuru's brother is my brother. So when he comes out, I'm going to protect him too. Even from Diablo." She paused. "Especially from Diablo."

From the doorway, without turning around, Diablo said:

"I heard that."

"Good," said Milim.

And Frieren, who had not smiled since late morning, felt the corner of her mouth move, just once, before it went still again.



Morning came.

Nothing changed.

Maomao checked the edges of the slime. Not runny.

Momo made breakfast for a house that did not eat it.

Okarun could not stay still any longer and went running, out the gate and down the road and back, again and again, Turbo Granny's speed in his legs and nowhere to go, until Momo went out and stood in the road and he had to stop or run into her.

He stopped.

She held him.

Neither of them said anything about it afterward.

The witches were still in the yard.

Diablo was still in the doorway.

Bocchi had fallen asleep against the corridor wall with her guitar in her lap, and Kita had not moved so as not to wake her, and Kita's arm had gone completely numb, and Kita did not care.

Noon.

Afternoon.

The light moved across the floor of the room and over Frieren's blankets and onto the small blue shape in her lap.

Coco and Umaru took turns on the top step.

Fern counted the hours and did not say the number out loud.

At some point in the afternoon she went into the pantry to get flour nobody needed and did not come out.

Stark noticed.

He did not go in.

He stood outside the pantry door with his back to it, between it and the rest of the house, the way he had stood in front of Frieren on a hundred roads, and if anyone came looking for Fern, he said she was busy.

After a while he heard her stop crying.

After a while longer the door opened and she came out with flour, and her face was dry, and she handed the flour to him without a word, and he carried it to the kitchen, and nobody ever mentioned it.



It was nearly evening when Frieren felt it.

Not movement.

Not exactly.

The slime in her lap was cool, and still, and then, for no reason she could name, it was very slightly less cool.

She went still.

Ori, who had woken hours ago and not left, saw her face change.

"What?"

Frieren did not answer.

She was looking down.

At the edges of the small blue shape.

At the bitten side, which was, she thought, very slightly less flat than it had been.

And then, slowly, the way a person stirs before they are fully awake, the slime moved.
