# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 13
## Chapter 22 — Not the Elf Route

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 13  
**Reading order:** 22  
**Continuity state:** Day 19 — dusk | Frieren learns the elf route is closed for now | Milim intervenes | Ring One holds her

Rimuru told her himself.

He could have let her piece it together. He could have let Maomao explain it in the morning, clinically, in the language of routes and reference states.

He did not.

He came out of the long session at dusk, grey with tiredness, his new arm grown back as far as the elbow and no further, and he found Frieren in the corridor, and he told her.

"It's done. For now. He's settled."

"Settled how?"

"Arrival-state," said Rimuru. "As close as Ciel could get. The routes are quiet. One outlet stays open, the eye, so the pressure has somewhere to go. Everything else is back where it was when he came."

Frieren looked at him.

"Everything else."

"Yes."

"The elf route."

Rimuru held her gaze.

"Closed," he said. "For now."



For a moment nothing happened.

That was what Milim would remember afterward.

Not the light.

The moment before it, when Frieren's face did nothing at all, and Milim, who had spent a thousand years around people far more dangerous than they looked, felt every hair on her arms stand up.

"You closed it," said Frieren.

"Frieren—"

"It was there. Ciel said so. His body could have done it. He could have—" Her voice did not rise. It went lower, and quieter, and somehow that was worse. "He could have lived. Not forty years. Not sixty. Long enough. Long enough that I wouldn't have to—"

She stopped.

"Do you know how many people I've watched die?" she said.

Rimuru did not answer.

There was no answer.

"I stopped counting," said Frieren. "Not because I forgot. Because the number stopped meaning anything. They all go. That's what happens to the people I love. They go, and I stay, and I go on and on and on, and I find out afterward what they were like, because I didn't look closely enough while they were alive." Her hands were shaking. "And for one day, for one day, there was a door. And you closed it."

"He chose arrival-state."

"He chose it because he was dying," said Frieren, "and you told him it was the safe one."



The light came up around her hands.

Pale.

Precise.

The killing spell, the one she had used more than any other in a thousand years, gathering at her fingertips without a word, without a gesture, the way breath gathers before a scream.

The glass in the corridor window cracked.

Down the stairs, someone shouted.

Diablo was moving.

He did not walk. He was simply, between one instant and the next, at Rimuru's side, and the air around him had gone very cold, and his smile was the most terrible thing in the house.

"Diablo," said Rimuru. "Stand down."

"Rimuru-sama, she is—"

"Stand. Down."

Diablo stopped.

His smile did not.

Frieren did not look at him.

She was looking at Rimuru, and the light in her hands was brightening, and the corridor was filling with a pressure that made the floorboards groan, and nobody in that house had ever seen Frieren like this, not even Fern, not even once.

Then Milim was in front of her.

Not attacking.

Not striking.

She simply stepped between them, small and barefoot, and put both her hands around Frieren's wrists, right over the gathering light.

It burned.

Milim did not let go.

"He's inside him!"

Frieren's eyes moved to her.

"Mau's inside him!" Milim's voice cracked straight down the middle. "If you hit Rimuru, you hit Mau! He's right there, Frieren, he's in there, he's asleep in there, you'll hit him—"

The light stuttered.

Frieren looked at Rimuru.

At the body that had been holding Mau for two days.

At the arm that had grown back only to the elbow.

"Hit me instead," said Milim. Tears were running down her face, and her hands were smoking where they held Frieren's wrists, and she did not let go. "I can take it. I can take anything. Hit me if you have to hit something. Not him. Please. Not my brothers."

The light went out.

Not all at once.

It guttered, the way a candle does in a draft, and then it was gone, and Frieren was just a small pale woman in a corridor with her wrists in someone else's hands, shaking.



"Let him explain," said Milim. "Please. Just let him explain."

Frieren did not answer.

But she did not pull her hands away, either.

Rimuru took a breath.

"It isn't gone," he said.

Frieren looked at him.

"The route. It isn't erased. It appeared. That's the thing that matters. His body knows how to model it now. Ciel recorded the shape of it." He paused, listening. "She says she keeps everything. She says it's in the inventory, next to everything else."

"Then why—"

"Because if we'd made it the template yesterday, while everything in him was coming apart, there was a very good chance it would have killed him." Rimuru's voice did not waver. "Not might. Probably. His body was already trying to become five things at once. If we'd pushed it toward one new thing, all the way, while he was like that, it would have been shock. Or he'd have locked halfway. Or he'd have come out and not been Mau. Maomao ran the numbers with Ciel. They weren't close."

Frieren said nothing.

"I didn't choose against your future," said Rimuru. "I chose for his tomorrow. That's all I could see. Tomorrow. I'm sorry it's all I could see."

"Not forever?"

"Not forever. When he's stable. When we understand more. When he can say yes to it while he's himself, and not while he's dying." Rimuru swallowed. "Maybe. I can't promise. I won't promise. But not never."

Frieren looked down at Milim's hands on her wrists.

At the red marks spreading across Milim's palms.

She made a small sound.

Milim let go.

Frieren did not move.



Fern came up the stairs first.

She had heard everything. Everyone had heard everything.

She did not say anything wise.

She walked up to Frieren and put her arms around her, the way she had done once on a road in another world when Frieren had found out too late about someone else, and held on.

"You wanted to burn down the inn," said Fern into her hair.

"Yes."

"Over a door that's still there."

Frieren's shoulders shook.

"Yes."

"That's stupid," said Fern. "I would have done it too."

Then Ori, who did not say anything at all, and simply pressed herself against Frieren's other side.

Then Bocchi, who could not get close because of all the arms, and so held on to the back of Frieren's coat with both hands and would not let go.

Coco.

Yuta, who put one hand on Frieren's head, very lightly, the way you would steady something.

Maomao, at the top of the stairs, arms crossed.

"If you'd forced it," Maomao said, "I'd have been writing a death record tonight instead of a progress note." She paused. "I prefer progress notes."

Momo, who had come up holding a ladle because she had been cooking, and who did not seem to notice she was still holding it.

Anko, who stood a little apart, the way an older sister stands when she is making sure everyone else is all right first, and then, when she was sure, came in too.

Nobody said it was okay.

Nobody liked any of it.

But somewhere in the press of arms, Frieren heard it anyway, in the way they held on.

Alive first.

Then the rest.

We'll find the rest.



Umaru was the one who finally made her laugh.

She had wormed her way in under Fern's arm, as she always did, and now she looked up at Frieren from somewhere around her elbow, face blotchy, nose running, and said:

"You know what we should do."

Frieren looked down at her.

"When Mau figures out how to use all his weird sealed stuff," said Umaru. "In like a hundred years. We make him make long life. For everyone." She sniffed. "Then you won't get left behind with a bunch of graves. You'll get stuck with all of us. Forever. Being annoying. Eating your snacks."

There was a pause.

Coco, very seriously: "That's actually a good plan."

"It's a great plan," said Umaru.

"It's a terrible plan," said Fern.

"I'd sign up," said Okarun from the stairs, and Momo elbowed him, and then said, "Me too."

And Frieren laughed.

It came out wet, and broken, and surprised, and she put her hand over her mouth as if to catch it, and it came out anyway.

She laughed until she was crying again, and then she was doing both, and then mostly she was just holding on to all of them, as many as she could reach, with both arms, and not saying anything.



Later, when the corridor had emptied, she went to find Rimuru.

He was sitting on the floor of the room, back against the empty bed, flexing his new hand to the elbow and watching it as if it belonged to someone else.

Frieren sat down beside him.

For a while neither of them spoke.

"I'm sorry," she said.

Rimuru shook his head.

"Don't be."

"I nearly—"

"I know." He looked at his hand. "If someone had taken that from me. From Mau and me. A door like that." He was quiet. "I'd have wanted to burn the world down too."

Frieren looked at him.

"You're not angry."

"I'm tired," said Rimuru. "And I have half an arm. And I just found out Raphael was a person all along and I never asked. I don't have room to be angry at you."

Frieren nearly smiled.

She did not quite manage it.

She did something else instead.

She leaned sideways until her shoulder was against his, and stayed there.

Down the hall, outside the door, Milim was sitting on the floor with her burned hands in a bowl of cold water that Maomao had brought, and Diablo was sitting beside her, and after a long time, in a voice nobody else would ever hear him use, Diablo said:

"That was very brave."

Milim sniffed.

"I know," she said. "I'm amazing."

Diablo did not argue.
