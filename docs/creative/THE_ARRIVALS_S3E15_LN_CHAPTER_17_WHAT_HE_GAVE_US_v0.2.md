# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 13
## Chapter 23 — What He Gave Us

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 13  
**Reading order:** 23  
**Continuity state:** Night of Day 19 | Rimuru and Yuta on the roof | The question Mau always asks

Rimuru could not sleep.

That was new.

For most of his life in his own world he had not needed to. Then he had learned to, for the pleasure of it, the way you learn to enjoy a meal you do not strictly require.

Tonight he lay on the roof of the inn with Mau asleep somewhere inside him and his new arm ending at the elbow, and sleep would not come, and he understood for the first time how it felt to want it and not have it.

Yuta found him there a little after midnight.

He climbed up the back of the inn with two cups of tea, which was harder than it looked, and handed one over, and sat down on the slope of the roof a little way off.

Rimuru took the cup in his good hand.

"How did you know I was up here?"

"Diablo told me."

"Diablo knows where I am?"

"Diablo always knows where you are," said Yuta. "Tonight he told me because he wanted someone to come and sit with you who wasn't him."

Rimuru looked at the cup.

"That's—"

"I know," said Yuta. "I think it's the nicest thing he's ever done."



They drank their tea.

The night was clear and cold. Down in the yard, the witches had finally gone home. Somewhere below, Bocchi was not quite playing, just touching the strings now and then, the way you touch a sleeping person to make sure they are still there.

"Is he all right?" said Yuta.

Rimuru listened inward.

He is asleep, said Ciel. Ordinary sleep. Nothing is pulling. The eye is closed.

"Asleep," said Rimuru. "Ordinary sleep."

Yuta let out a long breath.

Then he did not say anything for a while, and neither did Rimuru, and the silence was comfortable in the way silences are between people who have both been afraid of the same thing for two days.



"Can I tell you something stupid?" said Rimuru.

"Yes."

Rimuru turned the cup in his hand.

"In my world," he said, "after I became what I became, I stopped feeling things the way I used to." He frowned, looking for the words. "Not all at once. Not completely. I could still decide to care about people. I did. I cared about my people more than anything. I built them a whole country." He paused. "But the feeling itself. The real thing. It was like hearing music through a wall. I could tell it was there. I could tell it was beautiful. I just couldn't quite get to it."

Yuta was listening.

"Raphael did the thinking," said Rimuru. "I did the deciding. And somewhere in between, the feeling got quieter. I didn't even notice. I thought that was just what it was like, being strong."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Then I came here," he said. "And I walked into this inn, and there was a guy at the table who laughed at my joke before I'd finished telling it. And then collapsed. And then woke up and called me brother like it was obvious. Like it had always been obvious."

Yuta smiled a little.

"And the wall started coming down," said Rimuru. "Bit by bit. I didn't notice that either, at first. I just noticed that I was laughing more. And that when someone in this house got hurt, it hurt. Properly. In the chest. The way it used to, before." He looked at the empty end of his sleeve. "That's why yesterday terrified me. Not the arm. Not the shrine. Not the dragon." He swallowed. "The thing in the middle of all of it was the person who gave me my feelings back. And I could hear him screaming, and I couldn't get him out."

Yuta did not say anything for a long time.

Then he said:

"That isn't stupid."

"It feels stupid."

"It isn't."



"Can I tell you one?" said Yuta.

Rimuru looked at him.

Yuta set his cup down on the roof tiles, carefully, so it would not slide.

"When I was a kid," he said, "everyone I loved got hurt because of me. Not on purpose. But because of me. So I stopped letting anyone close." He looked at the dark. "Then I got lucky. I got a teacher. I got friends. Maki. Others. People who were strong enough that being near me didn't kill them." He paused. "I had friends. I had a school. I had a war."

"But not a house," said Rimuru.

Yuta looked at him.

"No," he said. "Not a house."

He picked up his cup again.

"He didn't do it on purpose," said Yuta. "That's the thing. Mau never sat down and decided to make a family. He just kept doing things. He fixed a hinge, and you stayed for dinner. He burned the dinner, and you stayed to help clean up. He asked you how your day was and actually waited for the answer. And one day you looked up and there were forty people at the table and you couldn't remember which of them you'd met first." He shook his head. "People gravitate. I don't know why. I've stopped trying to figure it out. They just do."

"You did."

"I was first," said Yuta. "Almost. I was there the first week." He smiled at the cup. "He gave me a family I didn't have. Indirectly. Without ever saying that's what he was doing. I don't think he knows he did it."

"He doesn't," said Rimuru. "He'd argue with you."

"He'd lose."



Down below, a door opened and closed. Fern, checking on someone. Then quiet again.

"There's something else," said Yuta.

Rimuru waited.

"In my world," said Yuta slowly, "there was a man. The strongest person who ever lived. My teacher. Everything he did, he did better than anyone. When I was a kid I thought nothing in the world could touch him." He looked down at his hands. "He fought Sukuna. And he lost. And there wasn't anything left of him that could come back."

Rimuru was very still.

"And then this idiot," said Yuta, and his voice wobbled, just slightly, "with no technique of his own, and none of the eyes, and nothing but a list in his head that he got from my drawings, stood in front of the same monster on a field. And built the thing my teacher used. Out of nothing. Out of understanding it. And it killed him too." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "And then he came back."

He did not say anything for a moment.

"My teacher didn't get to come back," he said. "Mau did. I don't know what that means. I don't know if it means anything. But when I saw that color again yesterday, coming through your skin—" He stopped.

"I know," said Rimuru.

"I thought it was going to happen again."

"I know."

"I don't think I could watch it again."

Rimuru reached out with his good hand and put it on Yuta's shoulder.

Yuta let him.



They sat together on the roof until the tea was cold.

"He asked me once," said Rimuru, after a long while. "A few months ago. Out of nowhere. Whether I'd go back, if I could. To my world. My people."

Yuta turned his head.

"He asked me too."

"Really?"

"In the kitchen. Late. Like it was nothing." Yuta's mouth twisted. "He asks everyone, eventually. Have you noticed? He waits until it's late and you're tired and then he asks like he's asking about the weather."

"What did you say?"

"I said I didn't know."

Rimuru looked out at the dark.

"Me too."

"What do you think he wants us to say?"

Rimuru thought about it.

"I don't think he wants us to say anything," he said finally. "I think he asks because he's afraid of the answer. And he wants to know how afraid to be."

Yuta was quiet.

"That's a terrible way to live," he said.

"Yes."

"We should tell him."

"Tell him what?"

Yuta looked at him.

"That we don't know," he said. "That we really don't know. But that whatever the answer is, it isn't because of him. And it isn't instead of him."

Rimuru looked down at himself.

At the place, somewhere inside, where a man was sleeping an ordinary sleep for the first time in days.

"When he wakes up," he said.

"When he wakes up," said Yuta.

Neither of them knew yet that the question would come back.

Much later, and much larger, and asked by something that was not Mau.

Tonight, it was just a question.

Tonight, it was just two brothers on a roof, with cold tea, keeping watch over a third.
