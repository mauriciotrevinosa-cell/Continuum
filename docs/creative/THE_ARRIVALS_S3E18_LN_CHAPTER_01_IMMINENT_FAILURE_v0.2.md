# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 14
## Chapter 1 — Imminent Failure

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 14  
**Reading order:** 1  
**Continuity state:** Weeks after containment | Pre-Move construction, old inn | Ciel warns the guardrails are failing | The distance leaks

It had been weeks.

That was the first thing everyone would say afterward, as if the number of quiet days should have counted as a promise.

Weeks since Mau came out of containment with one eye that was not always his and a list of rules pinned inside his door.

Weeks of protocol loosening one careful notch at a time. Maomao signing off on a full day at the worksite. Then two. Frieren no longer checking where he was every time he left a room. Then checking only sometimes. Then only when she wanted to, which was still often, but for different reasons.

The eye opened less.

The fever never came back.

Ciel spoke to him perhaps once a day, usually to say nothing was wrong, and once to tell him that his shelf was, objectively, badly placed, which he suspected Frieren had asked her to say.

Weeks.

Long enough that, on the morning it happened, Mau had been given the most boring job in the house and was grateful for it.



The first habitable cluster at the new site was nearly done, and nearly done meant everyone who could carry anything was out there carrying it.

Rimuru had gone at dawn with Wakana, Yuta, Mikasa, Stark and half the people who had arms.

The inn was quieter than it had been in a month.

Mau was in the kitchen with Seiko, relabeling every jar in the pantry, because the old labels had been written by six different people in four different languages and Maomao had declared the pantry a medical hazard.

"That one's salt," said Seiko.

"It says sugar."

"It's salt."

Mau tasted it.

It was salt.

"Who wrote sugar?"

"Your girlfriend," said Seiko, without looking up.

Through the doorway, from the common room, Frieren said, "It looked like sugar," and turned a page.

Momo was making bread at the end of the table. Ori was sorting lamp wicks with Coco and Umaru, which had turned into a competition nobody could explain the rules of. Bocchi was playing something quiet near the window. Fern had a ledger open and was pretending she could not hear the wick competition.

Ordinary.

Mau wrote SALT on a new label and stuck it on.

Then, very calmly, inside his head, someone said his name.

Mau.

He stopped.

Ciel did not use that tone.

She had a tone for good morning and a tone for your shelf is badly placed and a tone for nothing is wrong. This was none of them.

"What?"

Seiko glanced at him.

He had said it out loud.

The Construct beat is adapting to the guardrails, said Ciel. It has been for some time. I have been watching it press. It has stopped pressing.

"Stopped is good."

No. It stopped because it found the shape of the walls. A pause he had never heard from her before. Failure is imminent.

Mau put the jar down very carefully.

"How imminent?"

Minutes. Perhaps less.

"What will it do?"

I don't know. That is the part I need you to hear. I do not know what it will build.

The kitchen was full of jars and bread and Seiko's hands and Momo humming.

The common room beyond it was full of Ori and Coco and Umaru and Bocchi and Fern and Frieren.

Mau did not know what to do.

For a second, he simply stood there with a pen in his hand, and the whole of his mind went white with not knowing.

Then Ciel said:

Get away from people.



He ran.

He did not decide to.

He did not explain.

He went through the common room and out the front door at a speed that knocked a basket of wicks off the table and sent Coco's careful pile across the floor, and he did not stop, and he did not look back.

Behind him, the room froze.

Every single person in it had seen this before.

Not this exactly.

The shape of it.

Mau leaving.

Mau leaving without a word.

Bocchi was on her feet before the door finished swinging, guitar sliding off her lap, and the sound she made was not his name, exactly, but it was close enough.

Ori had gone white.

Umaru had grabbed Coco's arm without knowing she had done it.

Fern's ledger hit the floor.

And Frieren was already moving.

She did not run like Mau ran.

She did not need to.

She crossed the yard in a handful of steps that were not quite steps, and she caught up with him at the edge of the old field, and she reached for his arm.

Her hand stopped.

Not because she stopped it.

It kept going. She could feel it keep going. She could feel every muscle in her arm still reaching.

It simply never arrived.

The distance between her fingers and his sleeve was the width of a hair, and then it was half that, and then half that, and then half that, forever, her hand coming closer and closer and never, ever touching him.

Frieren stared at her own hand.

Mau turned around.

His face was the most frightened she had ever seen it.

"Don't," he said. "Don't, Frieren, I'm not leaving. I'm not leaving, I swear, it's Ciel, she says it's going to break, I don't know what it'll make, I have to get away from—"

"You're not getting away from me."

"Frieren—"

"I can't touch you."

He looked at her hand.

At the space that was not shrinking.

His face changed.

"That's not me," he whispered. "I'm not doing that."

I know, said Ciel, inside him. It is the distance. It came out first. It is keeping everything away.

"Then stop it!"

I am trying.

Frieren pulled her hand back.

Slowly.

As if the air itself might hurt him if she moved too fast.

"Where are you going?" she said.

Mau looked past her.

At the inn, and the faces in its doorway, and Bocchi with both hands over her mouth, and Ori standing in the yard not moving, and Fern holding Ori's shoulders.

Then the other way.

At the lake.

Wide. Flat. Empty.

"There," he said. "There's nobody there."

Frieren looked at the lake.

Then at him.

"Then I'm coming too."

"You can't touch me."

"I don't need to touch you," said Frieren, "to be there."



He walked to the shore.

Frieren walked beside him, close, as close as the distance would let her, which was not very close at all.

Behind them, at a distance Fern decided on and nobody argued with, the others followed. Ori. Fern. Bocchi, with Kita now, who had come running from the back of the house. Umaru and Coco, holding hands. Momo, still covered in flour. Seiko, who did not run anymore, walking very fast.

Nobody said he was running away.

Nobody had to say it.

They had all thought it, for one second, in the kitchen.

They would all, separately, be ashamed of that second for a long time.

At the worksite, hours away by road, Rimuru had dropped the end of a beam and vanished.

Wakana was left holding the other end.

Yuta, beside him, was already shouting for someone to bring the cart.



Mau sat down on the stones at the edge of the water.

He did not know why.

His legs simply decided.

The distance hummed around him.

It had a sound, now that he was paying attention. Not a noise. A pressure, like the quiet in a room after a bell has stopped.

Frieren sat down on the stones too.

As close as she could.

Which was not very close.

"Ciel," Mau said. "Talk to me."

The walls are bending, said Ciel. Not breaking. Bending. Something is coming out through the bend.

"What?"

I don't know yet.

"You keep saying that."

I know, said Ciel. I hate saying it. I am saying it because it is true.

Mau looked at the water.

It was very calm.

That frightened him more than if it had been stormy.

"Frieren," he said.

"Yes."

"If something happens—"

"No."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were going to tell me to go back to the inn."

He stopped.

He had been.

"I'm not going back to the inn," said Frieren. "Say something else."

Mau laughed.

It came out wrong.

But it came out.

Then the air in front of him, over the water, began to draw itself.

Not light.

Lines.

Thin as hair. Precise as a glyph. Too straight to be anything that grew, too fine to be anything that had been made by hands.

Frieren saw them and went very still.

So did everyone on the shore behind them.

"Ciel," said Mau. "What is that?"

For a long moment, Ciel did not answer.

Then, very quietly, in a voice Mau had never heard her use, because he had never heard her surprised:

I don't know. I have never seen it.

The lines kept drawing.

Above the lake, in the morning light, something that had never existed before in any world began, carefully, to be built.
