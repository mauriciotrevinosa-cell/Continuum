# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 14
## Chapter 3 — Two Hands Full

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 14  
**Reading order:** 3  
**Continuity state:** Three days later | Frieren's cold | Spatial leak noticed under rule eight

Frieren caught a cold.

That was the whole emergency.

An elf who had walked through a thousand winters, slept in ditches, crossed mountain passes in the wrong clothes and once spent a week in a swamp looking for a spell that made socks dry faster, came down with an ordinary cold in an ordinary inn, three days after the door on the lake.

Maomao diagnosed it in under a minute.

"Rest. Fluids. Warmth. She'll be fine in four days."

"Four?" said Mau.

"Five if she argues."

Frieren, from under three blankets, argued.

"Five," said Maomao, and left.



Mau took over.

Nobody asked him to.

Nobody could have stopped him.

He made tea. He made soup. He made a second soup because Frieren said the first one tasted like water, and then a third because she said the second one tasted like effort. He brought blankets. He took blankets away when she said she was too warm. He brought them back when she said she was too cold. He read to her from the grimoire she liked best until his voice gave out, and then he read to her from the one she liked second best, because she said his voice was more interesting when it was tired.

The whole house wanted to help.

The whole house was turned away at the door.

"I can make the soup," said Momo.

"I've got it."

"You've made three soups."

"Four."

"Mau."

"I've got it."

Fern came up with clean cloths. Mau took them at the door and thanked her and closed it.

Bocchi came up with her guitar, because music helped when she was sick, and Mau let her in, because that was different, and she played softly for half an hour while Frieren slept and Mau did not.

Umaru came up with snacks, which Frieren was not allowed, and Mau confiscated them, and Umaru said that was tyranny, and Mau agreed it was, and kept them.

He was happy.

That was the thing everyone noticed and nobody said.

After the lake, after the door, after every week of being the one being watched, checked, carried, asked are you all right, he had someone to take care of again, and he was so glad of it that he lit up like a lamp every time Frieren asked him for something.

Which was why nobody wanted to be the one to tell him.



Stark saw it first.

He was coming up the stairs with firewood for the room, which Mau had not asked for and would therefore accept, and Mau came out of the bedroom with the empty water jug.

"Back in a second," Mau said, to the room behind him.

He turned toward the stairs.

And then he was not there.

Stark stopped on the third step from the top.

Below him, faintly, from the well in the yard, he heard the bucket rope rattle.

Then Mau was there again, at the top of the stairs, beside him, with a full water jug.

"Oh, hey," said Mau. "Is that for us? Thanks."

He took the firewood with his free arm and went back into the room.

The door closed.

Stark stood on the third step from the top for a very long time.



By the second day, four people had seen it.

By the third, nine.

Mau did not walk to the well anymore. He did not walk to the kitchen. He did not walk to the pantry for honey or the cellar for apples or the storeroom for another blanket. He started toward each of those places, the way a person does, already thinking about the next thing, and somewhere in the first step the walking simply went missing, and he was there, and then he was back.

He never noticed.

He never once noticed.

He was too busy thinking about whether Frieren's tea had gone cold.

"Should we tell him?" said Kita, in the kitchen, very quietly, on the evening of the third day.

Nobody answered right away.

"He's so happy," said Umaru.

"He's teleporting," said Momo.

"He's happy and teleporting."

"Is it hurting him?" said Ori.

Everyone looked at Maomao.

Maomao looked annoyed to be the person everyone looked at.

"No," she said. "As far as I can tell. His pulse is normal. His temperature is normal. The eye hasn't opened once." She paused. "He is in better physical condition than he has been since the field camp. Mostly because he is too busy fussing over her to remember to worry about himself."

"Then why tell him?" said Umaru. "Let him have it."

Seiko, at the end of the table, put down her cup.

"Rule eight," she said.

The kitchen went quiet.

"He wrote it himself," said Seiko. "In his own hand. With his own name under it. 'If I break one of these without meaning to, tell me. Don't hide it to be kind.'" She looked around the table. "He's not breaking it out of meanness. He's not breaking it at all. The thing inside him is. And he asked us, specifically, not to protect him from knowing."

Nobody argued.

After a while, Fern stood up.

"I'll do it," she said.



She went up with a cup of tea she knew he would refuse to let her bring in.

He opened the door.

"I've got it—"

"Mau," said Fern. "You've been teleporting."

He stared at her.

"What?"

"To the well. The kitchen. The cellar. Since yesterday morning. Nine of us have seen it." Fern held the cup out. "You don't walk anywhere anymore. You start to, and then you're there."

Mau looked at the cup.

Then down at his own feet.

Then back at her.

"I've been walking," he said.

"You haven't."

"I remember walking."

"Do you?"

Mau opened his mouth.

He tried to remember the stairs. The yard. The rope on the well.

He remembered the rope. He remembered the full jug. He did not remember the yard in between.

His face went very still.

"Ciel," he said.

Spatial, said Ciel, a little guiltily. Small. The method is Diablo's. The sense of where you are is Master's. It is one of the joins. It came through two days ago and it has been coming through every time you want to be somewhere quickly. I was going to tell you tonight. I am sorry. You were so pleased.

"You were going to tell me tonight?"

I checked the guardrails. It is not dangerous. It is just you, not noticing.

Mau put his hand over his face.

Then, to Fern's astonishment, he started to laugh.

"I've been teleporting to get her soup," he said through his fingers. "For two days. I've been teleporting to get her soup."

"Four soups," said Fern.

"Five, now."

"Five soups."

Mau laughed harder.

From the bed behind him, in a voice still thick with the cold, Frieren said:

"I knew."

Mau turned around.

"You knew?"

"You kept coming back too fast." Frieren sniffed. "I thought it was nice."

"You didn't tell me."

"I was sick," said Frieren, with enormous dignity. "Rule eight is for people who aren't sick."

"That's not in the rule."

"It is now."



Diablo found out last, and took it personally.

"My method," he said, standing in the doorway of the kitchen with his hands folded behind his back and an expression of deep, complicated feeling. "Taken from my arrival. Studied. Copied. Joined with Rimuru-sama's own spatial sense. Refined." He paused. "And used to carry soup."

"Five soups," said Momo.

Diablo closed his eyes.

"I have never been so insulted," he said, "or so flattered, in my entire existence."

He went upstairs and stood outside the sickroom door for a while, apparently trying to decide which.



After Fern told him, Mau went back to walking.

Deliberately.

It took effort. Every time he started toward the well, something in him leaned forward, wanting to skip the space in between, and every time he caught it and made himself take the stairs.

On the fourth day, when he came back in with the water, Frieren was sitting up for the first time.

"You walked," she said.

"I walked."

"You don't have to."

"I know." He set the jug down. "I want to know when I'm doing it. If I stop noticing the small ones, I won't notice the big ones."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment.

"Then walk," she said. "I like hearing you on the stairs."

She lay back down.

Mau sat on the edge of the bed and listened to her breathe, which was easier now, and to the house below them, which had gone back to being loud.

On the fifth day, as Maomao had promised, Frieren was well.

She came downstairs in his hoodie, ate three bowls of the fifth soup, and announced that she had never been sick, and that everyone who said otherwise was misremembering.

Nobody corrected her.

Not even under rule eight.
