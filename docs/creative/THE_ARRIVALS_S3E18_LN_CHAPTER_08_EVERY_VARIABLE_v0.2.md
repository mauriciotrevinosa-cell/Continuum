# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 14
## Chapter 8 — Every Variable

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 14  
**Reading order:** 8  
**Continuity state:** Packing Day 1 — afternoon | Ciel's first confident prediction fails in four seconds | Spatial and flight leaks under rule four

Ciel spoke first that morning, without being asked.

That was unusual enough that Mau stopped halfway up the pantry ladder.

I have news, said Ciel.

"You're allowed to talk in emergencies," said Mau. "Is this an emergency?"

It is good news. Good news is a kind of emergency.

Below him, Momo held up both hands for the next jar.

"Who are you talking to?"

"Ciel."

"Tell her to talk while you pass jars."

Mau passed a jar.

I have finished reinforcing the spatial guardrail, said Ciel. She sounded, for the first time since Mau had known her, pleased with herself. I have been working on it for six days. I have accounted for every variable. Fatigue. Proximity to Frieren. Emotional load. The distances the pattern prefers. The time of day it tends to press. It will not bend again for weeks. Possibly months.

"Seriously?"

Seriously. You may carry things normally. You may walk to the well without arriving at the well. You may thank Master. A pause. Indirectly, me.

Mau laughed.

He felt lighter than he had in days.

"Thank you," he said. "Really. That's—"

He stepped down off the ladder.

He did not reach the pantry floor.

He reached the roof.

He was standing on the old inn's roof, on the slope above the kitchen, with a jar of pickled radish in one hand and the morning wind in his face, and below him in the yard Stark was splitting wood and had stopped, axe raised, to stare up at him.

Three seconds had passed.

Possibly four.

...Never mind, said Ciel.



"RULE EIGHT," Fern called from the yard. "YOU'RE ON THE ROOF."

"I KNOW."

"COME DOWN THE NORMAL WAY."

"WORKING ON IT."

Stark lowered the axe.

"Do you want the ladder?"

"Yes, please."

Stark went for the ladder.

Mau sat down carefully on the slope of the roof, the jar in his lap, and looked out at the road and the trees and the far brown smudge of the new site, where the first cluster was nearly done.

"Every variable," he said.

Ciel did not answer.

"Weeks. Possibly months. You may thank Master."

I am aware of what I said.

"You sounded exactly like Diablo."

Silence.

Then, with great precision:

I did not.

"You did. 'I have accounted for every variable.' That's a Diablo sentence. That's the most Diablo sentence I've ever heard. You just needed a laugh at the end."

Diablo would have said "every conceivable variable, for Master's sake," said Ciel. And he would have been wrong for longer than three seconds.

"Four."

Three, said Ciel. I am going to stop talking now.

She stopped talking.

Mau sat on the roof and laughed until Stark came back with the ladder.



Rimuru found out by lunch.

Mau did not tell him. He did not have to.

Rimuru came in from the worksite with sawdust on his shoulders, sat down at the long table, took one look at Mau, and put his face in his hands.

"She told you," said Mau.

"She didn't tell me. She's sulking. I asked why she was sulking." Rimuru's shoulders were shaking. "She's never sulked. Raphael didn't sulk. Raphael didn't have anything to sulk with."

"Is she okay?"

"She says she's concerned."

"About what?"

"About the roof." Rimuru wiped his eyes. "She says she is concerned about the roof."

Across the room, Diablo looked up from the cutlery he had been arranging, which nobody had asked him to arrange.

"Forgive me, Rimuru-sama. Did I hear correctly? Ciel-sama has been compared to me?"

"Don't," said Rimuru.

"An extraordinary honour."

Rimuru listened to something nobody else could hear.

"She says to tell you it was not a compliment."

Diablo placed one hand over his heart.

"Kufufufu. Then it is an even greater one."

Ciel, said Rimuru afterward, did not speak to anyone for the rest of the meal.



The afternoon went to the common-room shelves.

Books, mostly. Whatever the inn had been holding before any of them arrived, a few dozen of Frieren's grimoires, and the household's own accumulated nonsense: Umaru's game boxes, Coco's sketchbooks, Marin's pattern folders, a jar of buttons nobody would admit to owning.

The top shelf was the problem.

It had always been the problem. The old inn's common room had been built by someone very tall, or someone who hated whoever came after.

Fern went to get the step-stool.

When she came back, Mau was already up there.

Not on anything.

Just up.

He was hanging a hand's width above the floorboards, very slightly tilted, holding a stack of books against his chest and looking down at his own feet with deep suspicion.

Flight, said Ciel quietly. Frieren's. A small one. I am telling you I looked.

"I thought you weren't talking."

I am obeying rule four. I am not offering an opinion.

"Okay."

I will also not be making predictions.

"Okay."

Fern stood there with the step-stool.

She looked at Mau.

She looked at the top shelf.

She set the step-stool down.

"Since you're up there," she said.

Mau sighed, and began handing down books.

He drifted. He could not stop drifting. Every time he reached for the shelf, he rose a little higher, and every time he lowered his arms, he sank, and once he turned too fast and rotated slowly in place until Ori caught his ankle and turned him back.

Frieren watched the whole thing from the couch with her chin on her hand.

"Your posture is bad," she said.

"I didn't ask to float."

"You float like a sack of flour."

"I'm not trying to float well."

"Clearly."

"Do you want to come up here and do it?"

Frieren considered this.

Then she rose off the couch without any visible effort, drifted up beside him at exactly his height, took the stack of books out of his arms, and handed them down to Fern one by one, perfectly upright, perfectly still.

"Like this," she said.

Mau looked at her.

She looked back.

They hung there side by side, near the ceiling of the room where they had first become a household, two people floating over a pile of books for no reason that would ever go in a report.

Umaru took a picture with her eyes and said so out loud.

The flight ended twenty minutes later, while Mau was eating a biscuit. He sat down on the floor very suddenly.

Nobody made a big thing of it.

Okarun handed him the rest of the biscuit.



That night, Mau lay awake in the old room, in the old bed, with Frieren asleep against his back, and the whole inn creaking around them the way it had creaked every night for as long as he could remember.

Which was not as long as it should have been.

He found that he could think that without it hurting.

That was new.

"Ciel," he said, very quietly.

I'm here.

"How are the memories?"

He asked her that most nights now. It had become a kind of goodnight.

Rethreading, said Ciel. Slowly. It works hardest while you sleep. This week it closed three small breaks and opened none.

"Is that good?"

It is less broken. A pause. It is not truer. I cannot give you truer.

"I know."

He did know. It had stopped frightening him that she couldn't.

"And you? Are you okay?"

A pause.

Why do you ask?

"Because you hold the walls all day. All night. You never sleep. And today you were proud of something, and it fell over in three seconds, and everybody laughed."

Four seconds, said Ciel. You were right. I checked.

"Ciel."

Master laughed, said Ciel.

"Yeah."

He laughed kindly.

"He did."

Another pause. Longer.

Raphael was never wrong in front of anyone, said Ciel. Raphael did not have anyone to be wrong in front of. She seemed to consider her next words carefully, the way she considered everything. I think I would rather be wrong in front of people.

Mau smiled at the dark.

"Welcome to the family."

Is that what this is?

"Pretty much. Being wrong in front of people who don't stop liking you."

Ciel was quiet for so long that he thought she had gone.

Then:

Noted.

Mau closed his eyes.

For the record, said Ciel, the spatial guardrail will hold for at least a day.

"Ciel."

...Probably.
