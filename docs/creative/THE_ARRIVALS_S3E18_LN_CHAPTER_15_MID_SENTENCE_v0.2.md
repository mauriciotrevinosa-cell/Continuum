# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 14
## Chapter 15 — Mid-Sentence

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 14  
**Reading order:** 15  
**Continuity state:** Pre-Move | The Muslin's second section is being raised | Spatial leak to the worksite

"The shelf goes on the north wall," said Mau.

"No," said Frieren.

"The north wall doesn't get light. The books won't fade."

"The north wall is where the bed goes."

"The bed goes under the east window."

"The bed goes where I can see the east window from it," said Frieren, "which is the north wall."

They were at the long table in the old inn, eating late breakfast, with a piece of paper between them on which Mau had drawn, very badly, a square.

The square was their room.

Not the real one. The real house was a drawing nobody would make for months. This was their room in the Muslin, in the second section Rimuru swore would have walls within ten days. It did not have walls yet. It had stakes and string and a corner that a whole building had been turned around to give eastern light.

It had already been argued about more than most rooms that existed.

Mau pointed at the square with his spoon.

"If the bed goes on the north wall, the shelf has to go here, and then the door hits it every time—"

"Then move the door."

"We can't move the door, Wakana already—"

He was gone.

The spoon was gone with him.

Frieren sat at the table with her own spoon halfway to her mouth, looking at the empty chair where he had been a fraction of a second earlier.

The paper with the square on it fluttered in the air he had left behind and settled back onto the table.

Across the room, Momo dropped a plate.



At the worksite, hours away by road, Rimuru was holding a corner post upright while Wakana checked it with a plumb line.

Mau appeared beside them.

Spoon in hand.

Mid-sentence.

"—cut the frame for it."

Wakana dropped the plumb line.

Rimuru did not let go of the post, but it was a near thing.

Mau looked at the post.

At the stakes.

At the string.

At the exact corner, three steps to his left, that a whole building had been turned around to give eastern light.

He looked down at the spoon.

"Oh," he said.

Rimuru stared at him.

"You're early," Rimuru said finally.

"I was at breakfast."

"You're still holding the spoon."

Mau looked at it.

"I was talking about shelves."

Spatial, said Ciel. Master's, this time. Pure. No join. You were thinking about the room so precisely that some part of you decided to go and look at it. The room is here, so you came here. A pause. It was very efficient.

"Was it dangerous?"

No. Your body barely noticed. That is the part I am writing down.

Rimuru had begun, slowly, to laugh.

He held onto the post and laughed until Wakana took it from him, and then he sat down on a stack of timber and kept laughing.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm sorry, it's not funny, it's—"

"It's a little funny," said Wakana, very quietly, retrieving his plumb line.

"I was talking about shelves," Mau said again.

Rimuru wiped his eyes.

"How's breakfast?"

"Good," said Mau. "I think. I didn't finish it."



He did not try to go back the way he had come.

He did not know how. It had not felt like doing anything. It had felt like thinking about a wall.

So he stayed, and worked, and when someone asked him to hold something he set the spoon down carefully on a crate first, and every few minutes he looked at the corner with the eastern light.

Frieren arrived at the worksite a little after noon.

She had walked.

Hours.

The whole way.

She came up the slope with her staff over her shoulder and her face perfectly blank, and everyone who knew her well enough moved slightly out of the way.

She walked straight up to Mau.

Stopped in front of him.

"You left," she said, "in the middle of a sentence."

"I know."

"Finish it."

Mau blinked.

"What?"

"The sentence. You were saying Wakana already—" Frieren waited. "Already what?"

Mau thought back.

The table. The square on the paper. The spoon.

"Wakana already cut the frame for it," he said. "The door. So we can't move the door. So if the bed goes on the north wall, the door hits the shelf."

Frieren considered this.

Then she turned and looked at the corner.

At the stakes, and the string, and the place where the door would be.

She walked over and stood in it.

She looked east.

She looked north.

She looked at the place where, someday, a shelf would be, and a bed, and two people who argued about which wall each one went on.

"The bed goes on the north wall," she said.

"And the shelf?"

Frieren came back and took the spoon off the crate and handed it to him.

"We'll argue about the shelf," she said, "when there's a wall to put it against."

Mau stared at her.

"You walked for hours to tell me that."

"Yes," said Frieren. "And to see the room. You got to see it first. That wasn't fair."

Wakana, who had been listening, quietly wrote BED — NORTH WALL on his board, and underneath it, after a moment's thought, SHELF — DISPUTED.

Rimuru, on his stack of timber, put his face in his hands.

Mau looked at the corner.

At Frieren, standing in it, in the place where the door would be.

At the spoon in his own hand.

He laughed.

He was still laughing when Rimuru drove them both back in the cart that evening, with the spoon in his pocket, because Frieren had refused to let him leave it at the site.

"It's evidence," she said.

"Of what?"

Frieren looked out at the road.

"That you went to look at our room," she said, "before you finished your sentence."

She did not explain why that mattered.

She did not need to.
