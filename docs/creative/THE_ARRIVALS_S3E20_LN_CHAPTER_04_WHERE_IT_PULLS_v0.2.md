# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 14
## Chapter 25 — Where It Pulls

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 14  
**Reading order:** 25  
**Continuity state:** Move Day 3 — evening | The Muslin's first fitting: forty hooks | Mau leaves the narrow room at the turn unmarked

On the third day, Wakana started walking the Muslin with a piece of chalk.

He did not announce it. He simply went from room to room, corridor to corridor, slowly, the way he walked around a person in a half-finished costume, and wherever something was wrong he drew a small mark on the nearest board. Not a word. Just a short line with a hook on the end.

Marin followed him with a notebook.

"What does the hook mean?"

"Pulls."

"What pulls?"

Wakana pointed at a door that, when opened fully, hit the door across from it.

"That."

Marin wrote it down.

By noon there were twenty-three hooks.

By evening there were forty.



Dinner was where the Muslin pulled worst.

Momo had made stew. A lot of stew. Enough stew for a household that had been eating in shifts for three days and was tired of it. She stood in the kitchen doorway with a ladle in one hand and shouted the way she had shouted at the old inn every evening for as long as anyone could remember.

"FOOD!"

At the old inn, that shout had reached every room. The inn was old and small and made of wood that carried sound like a drum.

The Muslin was long.

Half the household arrived within a minute. Fern. Okarun. Aira. Seiko, who had been waiting. Umaru, who had been waiting harder. Frieren, who had not heard the shout at all but had smelled bread twenty minutes earlier and had been sitting at the table ever since with her hands folded, looking innocent. Mau, who had come because Frieren was there.

The east end of the shell did not hear a thing.

Yuta and Maki arrived half an hour later, sweaty from training, cheerful, and stopped in the doorway of the common room to look at an empty pot.

Momo looked at them.

They looked at Momo.

"I yelled," said Momo.

"We didn't hear," said Yuta.

"I YELLED."

"We were at the east end."

"The east end," said Momo, "is not a different country."

Maki looked down the long corridor toward their room. From where she stood, it was a very long corridor.

"It might be," she said.

Senku, at the table, did not look up from his bowl.

"Speaking tube," he said. "Copper, if we can get it. Clay if we can't. One end in the kitchen, one end at the turn."

"Bell," said Fern.

"Bell is less elegant."

"Bell is cheaper."

"I'll yell louder," said Momo.

Everyone said "Bell" at the same time.

Momo glared at all of them, then went to the back of the kitchen and found a second, smaller pot of stew she had hidden for exactly this reason, and gave it to Yuta and Maki without comment.

Wakana drew a hook on the kitchen doorframe.



Ori was the last to arrive at almost everything that week.

Mau noticed on the second day and did not understand it until the third.

On the plan, her room had been across the courtyard from theirs. Twelve steps. She had counted them on the paper with two fingers, the way she counted everything.

The plan had not known about the mud.

The courtyard between the two halves of the Muslin was not a courtyard yet. It was a drainage trench, a pile of spare boards, a puddle that Senku called "a feature under evaluation", and mud. Nobody crossed it. To get from Ori's door to theirs, you went out along the west side of the shell, around the far end where the stakes of the next section stood, and back along the east side.

Forty-one steps.

Ori had counted those too.

She did not complain. She would not have complained if it had been four hundred. She simply arrived a little later than everyone else, every time, with the hem of her dress a little muddier, and sat down a little farther from Mau than she used to.

Mau saw it at dinner, with the empty pot.

He did not say anything yet.

But he went and found Wakana afterward, and stood beside him in front of the board, and pointed at Ori's door on the plan.

Wakana drew a hook on it.



Umaru discovered her problem the same evening.

She had wanted to bring Mau a game. It was a very important game, one she had been saving, and she set out from her room beside the Witch Hat girls' room with the box under her arm, and turned left, and went down the corridor, and around the turn, and past Qifrey's door, and past the narrow room nobody had marked, and past Rimuru's door with Diablo standing outside it like a coat stand, and then she had to go back because she had turned the wrong way at the turn, and then she arrived.

She stood in Mau and Frieren's doorway, breathing hard, holding the game.

"This," she announced, "is a siege."

"It's a corridor," said Mau.

"It's a siege. They've put me behind walls. I have to go past four doors and a demon to reach my own brother." She held up the box. "I came all this way."

Frieren, on the bed, did not look up from her book.

"Then sit down."

Umaru sat down.

They played. Umaru won. She always won.

On her way back, she stopped at the board in the common room, took Wakana's chalk off its ledge, and wrote under the forty hooks, in large and furious letters:

UMARU CANNOT REACH MAU. THIS IS WAR.

Wakana, in the morning, looked at this for a long time.

Then he drew a hook beside it.



Rimuru had not slept in three nights.

This was not, strictly speaking, a problem for a slime. Slimes did not need to sleep. Rimuru had explained this to several people several times, in the tone of someone explaining it mostly to himself.

But he liked to sleep. He had liked to sleep in his old world, where he had been a person with a bed and a job and a bad back. He had liked to sleep in Tempest, on good nights. And he had liked to sleep at the old inn, in the small room under the stairs that nobody else wanted, where nobody knocked.

In the Muslin, people knocked.

He was the builder. Of course they knocked. Where does this board go. Is this wall load-bearing. Can we move the door. Why does the chimney smoke. They knocked at dawn and they knocked at midnight, and when they did not knock, Milim was there.

Milim did not knock. Milim did not need to. Milim had put her marker on his and she had meant it, and every night she arrived in his room, took the side of the bed nearest the wall, fell asleep in about four seconds, and then spent the rest of the night sleeping in every direction at once.

Diablo stood outside the door all night. He said it was to keep people from disturbing Rimuru-sama. It meant that when people came to knock, they found Diablo first and had a long conversation with him in the corridor, in voices that carried perfectly through board walls.

At dinner, Rimuru put his spoon down halfway through his stew, folded his arms on the table, and put his face in them.

"Rimuru?"

"I'm fine."

"You look tired."

"Slimes don't get tired."

"You look like a tired slime."

Rimuru lifted his face just enough to look at Mau with one eye.

"Is there a hook for this?"

Mau looked over at the board.

There was.

Wakana had drawn it that morning. On Rimuru's door.



Bocchi was happy.

This was so unusual, and so visible, that people kept stopping to look at it.

The music room sat exactly where she had wanted it, between her room and everything else. When she played, the sound went into the music room's walls and stopped there. When the corridor got loud, the music room swallowed the noise before it reached her door. When she wanted people, they were twenty steps away. When she didn't, they might as well have been in another settlement.

Kita came to find her after dinner and found her sitting on her own floor, guitar in her lap, not playing, smiling at the wall.

"What's wrong?" said Kita, alarmed.

"Nothing," said Bocchi. "Nothing's wrong. It works."

Kita sat down next to her.

"What works?"

Bocchi gestured vaguely at the wall, the music room beyond it, the door, the corridor, the whole long ridiculous shell.

"Being here," she said. "And being close. At the same time."

Kita looked at her for a moment.

Then she leaned her head on Bocchi's shoulder, and Bocchi went very red, and neither of them said anything else.

There was no hook on Bocchi's door.

Wakana had checked twice.



Agott's curtain went up that evening.

Tetia made it. It was dark green, heavy, and hemmed with a line of tiny silver stars, because Tetia had decided that if Agott was going to have a curtain then it was going to be a curtain worth having. She hung it on a rail across one corner of the Witch Hat girls' room, so that the bed behind it was a room of its own, more or less.

Agott drew it shut as soon as it was up.

She kept it shut all evening, while Coco and Richeh and Tetia talked and argued and laughed on the other side of it.

Late, when the lamp was turned low and the others were half asleep, Tetia heard the rail creak.

She opened one eye.

The curtain had been drawn back a hand's width.

Not more.

Just enough that, from inside, you could see the other three beds.

Tetia closed her eye again and did not say anything, then or ever.



Rem had come that day carrying supplies. She was going back to the old inn tonight. She had said so, plainly, to Fern that morning, and Fern had said "good", and meant it.

Mau walked her to the cart.

On the way out, they passed the turn of the corridor, and the narrow room nobody had marked.

He did not look at it.

He was very careful not to look at it.

Rem climbed onto the cart. She looked down at him.

"Thank you for dinner."

"Momo made it."

"Thank you for the walk, then."

He stood and watched the cart go down the road toward the old inn until it was too dark to see.

When he came back, Ori was standing at the turn of the corridor, beside the narrow room, with the muddy hem of her dress and her arms folded.

"You want her to stay," said Ori.

"She took care of me." Mau leaned against the wall. "When I couldn't. When I didn't know what was real. She was there every day. She never asked for anything."

"I know," said Ori. "I was there."

"I want her to stay."

"Then don't ask her."

Mau looked at her.

"What?"

"If you ask," said Ori, "she'll say yes for you. She'll stay because you wanted it." She looked at the narrow room, its door ajar, its floor bare. "Leave it empty. Let her find it. Let her say yes for herself."

Mau was quiet for a long moment.

"When did you get so smart?"

"I've always been smart," said Ori. "You just had worse memory."

He laughed, and it surprised him how much it didn't hurt.



By the end of the third day, Wakana's board said this, in his small neat writing, under the forty-one hooks:

Kitchen cannot be heard from the east end.

Courtyard is mud. Ori walks 41 steps. (Should be 12.)

Umaru cannot reach Mau. (Umaru's note: THIS IS WAR.)

Rimuru cannot sleep.

Agott's curtain: fine. (Agott's note, very small: it is fine.)

Bocchi: no hook.

Wakana stood in front of it with his chalk and read it over twice.

"Good," he said.

Marin looked at him.

"Good?"

"Now we know where to cut," said Wakana.
