# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 14
## Chapter 29 — Rimuru's Left Hand

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 14  
**Reading order:** 29  
**Continuity state:** Move Day 5 — dawn, then breakfast | Rimuru and Mau on the roof | Household writes the shadow protocol | Scratches kept under varnish

Neither of them went back to bed.

Frieren did, eventually, because Mau asked her to and because she could see he meant it. Yuta stayed in the common room with every lamp burning and a blanket over his knees, not on watch, exactly. Just there.

Mau and Rimuru went up onto the roof.

The common room roof was one of the finished ones. Mostly. Wakana had declared the eastern half sound and the western half "sound if you are not Milim", and they sat on the eastern half, on the new boards, with their backs against the chimney that still drew badly, and watched the dark over the settlement begin, very slowly, to thin.

Rimuru was flexing his left hand.

He did it absently. Open, closed. Open, closed. The fingers spreading wide and curling in, one at a time, the way you would test a tool you had not used in a while.

Mau had seen him do it before.

He had never asked.

"Does it feel like yours?"

Rimuru looked down at the hand as if he had not noticed it moving.

"It is mine," he said. "It's the same hand. Same shape. Same everything. Ciel checked it about four hundred times." He closed it slowly. "It just remembers being gone."

"What does that feel like?"

Rimuru thought about it.

"Like reaching for something and being a little late," he said. "Not slow. Late. As if part of me still thinks there's nothing on that side to reach with, and has to be reminded." He shrugged. "It's getting better. I don't notice it when I'm working. Only when I'm sitting still."

"I'm sor—"

"No."

Mau stopped.

"You've apologized for the arm eleven times," said Rimuru. "I've been counting. Ciel's been counting. Eleven is the limit."

"Is that a rule?"

"It is now."

Mau looked at the hand.

Then he looked away, out over the roofs, and let the twelfth apology go unsaid. It took more effort than he expected.



"Can I ask you something?" said Rimuru.

"Yes."

"Last time Ciel told you something was wrong, you ran."

Mau remembered. The jar of salt. The kitchen. The wicks scattering across the floor. The road.

"She told me to get away from people."

"She did. And she was right to. She didn't know what was coming." Rimuru tipped his head back against the chimney. "Tonight she didn't tell you to run. She didn't tell you to stay, either. She told you not to move and not to name it, and then she closed it, and then you sat on the floor and asked her to tell me."

"Yes."

"You stayed."

Mau did not answer.

"You could have gone out the door," said Rimuru. "Walked until there was nobody around you. That's what you'd have done a month ago. That's what you did, more than once, back when you thought the most useful thing you could do with something dangerous in you was to take it somewhere nobody could get hurt and deal with it alone."

"I thought about it."

"I know. Ciel said."

"Ciel is supposed to watch the guardrails."

She watched your feet, said Ciel. Your feet are close to the guardrails.

Rimuru smiled without opening his eyes.

"You sat down," he said. "That's all I wanted to say. I've watched you get up and walk away from people for as long as I've known you. Tonight you sat down on the floor and asked for help, and you waited for it to come."

Mau looked at his own hands.

"It felt worse," he said. "Staying. It felt so much worse than leaving."

"Yeah," said Rimuru. "It does. That's how you know it's the right one, most of the time." He opened his eyes. "Not always. Most of the time."



The sky was gray now. Then pale.

Somewhere below them, Momo's kitchen door opened and closed, and a moment later the first smell of bread came up through the chimney, which drew badly but not, it turned out, in that direction.

"Can I ask you something?" said Mau.

"Fair's fair."

"Is it heavy? Holding it?"

Rimuru was quiet for a while.

"Not heavy," he said. "Loud. Not loud like noise. Loud like a room you know someone's in." He turned his left hand over. "I held Veldora for two years, back in my world. Inside me. You'd think that would be worse. A True Dragon. But he talked. He complained. He read manga I'd remembered for him and argued with me about the endings." He smiled at something far away. "He was a friend in there. You know what I mean? It was company."

"And mine?"

"Yours doesn't talk." Rimuru said it gently. "It just works. All the time. Building, inside the walls, because it doesn't know how to do anything else. It's not angry. It's not anything. It's just busy." He paused. "That's the part I find hard. Not that it's dangerous. That it doesn't know it's being held."

Mau thought about the muzzle breaking the surface of his shadow. The single eye. The breath on his ankles.

"It knew tonight," he said. "It looked at me."

"Yeah," said Rimuru quietly. "I know. That's new too."

They sat with that.

The first edge of the sun came up over the trees to the east, and lit the roofs, and the stakes and string of the unbuilt wing, and the window of the room where Frieren was sleeping.

Rimuru held up his left hand against it, fingers spread, and looked at the light coming through the gaps.

"Mine," he said.

"Yours," said Mau.



He told them at breakfast.

All of it, the way he had promised Frieren. He told it standing up, because he could not sit still to tell it, and he told it plainly, the hole and the cold and the muzzle and the word behind his teeth, and Yuta told the part about Ten Shadows, and Rimuru told the part Ciel had told him, including the sentence about the part she did not like.

Nobody interrupted.

When he finished, the common room was very quiet.

Then Mikasa said, "What do we do if it happens again?"

Not frightened. Practical. The way she said everything that mattered.

"I don't know," said Mau.

"Then we decide." Mikasa looked around the table. "Now. While nobody's frightened yet."

So they decided.

It took an hour, and it was not elegant, and Fern wrote it down.

If his shadow went wrong, nobody said anything to it. Nobody named anything. Nobody shaped their hands, even by accident, even to point. Whoever saw it called Rimuru, or Yuta, or Ciel through Mau, and then got everyone else out of the room. Children first. Nobody stood between Mau and the thing unless Rimuru said to, and Rimuru would not say to.

Momo listened to all of it, and then said, "So we need more lamps."

Everyone looked at her.

"Shadows come from lamps," said Momo. "If the light's everywhere, the shadows are short. Short shadows, short holes." She shrugged. "We needed more lamps anyway. The corridor's a deathtrap."

Senku opened his mouth, closed it, and wrote something down.

"Lamps," said Fern, and wrote that down too.

Anko reminded everyone, unnecessarily but firmly, of rule seven.

Nobody outside the household. Not a word.

Wakana came to Mau afterward, very quietly, and asked about the floor.

"I can sand it," he said. "Or replace the boards. It's no trouble."

"Can you leave it?"

Wakana looked at him for a moment.

Then he nodded, and went away, and came back an hour later with a pot of varnish. He knelt on the common-room floor and sealed the four pale scratches under a clear, hard coat, so that they would not splinter or darken or wear away under a hundred people's feet. They would stay exactly as they were, where anyone could see them.

Kept, counted and held.

Mau stood and watched him do it.

He thought, not for the first time, that this was a very strange house to have ended up in, and a very good one.
