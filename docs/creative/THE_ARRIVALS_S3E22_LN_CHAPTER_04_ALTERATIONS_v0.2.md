# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 15
## Chapter 4 — Alterations

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 15  
**Reading order:** 4  
**Continuity state:** Ordinary Week 1 | Alteration Day: bells, boardwalk, Ori moves next door | Milim leaves Rimuru's room for the end bay | Umaru declares war

Wakana called it Alteration Day.

Marin, who had cancelled construction three days earlier, tried to cancel it too, and failed, because Wakana pointed out very quietly that it was not construction. It was tailoring. Nothing new was being built. Things that already existed were being made to fit.

Marin considered this for a long moment and then said, "Fine, but I'm supervising," which was what she had wanted all along.

The board in the common room had forty-one hooks on it.

By the end of the day, it had nineteen.



The bell went up first.

It was a small brass bell from the old inn's stable, polished by Momo until it embarrassed the rest of the kitchen, and it hung on a bracket outside the kitchen door with a cord that ran all the way down the corridor to the turn.

When Momo pulled the cord in the kitchen, the bell rang in the kitchen.

When anyone at the turn heard it, they pulled the second cord, which Senku had insisted on, and which rang a second bell at the east end.

"This is a relay," said Senku with satisfaction.

"It's two bells," said Fern.

"It's a relay of two bells."

Momo tested it. Both bells rang. At the east end, Yuta and Maki appeared in their doorway, looked down the corridor, and waved.

Momo nodded once, grimly, as if a war had ended.

Senku built the speaking tube anyway. Clay, because there was no copper. It ran from the kitchen wall to the turn and it did not work at all, except that if you put your ear to the end at the turn you could hear Momo humming while she cooked, faintly, as if from very far away.

Nobody took it down.

Bocchi, it turned out, liked to sit near it.



The courtyard got a boardwalk.

Senku's "feature under evaluation" was formally evaluated, found to be a puddle, and bridged with spare boards on short posts so that people could cross it without going around. Twelve steps, from one side to the other.

It solved the mud.

It did not solve Ori.

Mau had thought it would, for about a day. Then he watched her cross the boardwalk at dinner, perfectly on time, and sit down at the far end of the table from him, and he understood that the forty-one steps had never been the real distance.

He did not know how to say that.

Frieren did.

She did not say it either. She went and got a board.

She came back to the east end of the shell with the board under her arm and stopped in front of the narrow bay beside their own room, the one on the side away from Bocchi's, where all the spare boards and posts had been stacked since the second section went up. It was not a room. It had never been on the plan. It was a gap the builders had filled with whatever was left over.

Frieren looked at it.

Then she walked across the boardwalk, all twelve steps, and knocked on Ori's door.

Mau, watching from their doorway, could not hear what she said. It was not long. Ori said something back. Frieren said one more thing, shorter.

Then she came back across the boardwalk, alone, and started lifting boards out of the bay and stacking them in the corridor.

"What did you say?" said Mau.

"That there's a room next to ours full of boards," said Frieren, "and that someone needs to move them."

"That's it?"

"And that forty-one steps was too many."

"It's twelve now."

Frieren lifted another board.

"It was always too many," she said.

Ori arrived twenty minutes later with her things in her arms and her face very carefully doing nothing at all. She did not thank anybody. She simply started helping Frieren stack the boards, and when the bay was empty she stood in the middle of it and turned around once, slowly.

On the other side of the wall was the bed where Mau and Frieren slept.

"I'll hear you snore," she said to Mau.

"I don't snore."

"You do," said Frieren and Ori, together.

Ori moved in that night.

Wakana erased the hook on her old door and did not draw a new one on the new one.



Rimuru was a harder problem.

Everyone knew the problem. The problem had a name and pink hair and was the strongest being anyone in the settlement had ever met, and she was, at that moment, sitting on Rimuru's bed swinging her legs while Rimuru stood in the doorway trying to find a way to say something that did not hurt her.

Several people had offered to say it for him.

He had refused all of them.

"Milim."

"Mm?"

"I can't sleep."

Milim stopped swinging her legs.

"Because of me?"

"Because of everything. People knocking. Diablo in the corridor. And—" Rimuru made himself say it. "And you, yes. A bit. You sleep in every direction. Last night you were across my face."

Milim looked at him.

For a moment, she looked exactly as old as she was, which was very old. Then she looked away.

"Where Rimuru sleeps, I sleep," she said, but quietly, and not like law.

"I know."

"In my world," said Milim, to the wall, "I slept alone for a very long time."

Rimuru came in and sat down beside her on the bed.

He did not say anything clever. He had learned, over a long time and in two worlds, when not to.

They sat there.

Mau had come down the corridor to ask Rimuru something about the second bell, and had stopped at the door, and was standing very still, not sure if he should leave.

Milim saw him.

She looked at him for a long moment, at the end of the corridor behind him, and at the small room at the very end of the shell, past his own door: the little end bay, hardly wider than a bed, that shared one wall with his and Frieren's room and had a window looking straight back up the corridor toward Rimuru's door.

Somebody had written UMARU on its door in chalk.

Somebody else, in much smaller letters, had written *(provisional)* underneath.

"That one," said Milim.

Rimuru looked.

"That's tiny."

"I can see your door from it."

"Milim—"

"And Mau's next to it. Rimuru's brother is next to it." She stood up. She was already more cheerful, the way Milim always was a second after deciding anything. "If Rimuru won't let me sleep where he sleeps, I'll sleep next to Rimuru's brother, where I can see Rimuru's door. That's fair."

Rimuru opened his mouth.

He closed it again.

It was, he realized, extremely fair.

"Yes," he said. "Okay. Yes."

Milim picked up her pillow and walked down the corridor, past Mau, past Mau's door, and into the tiny end bay, and threw the pillow on the floor, and lay down on it, and looked up at the ceiling, and smiled.

"It's good," she announced.

Mau looked at the chalk on the door.

UMARU.

*(provisional)*

"Oh no," said Mau.



Umaru found out at dinner.

Both bells rang. She arrived at the table with the rest of the east end, and she was halfway through her bowl before Coco, beside her, mentioned in passing that Milim had moved, and where.

Umaru put her spoon down.

She turned, very slowly, and looked down the table at Milim, who was eating her third helping with enormous satisfaction and waved.

"That," said Umaru, "is MINE."

"It said provisional," said Milim.

"Provisional means MINE UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE."

"This is further notice," said Milim cheerfully.

Umaru stood up on the bench.

Fern said "Get down" without looking up.

Umaru did not get down.

"I," she announced, to the entire common room, "declare war."

Milim's face lit up like a sunrise.

"Really?"

"Really."

"A WAR?"

"A war."

Milim jumped up onto the bench at the other end of the table.

"I ACCEPT."

Fern put her bowl down.

Rimuru put his head in his hands.

Mau, sitting between them all, looked at Frieren.

Frieren went on eating.

"This is your fault," she said to him.

"How is this my fault?"

"Everyone wants to be next to you," said Frieren. "That has always been the problem with this house."

She did not sound like she thought it was a problem.
