# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 15
## Chapter 6 — The Appointment

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 15  
**Reading order:** 6  
**Continuity state:** Ordinary Week 2 | Missed witch appointment at the old inn | First asked-for spatial leak | Two hours owed

The witches came to the old inn on the agreed day, at the agreed bell, every second week, the way they always did.

They had never once been late.

They had never once been early, either, which Fern found more unsettling than lateness, because it meant they were waiting somewhere just out of sight until the exact moment arrived.

Fern was at the old inn that morning only because it was the day she did the old inn's inventory. One day a week, since the Move. She walked over at dawn with a ledger and a list, counted what remained, checked the roof, checked the well, drank one cup of tea with Rem in the kitchen, and walked back. It was a ritual she had invented for practical reasons. She had stopped pretending, privately, that the reasons were only practical.

She was in the pantry counting jars when the knock came.

She knew the knock.

She put the ledger down.

When she opened the door, there they were on the step, as composed as ever, as strange as ever, looking past her into the inn the way they always looked into rooms, as if the walls were a suggestion.

"Good morning," said the first witch.

"He's not here," said Fern.

The first witch tilted her head.

"Today we had an appointment."

"He moved."

"When?"

"Two weeks ago. The whole household moved. To the new site."

The witches looked at one another.

"He did not move the appointment," said the first witch.

Fern opened her mouth.

Closed it.

She thought about Mau, two weeks ago, buried in boxes and room markers and arguments about shelves, and Mau the week after, learning a new building's sounds, and Mau since, and she understood, with a sinking certainty, that nobody had told the witches anything, because the one person whose job it was to tell them had completely forgotten they existed.

"No," said Fern. "He didn't."



Rimuru walked into the middle of this carrying a pot.

He had come back for it on Momo's orders. It was her second-best pot, and she had discovered that morning that it was still at the old inn, and she had sent him for it with the kind of threat that did not need to be specific.

He came in through the kitchen, pot under one arm, saw the witches on the front step, saw Fern's face, and stopped.

"Ah," said Rimuru.

"They had an appointment," said Fern.

"Ah," said Rimuru again.

The first witch looked at him with mild interest, the way she looked at most things.

"You are the one who holds the walls."

"One of them."

"Is he unwell?"

"No," said Rimuru. "He's fine. He's at the new site. He just—" He looked at Fern. Fern looked back. "He forgot."

The witches received this in perfect silence.

Rimuru set the pot down very carefully on the kitchen table.

"Give me a moment," he said.



At the new site, Mau was on the bed under the east window, reading.

Frieren was beside him, reading something else, her feet in his lap. It was the middle of the morning, there was nothing that needed doing that someone else was not already doing, and the light through the window was exactly the light she had turned a whole building to get.

Mau, said Ciel.

"Mm."

Master asks me to tell you that the witches are at the old inn.

Mau turned a page.

Then he stopped turning it.

They say you had an appointment.

Mau sat up so fast that Frieren's feet slid off his lap.

"Oh no."

Frieren looked at him.

"Oh no," said Mau again. "Oh no. The witches. It's the— it's today. It's today. I didn't tell them we moved. I didn't tell them anything. I completely—"

He stopped.

Hours by road. The cart was at the old inn with Rimuru. Even if he ran, even if he borrowed someone's horse, the bell was already past.

He looked at Frieren.

He did not say anything.

He did not have to. She had been reading his face for a long time now, longer than he had been able to read it himself, and she saw exactly what he was thinking, and he saw her see it.

She was quiet for a moment.

Then she put her book down.

"Just this once," said Frieren.

Mau stared at her.

"Then you come back with Rimuru, in the cart, like a person," she said. "And you tell him I said that."



I cannot do this for you, said Ciel. I want that understood. I hold the walls. I do not open them.

"I know."

This is not the seal. This is something your body has already learned to carry. I will not stop you. A pause. I will not help you either.

"Okay."

Master says yes, said Ciel, with audible disapproval. Master also says you are an idiot. I am relaying that accurately.

Mau sat on the edge of the bed and closed his eyes.

He thought about the old inn.

Nothing happened.

He thought about it harder. The roof. The kitchen. The long table. The road up to the door. He thought about it until his jaw ached, and something behind his left eye began to burn, and still nothing happened.

You are pushing, said Ciel. It does not come when it is pushed. It has never once come when it was pushed.

"Then how—"

You were not pushing when you went to the worksite. You were thinking about the room so precisely that part of you went to look at it.

Mau opened his eyes.

Frieren was watching him.

"Precisely," he said. "I need to think about it precisely. Not the inn. One place."

"Which place?"

"Where I meet them."

Frieren knew where. Everyone knew where. Upstairs, the second door, the small room at the end that nobody slept in anymore.

She sat down beside him on the bed.

She did not touch him.

She said, quietly, as if she were reading it off the inside of her own eyelids:

"Upstairs. The second door. It sticks when it rains, so you lift it a little as you push. The floor creaks two steps in, on the left. There's a chair by the window that wobbles because Umaru stood on it to hang a paper lantern and never admitted it. The room smells like the herbs Maomao used to dry in there before she took the cellar. In the morning the light comes in sideways and lies across the floor in a long stripe, and the dust moves in it." She paused. "You always sit in the wobbling chair. You say it's fine. It isn't."

Mau closed his eyes.

The door that stuck.

The creak, two steps in.

The stripe of light across the boards.

The dust.

The chair rocking under him, very slightly, every time he leaned forward to answer a question.

He went.



The room was exactly as she had described it.

The light lay across the floor in a long stripe and the dust moved in it. The chair by the window rocked once, very gently, as if he had just stood up from it.

He was standing beside it.

His left eye was open and burning, and his head felt as if someone had wrung it out, and his legs were not entirely sure of the floor. But he was there.

He stood still and breathed until his legs agreed.

Then he went to the door, lifted it a little as he pushed, and went downstairs.



They were all in the front room. The witches by the door. Fern with her arms folded. Rimuru leaning against the kitchen doorframe with Momo's second-best pot under his arm again, as if he expected to need to leave quickly. Rem, holding her blue-rimmed cup, who had come out of the kitchen when she heard the stair.

Everyone looked up at him.

The first witch looked at him longest.

"You are late," she said.

"I moved."

"You forgot."

Mau opened his mouth to argue.

Then he closed it.

"I forgot," he said.

The first witch regarded him for a long moment.

The sessions had been an hour since the second week after the containment. Mau had raised them from thirty minutes himself, without being asked, and he had never fully explained why, except that it turned out he liked being asked questions by people who were not afraid of the answers.

"Instead of an hour," said the first witch, "you owe us two."

"Okay," said Mau.

"Mau," said Fern.

"It's fine."

"You look like you're going to fall over."

"I'll sit down," said Mau. "In the wobbly chair."



The witches did not ask about the old things first.

They asked about the new thing.

They had felt him arrive. All of them. One moment the house had been empty above their heads and the next it had not, and there had been no stair, no door, no road, just a fold, a seam opening and closing in the shape of a man.

"You came through the fold," said the first witch.

"Yes."

"Did you choose it?"

Mau thought about that honestly, because they always asked honestly, and it was one of the things he had come to respect about them.

"No," he said. "I asked it."

The witches went very still.

"And it came?"

"This time."

One of the others wrote something down and underlined it twice.

They asked him what asking felt like. He told them: like describing a room to someone who wasn't listening, over and over, until suddenly they were. They asked whether it would come again. He said he didn't know. They asked whether he wanted it to.

He thought about the spoon. The jar. The roof. The four scratches varnished into the common-room floor at the new site.

"Some of it," he said. "Not all of it."

They asked which.

He told them that was not their question.

The first witch smiled the small, pleased smile she gave whenever he drew one of those lines and held it.

At the end of the first hour, she looked at his face, at his open eye, at his hand gripping the arm of the wobbly chair.

"And the eye?"

"Still not your question."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

They took their second hour. Exactly their second hour. Not a minute more. When the bell rang below, the first witch closed her book in the middle of a sentence and stood.

At the door, she paused.

"Next time," she said, "tell us where you live."

Fern handed Mau the ledger and a pencil without a word.

He wrote it down.



Rimuru drove them back in the cart in the late afternoon, with Momo's second-best pot wedged between their feet.

Mau lay in the back for the first hour, eyes closed, Marin's dark blue patch over the left one, letting the road rock him. His head had stopped wringing itself out somewhere around the halfway mark of the second witch hour. The eye had closed on its own a little after that.

When he sat up, Rimuru glanced back at him.

"Alive?"

"Mostly."

They rode for a while.

"Frieren said 'just this once,'" said Mau eventually. "And to tell you she said that."

Rimuru laughed out loud.

It startled the horse.

"She knew I'd be mad," he said, when he had the horse settled again.

"Are you?"

Rimuru thought about it.

"I'm mad you didn't ask me first," he said. "I would have said yes."

"You did say yes. Ciel told me."

"I said yes and that you were an idiot."

"She told me that too."

For the record, said Ciel, to both of them at once, I advised against it.

"You did not," said Rimuru. "You didn't say a word."

I advised against it quietly.

"That's not advising. That's having an opinion."

It was a very strong opinion.

Mau leaned back against the side of the cart.

"You're doing the Diablo thing again."

I am not.

"She is," said Rimuru.

Ciel did not dignify this.

The road went on. The trees went by. Ahead of them, slowly, the brown smudge of the settlement grew into roofs and smoke and the half-built walls of the wing, and somewhere in it there was a room with an east window where Frieren was waiting to hear how it went.

"It came when I asked," said Mau quietly.

Rimuru did not look back this time.

"It came when you thought very hard about a wobbly chair," he said.

"Same thing?"

No, said Ciel. But close enough to be interesting.

Rimuru was quiet for a long moment.

"Don't make that a plan," he said.

Mau looked out at the road.

"Not yet," he said.

Rimuru did not answer that.

But he did not argue with it, either.
