# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 15
## Chapter 7 — Flowers, Again

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 15  
**Reading order:** 7  
**Continuity state:** Ordinary Week 2 — three days later | The clearing | One guardrail opened for nineteen minutes | Kept from the household

The clearing was smaller than Mau remembered.

It always was.

They reached it a little after dawn, the three of them, Rimuru carrying a blanket and a flask he had insisted on, Frieren carrying nothing but her staff, Mau carrying the feeling that he was about to do something he could not take back and the knowledge that he could.

Frost was lifting off the grass.

Exactly as it had been through the door.

Mau stopped at the edge of it.

Frieren stopped beside him.

"Here," she said, and pointed, without looking, at a place in the middle of the clearing that looked like every other place.

"You remember exactly?"

"You were unconscious," said Frieren. "I wasn't."

He went and lay down in it.

Not because anyone told him to.

Because it felt like the right way to begin.

The grass was cold through his shirt. Above him, the trees made the same shape against the sky that they had made the first morning, when he had opened his eyes and the first thing in the world had been white hair.

Frieren sat down in the grass beside his head.

Rimuru sat a little way off, close enough to touch, far enough not to crowd.

"Ciel," said Mau.

I'm here.

"Open it."

One guardrail, said Ciel. The elf route. Only that one. I am going to open it very slowly. If you feel anything wrong, say so. If Frieren says stop, I close it. If I see cross-feedback, I close it before either of you says anything. Agreed?

"Agreed," said Mau.

"Agreed," said Frieren, though Ciel could not hear her except through Rimuru, who repeated it.

Opening.



At first nothing happened.

Then warmth.

Not fever. Not yet. A slow, even warmth, spreading outward from somewhere behind his breastbone, the way sunlight spreads across a floor.

Then his ears.

A tingling at the very tips, faint, like the moment before a sneeze.

Frieren saw something.

She did not say what.

She put her hand on his chest, flat, over the warmth, and kept it there.

Two minutes, said Ciel. Stable.

Then the world opened.

Mau did not have another word for it.

One moment there was a clearing, and grass, and trees, and the sky.

The next, there was all of that, and underneath it, threaded through it, there was something else. A faint, living light in everything. Thin in the grass. Deeper in the trees, slow and old, moving in their trunks like water moves in a river. A bright cold thread in the frost as it lifted.

Mana.

He had never felt it.

He had watched it his whole life in this world. Watched Frieren use it, Fern use it, Qifrey draw with it, Coco argue about it. He had built theories about it. He had never once felt it, the way you never feel the air until the wind blows.

Now the wind was blowing.

He turned his head.

Frieren was sitting beside him, and she was almost nothing.

A tiny, quiet flame.

So small. So carefully kept small. The way she always kept it, the way she had kept it for a thousand years, so that no enemy would ever look at her and see what she was.

"You're so quiet," Mau whispered.

Frieren went very still.

"You can see it?"

"You're so quiet," he said again. "You're making yourself so small."

"On purpose," said Frieren.

Her voice was not steady.

"I know," said Mau. "I know. I just didn't know it looked like that."

Eight minutes, said Ciel. Stable. Fever beginning. Slow.



At fourteen minutes, Mau sat up.

Carefully. Rimuru's hand was there before he needed it. He did not need it.

"Can I try something?" he said.

Frieren knew what he meant before he said it.

He could see her know it.

"Yes," she said.

Mau held out his hand over the grass.

He had tried this before.

Twice.

Once alone, the first time he came back here, kneeling in the cold, holding his hand over the grass the way he had seen her hold hers, and nothing had happened, and something in his head had scraped out a fragment that sounded like the word form, and he had told it, out loud, that it was not helpful.

Once with Rika watching, and nothing again, and a scratch on his arm on the way home.

He had never told anyone why it mattered.

He did not entirely know.

The first beautiful thing he remembered had happened here.

He wanted, once, to be the one who made it.

He did not copy her gesture.

He had learned, a long time ago, that the gesture was not the point.

He thought about the intention instead.

Not flower.

Not form.

Not create.

Something smaller.

A frightened man in the grass, who did not know his own name, and a woman who did not know what was wrong with him, lifting her hand anyway, so that he would have something to look at that was not empty.

Here. Look. You are not alone in this.

That.

The warmth in his chest moved down his arm.

He felt it go.

It went out of his palm and into the grass, and the grass took it the way dry earth takes rain.

And a flower opened.

Small.

White.

Then another, beside it. Blue. Then three more. Then a cluster, spreading out from under his hand through the cold green, color pushing up through frost, exactly the way it had spread that first morning, until the place where he had woken was a ring of small bright flowers, and he was sitting in the middle of it, and so was she.

Nobody said anything.

Rimuru had his hand over his mouth.

Frieren was not moving at all.

She was looking at the flowers.

Then at him.

Her face did nothing.

Then it did everything.

She did not make a sound.

Tears simply ran down her face, steadily, as if they had been waiting a very long time behind a door, and she did not wipe them, and she did not look away from him.

That is not a sealed pattern, said Ciel, very quietly, into Mau's head. Her voice sounded strange. That is not anything you copied. That is your own mana. From the route. Used once. By you.

Mau looked at his hand.

"I did that," he said.

Yes.

"Me."

Yes.

He started to laugh.

Not loudly. Not for long.

Just once, a breath of disbelief, and Frieren reached out and took his hand, the one that had done it, and held it against her face.



At nineteen minutes, the ground tilted.

Not the ground.

Him.

The clearing slid sideways, gently, the way a boat lists, and Mau put his free hand down into the flowers to stop himself falling and his fingertips went soft.

Not numb.

Soft.

Slime-soft. Yielding. The edges of his fingers losing their argument with being fingers.

Behind his left eye, heat.

Along his back, pressure.

Cross-feedback, said Ciel, sharp. The other routes are waking. They want what this one has. Closing.

"Close it," said Rimuru.

"Stop," said Frieren, in the same instant, and she did not let go of his hand.

Closing. Now.

The warmth went out of him like a tide going out.

The mana went with it. The light in the trees, the thread in the frost, the tiny careful flame beside him that was Frieren, all of it dimmed, and dimmed, and was gone, and the clearing was only a clearing again, grass and trees and sky.

His fingers firmed.

The heat behind his eye faded.

The pressure along his back let go.

Arrival-state, said Ciel. Returning. Stable. Mau, you are going to—

He fainted.

He did not hear the end of her sentence.

He went over sideways into the flowers he had made, and Frieren caught his head before it touched the ground, and Rimuru caught the rest of him, and for a moment the three of them were tangled together in a ring of small bright blooms in the middle of the clearing where it had all started.



He woke in the old inn, in the old room, in the old bed, with a fever that was already breaking.

Frieren was sitting beside him.

Of course she was.

"How long?" he said.

"Five hours."

"Is it bad?"

"No." Frieren pressed a cool cloth against his forehead. "Ciel says it was the body slamming a door. It's tired. It'll be fine by morning." She paused. "It was fine by morning."

Mau looked at the window.

It was morning.

"Oh," he said.

He tried to sit up.

Frieren put one finger on his chest and pushed him back down.

"Nineteen minutes," said Rimuru, from the chair by the wall. He looked like he had not slept. "Ciel wrote everything down. Stable for nineteen minutes. Fever started at eight. You could sense mana from two minutes. You could use it once, deliberately, at fourteen. Cross-feedback at nineteen." He let out a long breath. "Nothing lethal. Nothing that didn't close when we closed it."

"So it works."

"It works for nineteen minutes," said Rimuru. "And then it doesn't. That's not nothing. It's also not a life."

Mau was quiet.

"It's a beginning," he said.

Rimuru looked at him for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "It's a beginning."



Maomao found out that afternoon.

They had planned to tell her.

They did not get the chance.

She walked into the room at the old inn with a basket, took one look at Mau in bed with a fading fever, took one look at Frieren's face and Rimuru's face, and set the basket down very, very slowly.

"What," she said, "did you do."

They told her.

All of it.

Rule five, Mau said afterward. It applies to Maomao too. He decided that on the spot.

Maomao listened to the entire account in perfect silence.

When they finished, she stood there for a long time.

Then she said, very calmly:

"You opened a route in his body that nearly killed him at the containment, in a field, hours from my medicine, without telling me."

"Yes," said Rimuru.

"Without telling anyone."

"Yes," said Frieren.

"Because you did not want the house to hope."

"Yes," said Mau.

Maomao looked at each of them in turn.

"Next time," she said, "I am there."

All three of them stared at her.

"Next time?" said Mau.

"Obviously there is going to be a next time," said Maomao. "Look at your faces. You three are idiots. You are going to do it again whatever I say." She picked the basket back up. "I would rather be there when you are idiots than find out afterward."

She went to the door.

Stopped.

Without turning around, she said:

"Did it work?"

Frieren reached into the folds of her coat.

She took out her grimoire, opened it to a page near the back, and held it out.

Pressed flat between the pages, still faintly colored, was a small white flower.

Maomao looked at it for a long time.

She did not say anything.

She nodded, once.

Then she left, and closed the door behind her very gently, and they heard her stop on the stairs for a while before she went on down.



They did not tell the house.

Not that week.

Not for a long time.

Fern noticed that Mau slept late one morning at the old inn and asked why, and Frieren said he had stayed up reading, and Fern looked at her for a moment and let it go.

Ori noticed that Frieren had started carrying her grimoire everywhere, even to meals, even to the bath once, and did not ask.

And Mau noticed, over the following days, that sometimes, for no reason, when the light came through the east window in the morning, he would look at Frieren asleep beside him and remember, very exactly, how small and quiet and carefully kept she had looked when he could see.

He did not need to check that memory.

He knew it was real.

He had made flowers in it.
