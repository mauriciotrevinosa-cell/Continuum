# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 15
## Chapter 7 — One Door at a Time

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 15  
**Reading order:** 7  
**Continuity state:** Ordinary Week 2 | Nineteen quiet days | Mau, Rimuru and Frieren decide a controlled elf-route trial

Mau woke up feeling good.

Not better.

Good.

It took him a while to recognize it, because it had been so long. He lay in the bed under the east window, on the north wall, in the room that finally had walls, and listened to the settlement waking up below, and waited for the catch.

There was no catch.

No headache.

No heat behind the left eye.

No pressure along his back.

He checked, the way he had learned to check, and then he asked, the way he had promised to.

"Ciel."

Good morning.

"How are the guardrails?"

Quiet, said Ciel. Nothing has come through on its own in nineteen days. The pattern is still working inside. I can feel it. But it is working slowly, the way a person hums while doing something else.

"Nineteen days."

Yes.

"The witches?"

You asked for that one. I do not count what you ask for.

"Is that good?"

It is the longest it has ever been, said Ciel. I think your body has learned to carry most of what leaked. I think it is waiting to see what comes next.

Mau lay there a while longer.

Frieren was asleep beside him, half on her stomach, one arm flung across his chest as if to stop him leaving in the night.

He looked at her for a long time.

Then he very carefully lifted her arm, set it down on the warm place he had left, and went to find Rimuru.



Rimuru was down by the lake, sitting on the flat rocks with a ledger he was not reading.

He looked up when Mau sat down beside him.

"You look awake."

"I feel awake."

Rimuru studied him.

"That's new."

"Nineteen days," said Mau. "Ciel says."

"She told me." Rimuru closed the ledger. "You came down here at dawn to tell me something I already know?"

"No."

Mau looked at the water.

It was the same lake. Not the old one by the inn, the one where the door had opened. A different one. Wider. Theirs.

"We've been holding it closed," he said. "The whole thing. Since the containment. Ciel holds it, and it presses, and it leaks, and my body catches whatever falls out and learns to carry it." He picked up a flat stone and turned it over in his fingers. "It's been working. Sort of. But it's not us deciding anything. It's the pattern deciding, and us cleaning up after."

Rimuru said nothing.

"What if we opened one door," said Mau. "On purpose. One. With you watching. With Ciel watching. And let my body learn one thing, slowly, instead of whatever falls out next."

Rimuru was quiet for a long time.

"You're thinking about the wobbly chair," he said.

"I'm thinking it came when I asked."

"I told you not to make that a plan."

"This isn't that." Mau turned the stone over. "That was me asking something that had already leaked to come out again. This is asking you to open a door. With Ciel watching. On purpose. Before it opens itself."

Rimuru was quiet again.

"Which one?" he said.

He already knew.

Mau could tell he already knew.

"The elf route."



"Tell me why," said Rimuru.

"You know why."

"Tell me anyway. Out loud. I want to hear you say it, so I know it's you deciding and not the pattern."

Mau turned the stone over again.

"She outlives everyone," he said. "Everyone she's ever loved. She told me. She stopped counting. Not because she forgot. Because the number stopped meaning anything." He looked at the water. "And every single time, she finds out afterward what they were like. Because there wasn't enough time while they were alive. She goes around asking people. She did it for Himmel. She'll do it for all of us, eventually."

Rimuru did not answer.

"I don't want to be one of the ones she finds out about afterward," said Mau. "I don't want to be a short thing that happened to her once. An instant." His voice did something. He let it. "I want to get old with her. Or not old. Whatever elves do instead. I want to be there long enough that she doesn't have to go around asking anybody what I was like."

He put the stone down.

"That's it. That's the whole reason."

Rimuru looked at him for a long moment.

Then he looked away, out over the lake, and his face did something private, and he let it.

"That's a good reason," he said.

"Is it a stupid idea?"

"Probably," said Rimuru. "Ciel?"

Possible, said Ciel, to both of them. One guardrail. One route. Not to finish it. Only to see. How long his body can hold it. What it can do. What he can control. How long it stays stable. I would watch every second. I would close it at the first sign of cross-feedback, before he asked.

"Fever?"

Likely. Brief.

"Risk?"

Low, if I close it early. Not none. Nothing about this is none.

Rimuru rubbed his face.

"We tell Frieren," he said.

"Obviously," said Mau. "Rule five's mine too."



He told her that afternoon, on the bed under the east window, with the door closed.

All of it.

The nineteen days. The door. The one route. The reason.

He said the reason last, and he said it to her face, which was harder than saying it to the lake.

"I don't want to be someone you find out about afterward."

Frieren did not say anything for a very long time.

Her face did nothing at all.

Mau had learned, by now, that this was the expression she wore when she was feeling the most.

"Say no," he said. "If you want. If you say no, we don't do it. That's the only condition I care about."

Frieren looked at him.

"I want it," she said.

Her voice was very quiet.

"I want it so much I'm angry about it." She looked down at her hands. "When Rimuru told me the elf route was closed, I nearly burned down an inn. You know that. Everyone knows that. And now you're offering me the thing I wanted to burn it down for, and all I can think is that if it goes wrong, it'll be because I wanted it."

"It'll be because I wanted it," said Mau. "I asked. Not you."

Frieren looked up at him.

"That's not how it feels."

"I know."

She was quiet again.

Then she said, "Conditions."

Mau almost smiled.

"Okay."

"I'm there. The whole time. Not in the doorway. Next to you."

"Yes."

"If I say stop, it stops. I don't care what Ciel's numbers say. I don't care if it's working."

Agreed, said Ciel, and Mau repeated it.

"And nobody else knows." Frieren's jaw set. "Not Fern. Not Ori. Not Coco. Nobody. If it doesn't work, I don't want this whole house hoping for a thing that isn't coming. I've watched them hope. I don't want to watch them stop."

"Maomao," said Mau.

Frieren's face went through several things.

"Maomao will say no."

"Maomao will be right."

"Then we tell her after," said Frieren, "and let her kill us."

"That's a terrible plan."

"Yes," said Frieren. "Do you have a better one?"

Mau did not.



They chose the place together.

Mau suggested the lake here, the new one, where nothing had happened yet.

Frieren shook her head.

"The clearing," she said.

Mau went still.

The clearing where he had woken. Where she had made the flowers. Where, a few weeks ago, a door had opened over the old lake and shown it to him, frost lifting, the shape of nobody pressed into the grass.

"The pattern tried to take you there," said Frieren. "It wanted to go back to the beginning so badly that it built a door."

"Yes."

"Then let it go back," she said. "With you awake. With me there. On purpose." She looked at him. "So it can see that the beginning has me in it."

Mau did not say anything.

He could not.

He just nodded.

Rimuru, when they told him, was quiet for a moment, and then said, "That's either the best idea either of you has ever had or the worst."

That is the correct assessment, said Ciel.

They went three days later, at dawn, on the excuse that Frieren wanted books from the old inn.

Nobody questioned it.

Frieren always wanted books from the old inn.
