# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 15
## Chapter 12 — One Song

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 15  
**Reading order:** 12  
**Continuity state:** Ordinary Week 3 — the day they picked | Late birthday | Visiting-hours karaoke agreement paid

Mau knew something was wrong the moment he and Frieren came back from the river.

The settlement was too quiet.

Not empty.

Quiet.

The specific quiet of forty people trying very hard not to make noise at the same time.

Milim and Umaru were standing directly in front of the common room door with their arms spread wide.

"You can't go in there," said Milim.

"Why not?"

"Because," said Umaru.

"Because why?"

Milim looked at Umaru.

Umaru looked at Milim.

Neither of them had practiced this part.

"Because it's on fire," Milim said.

Mau's face changed.

"Not on fire!" said Umaru. "It's not on fire. It's—"

Frieren reached past both of them and opened the door.



The common room had paper stars strung across the ceiling, and they were glowing.

Paper flowers on every table, folded so precisely that some of them looked like they might actually open.

A cone of rolled paper the size of a person, mounted on a stand at the front of the room, pointed at an empty chair.

Food.

A great deal of food.

Lamps everywhere, and under the lamps, everyone.

Every single person.

Mau stood in the doorway.

He looked at the stars.

At the flowers.

At the cake in the middle of the long table, with Maomao standing beside it like a guard who had personally inspected every crumb.

"It's not—" he said.

He stopped.

"It's not my birthday."

"It was," Fern said. "Weeks ago. We were busy."

She held up the calendar from the old inn kitchen.

Two circles now.

"So it's today."

Mau looked at the calendar.

Then at her.

"You can't just—"

Rimuru, from the back of the room, said:

"We just did."

Somewhere in the crowd, someone who had been at the first one laughed, and then several people who had been at the first one laughed, and Mau found that he could not say anything at all.



They made him sit down.

They made him eat.

Maomao watched him eat the cake the way she had watched him take his first steps after the fever, and only after the first slice had gone down without incident did she cut herself a piece.

Kaneki handed him a cup of something hot and dark and bitter.

Mau drank it.

Stopped.

Drank it again.

"What is this?"

"Coffee," said Kaneki. "Close enough."

Mau looked at the cup for a long time.

He did not remember coffee.

His mouth did.

"Thank you," he said, and Kaneki, who had not expected that to matter as much as it did, looked away.



Then the presents.

Nobody had been told to bring one.

Everybody had.

Most of them were small.

A carved spoon from Wakana, with a groove worn in the handle where his thumb would go.

A book from Frieren with no title on the cover, which turned out on the first page to contain every spell she had ever found that served no purpose at all, copied out in her own hand.

A file folder from Anko, labeled MAU, very thick, which upon inspection contained nothing but blank pages and a single note on the first one: TO BE CONTINUED.

From Seiko, a scarf.

"It gets cold here," she said. "You're too thin."

"Thank you."

"Wear it."

"Yes ma'am."

"Hm," said Seiko, and patted his hand twice, briskly.

Yuta's present was a small wooden box.

Inside was a smooth flat stone.

Nothing special about it.

Mau turned it over in his fingers.

He did not understand.

Yuta did not explain.

Later, much later, Mau would find out where Yuta had picked it up, and how long he had carried it without saying so, through two searches and a move, waiting until he decided it was time.

That night, he only said thank you.

Yuta said:

"Happy birthday."

As if that covered it.

It did.



Ori's present came last.

It was very small.

A bracelet, woven out of thread.

Blue.

Mau recognized the thread.

He looked up at her.

"From our—"

"From the date," Ori said. "The one you and Frieren know happened. Frieren gave me what was left."

Mau put it on.

His hands were not quite steady.

Ori looked at the bracelet on his wrist, and then at him, with an expression that was not quite a daughter's and not quite anything else, and that was entirely hers.

"So you have something you know is real," she said. "Every day. Without checking."

Mau did not answer.

He could not.

Frieren, beside him, put her hand over the bracelet and over his wrist and left it there.



"Now," said Coco.

The whole room turned.

Coco and Umaru stood together at the front, beside Senku's paper horn, wearing expressions of terrible solemnity.

"By agreement," Coco said, "signed during visiting hours, in the presence of witnesses—"

"Frieren," said Umaru.

"—Frieren," said Coco, "the birthday person owes one song."

The room erupted.

Mau put his face in his hands.

"I was concussed when I agreed to that."

"Still counts," said Umaru.

"Dancing optional," said Coco.

"And mandatory," said Umaru.

He looked to Frieren for rescue.

Frieren was already writing something in a small notebook, and her mouth was doing the thing it did when she was trying very hard not to smile.

He looked to Maomao.

"Medically," Maomao said, "singing is fine."

"Betrayal everywhere," Mau said, and stood up.



He did not know any songs from this world.

He knew one from his own.

He did not remember where he had learned it.

He did not remember who had sung it to him, or whether anyone ever had.

He only knew the words, the way his mouth had known the coffee.

He started badly.

Halfway through the first line his left eye opened, black and red, the way it did now when too much was happening inside him at once.

He did not cover it.

Nobody stopped him.

Off key.

Too quiet.

The paper horn turned his too-quiet into a wobbling, enormous, extremely audible noise, and Senku looked pleased with himself and also very sorry.

Mau kept going anyway.

Halfway through the first verse, a guitar came in underneath him.

Soft.

Searching.

Then sure.

Bocchi, at the edge of the room, eyes closed, finding the shape of a song she had never heard by listening to a man who barely knew it himself.

Nijika picked up the rhythm.

Ryo found the bottom of it.

Kita came in on the second verse, loud, delighted, in a language she did not speak, making up the sounds she could not catch.

And then everybody else did too.

Badly.

All of them.

Forty people who had never heard this song and never would again, singing it at the top of their lungs in a half-finished room because a man with no birthday had one.

Mai stood near the back with her arms folded.

She did not sing.

She watched her sister sing.

She watched Maki, who had never once in Mai's memory done anything in front of a crowd that she could not win, singing a song in a language neither of them knew, laughing at herself, completely unguarded.

Mai unfolded her arms.

Nijika, beside her, nudged her shoulder.

"You're allowed to," Nijika said.

"I don't know the words."

"Nobody knows the words."

Mai looked at the room.

At Mau, who was singing worse than anyone, and grinning like an idiot, and so obviously did not understand why all of these people were here.

She did not sing.

But she stopped standing like someone who was about to leave.



It ended the way those things end.

Too late, with too much food left, and Nazuna, who had slept through the entire day of preparation and most of the party, waking at last to an empty cake plate and asking whether she had missed anything, and everyone laughing so hard she had to be told three times.

Later, when the lamps were lower and most of the room had gone quiet, Mau sat on the floor with his back against the wall and the scarf around his neck and the bracelet on his wrist and the coffee gone cold beside him.

Frieren sat down next to him.

"It wasn't my birthday," he said again.

Quietly, this time.

As if he were still trying to work out the trick.

Frieren looked out at the room.

At the paper stars still glowing faintly, and the people asleep in chairs, and Stark carrying the last table back to where it had been, and Fern telling him it went by the window.

"It was never about the date," she said.

Mau looked at her.

He did not understand.

Not yet.

But he put his head on her shoulder, and she let him, and the stars Coco and Qifrey had drawn went on glowing until the very last of the ink was gone.
