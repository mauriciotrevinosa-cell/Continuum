# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 15
## Chapter 32 — Catching Up

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 15  
**Reading order:** 32  
**Continuity state:** Refuge pressure rising | Mau notices Yuta has drifted | 'We've been catching up' | Mau, Rimuru and Yuta make roof night weekly | Yuta seeds Gojo

Mau noticed it on a Tuesday, in the middle of an argument about refuge blankets, and could not stop noticing it afterward.

He had not really talked to Yuta in weeks.

Not since the Move. Maybe not since before it. He had seen him every day: at meals, at the worksite, in the corridor, on the other side of the courtyard with a crate on each shoulder. They had said good morning and pass the salt and did you see where Wakana put the long saw. But talked, the way they used to talk at the old inn at two in the morning when neither of them could sleep and the whole household had been six people and a slime?

No.

He went looking the next morning, early, and found Yuta where he usually was at that hour: on the training ground past the washing court, with Maki.

They were sparring. They did it most mornings now. Mau stood at the edge of the packed earth and watched, and understood within about ten seconds that he had never once seen either of them move like this. Not in a fight. Not in a crisis. Not even in the drills Yuta ran for the Searchers.

They moved like people who had done this thousands of times. In another life. For a whole life. Every feint already known, every answer already waiting, and both of them laughing, a little, under their breath, at the ones they got wrong on purpose.

When they finished, Maki saw him first.

"He's been standing there for ten minutes," she said to Yuta.

Yuta turned. His face did the thing it always did when he saw Mau: opened, like a door.

"Hey."

"Hey."

Mau did not know how to say it, so he said it badly.

"I feel like I haven't seen you."

Yuta blinked.

Then he looked at Maki, and something passed between them, and Maki picked up both practice weapons and walked off toward the washing court without being asked.

"We've been catching up," said Yuta.

"On what?"

Yuta thought about it, honestly, the way he thought about everything.

"A whole life," he said. "And then this one. We had a whole life together and then it ended, and then we woke up here, young, and she was still her and I was still me, and nobody gave us any instructions for that." He smiled a little. "It's taking a while."

Mau felt something in his chest that he did not have a name for. Not jealousy. Something smaller and more embarrassing.

"Right," he said. "No, that's— that makes sense. That's good. I didn't mean—"

"Mau."

He stopped.

"I've been gone," said Yuta simply. "I know. I'm sorry."

"You weren't gone. You were right there."

"Right there isn't the same," said Yuta. "You taught me that, actually. A long time ago. At the inn."

Mau did not remember teaching him that.

He decided, for once, not to check.



That night, Mau went up onto the common-room roof, the eastern half, the sound half, where he had sat with Rimuru at dawn after the shadow.

Rimuru was already there.

He had started coming up most nights, he admitted, after the loft. Not to sleep. Just to sit somewhere above everyone for an hour before he went in, the way he used to sit on the roof of a very different building in a very different world.

They sat.

After a while, there was a creak on the ladder.

Yuta's head appeared over the edge of the roof. He looked at the two of them, sitting against the chimney with their knees up, and raised his eyebrows.

"Is this a club?"

Rimuru considered.

"It is now."

Yuta climbed up and sat down on Mau's other side, and for a moment none of them said anything at all.

It used to be the three of them, a lot, at the beginning. Before the inn was full and the settlement had a name, before the Searchers and the refuge and the rumors, before Ring One and Ring Two. A boy who did not know who he was, a slime from another world and a sorcerer from a third, sitting on a sagging roof at night while Frieren slept downstairs, trying to work out what to do with themselves.

"Remember the first roof?" said Mau.

"The one with the hole?" said Rimuru.

"You fell through the hole," said Yuta.

"I did not fall. I chose to go through it."

"You fell through it into the kitchen and Frieren didn't look up from her book."

Rimuru put his face in his hands.

Mau laughed so hard he had to hold onto the chimney.



They talked about nothing for an hour. It was the best hour Mau had had in weeks.

Then, later, when the settlement had gone mostly dark below them and only the refuge lamps were still burning out by the road, Yuta said, quietly:

"People in the refuge are talking about you."

"I know."

"Not bad things. Mostly." Yuta looked out at the lamps. "They say this place is safe because of you. That you're the reason it exists. That if anything comes, you'll stand in front of it."

"That's not true."

"I know it's not true." Yuta was quiet for a moment. "Can I tell you about someone?"

Mau looked at him.

"There was a man in my world," said Yuta. "My teacher. Everyone called him the strongest. He was. Nobody could touch him. And because nobody could touch him, everyone let him carry everything. The students. The higher-ups. The war. All of it. Nobody ever asked him to. They didn't have to. They just stood behind him, and expected him to be there, and he always was."

He did not say what happened to that man.

He did not have to. Rimuru, on the other side of Mau, had gone very still in a way that said he knew exactly what Yuta was describing, and from which side.

"I let him," said Yuta. "I was one of the ones standing behind him. I loved him, and I let him carry it, and I never once thought about what it weighed." He turned his head and looked at Mau. "I don't want to do that to you."

"I'm not the strongest," said Mau. "I'm not even close. Rimuru's right here."

"That's not how it works," said Yuta gently. "He didn't carry it because he was strong. He carried it because everyone kept handing it to him. And he was the kind of person who couldn't put anything down once it was handed to him." He paused. "Sound like anyone?"

Mau opened his mouth.

He closed it again.

Rimuru said nothing for a long time.

Then, very quietly: "In my world, I built a country. Everyone handed me things. I kept saying yes." He looked at his left hand. "It took me a long time to learn that saying yes to everything isn't the same as being trustworthy. Sometimes it's the opposite."

They sat with that.

The refuge lamps burned out by the road.

"So what do I do?" said Mau finally.

"Nothing yet," said Yuta. "Just come up here. Once a week. The three of us. No business. No refuge, no Searchers, no rumors. And if any of us starts carrying something he shouldn't, the other two say so."

"That's a rule," said Mau. "You're making a rule."

"It's a roof," said Yuta. "Roofs don't have rules."

"Rule eight," said Rimuru.

Mau looked at him.

"Don't hide it to be kind," said Rimuru. "You wrote that one. We're just borrowing it."



Maki was waiting at the bottom of the ladder when Yuta came down, an hour later.

She had a blanket around her shoulders and the patient, unsurprised expression of a woman who had waited at the bottom of a great many ladders in her life.

"Did you tell him?" she said.

"Some," said Yuta.

"About Gojo?"

"Some."

Maki took his hand and they walked back along the corridor toward the room with one name on its door written as two.

"He'll need the rest eventually," she said.

"I know," said Yuta. "I'll be there when he does."
