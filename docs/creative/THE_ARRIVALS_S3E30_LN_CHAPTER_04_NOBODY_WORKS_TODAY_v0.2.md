# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 16
## Chapter 7 — Nobody Works Today

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 16  
**Reading order:** 7  
**Continuity state:** Audit paused by rain | Ordinary household day | Rem chooses space

It started raining before dawn and did not stop.

Not storm rain.

Not the kind that broke things and gave Senku something to measure.

Just rain.

Grey, steady and patient, the kind that soaked through every coat before you reached the far side of the courtyard, swelled every rope and made every joint too slick to set.

By breakfast, three work crews had gone out and three work crews had come back in.

Senku stood at the common-room window with his notebook and looked at the sky as if it had made an error in his numbers.

"It'll clear by noon."

"It won't," said Agott, from the table.

He looked at her.

She did not look up from her bread.

"Those are the same clouds the atelier used to get before the long rains. They don't clear by noon."

Senku wrote that down.

He did not say she was right.

He also did not cross it out.



Marin climbed onto a chair.

Wakana put one hand out without looking, in case the chair had opinions.

"Announcement," Marin said.

The room turned.

Most of the household was in it, plus a few guests from Arrival House who had come over for breakfast because the Arrival House canvas was leaking into the porridge.

"Nobody works today."

Fern put down her list.

"The western drain—"

"Is underwater. It'll still be underwater tomorrow. You can't fix a drain in the rain. I asked Wakana."

Wakana nodded. Marin had.

"The medical inventory—"

"Maomao did it yesterday. I watched her."

Maomao, at the far end of the table, did not deny it.

"Arrival House—"

"Has Nano," said Marin. "And Nano already said she'd rather be over there today because over here is going to be loud."

From the doorway, already in a rain cloak, Nano said, "It is going to be extremely loud," and left.

Fern looked at Seiko.

She knew it was a mistake as she made it.

Seiko had her tea in both hands and did not even look up.

"Girl's right," Seiko said. "Sit down."

Fern sat down.

Marin climbed off the chair, enormously pleased with herself, and Wakana took his hand back.



Rem had come over from the old inn the night before, with a satchel of travelers' notes for Arrival House and every intention of walking back after breakfast.

She stood at the window beside Senku and looked at the road.

By now it would be more river than road.

She thought about the last time she had walked back to the old inn in rain like this.

She remembered deciding, halfway there, that it had been stupid.

She put the satchel down.

Nobody said anything about it.

Momo slid a second bowl of porridge across the table toward the empty place beside Ori, and Rem sat in it, and that was that.



The common room got loud almost immediately.

It started with Umaru, who announced that a day with no work was a day with a fort, and who had found every spare blanket in the family wing before anyone could object.

Coco and Suika were her engineers.

Milim was her army.

The fort went up between the two sofas.

The fort came down between the two sofas.

"It keeps falling," Suika reported, to the window, where Senku was still pretending to work.

Senku did not turn around.

"Because you built a load-bearing blanket."

"What's a load-bearing blanket?"

Senku closed his eyes.

Then he put his notebook down, walked over, and spent fifteen minutes explaining tension, anchor points and why you could not hold up a roof with a cushion, while Suika nodded seriously and Umaru and Coco ignored every word and Milim tried to hold up the roof with a cushion.

The second fort stood.

Senku stood back and looked at it with the expression of a man who did not care about forts.

Then Suika pulled the flap aside and said, "Senku, come see the inside," and he went in, and did not come out for most of an hour.

Diablo was appointed inspector.

Nobody remembered appointing him.

He walked around the fort with his hands folded behind his back, making small approving sounds, and pronounced the eastern wall unworthy, and fixed it himself with a single blanket fold that made the entire structure noticeably straighter.

Rimuru watched this from the sofa.

"I didn't ask you to do that."

"No, Rimuru-sama," Diablo said, very pleased. "You didn't."

Milim dragged Rimuru inside the fort shortly afterward, on the grounds that every fort required a king and a king required a brother, and Rimuru said that was not how either of those things worked, and went in anyway.



At the long table, Turbo Granny was running a card game, and cheating.

Everyone knew she was cheating.

Nobody could prove it.

Okarun had lost four hands in a row and was beginning to take it personally.

"You can't have three of those."

"I have three of those."

"There are only two in the deck."

"Then the deck is wrong, idiot."

Hakari, Karane and Shizuka had joined on the theory that four players could watch one old woman better than one could.

They had lost too.

Karane announced that the game was rigged, the deck was rigged, the table was rigged and possibly the weather.

Shizuka slid a note across the table that said: SHE HIDES THEM IN HER SLEEVE.

Turbo Granny read the note.

She did not have a sleeve.

That made everything worse.

Then Seiko set down her tea, pulled a chair over and said:

"Deal me in."

The table went quiet.

Momo, who had been eating on the far side of it, slowly put down her spoon.

"Grandma."

"What?"

"Please don't."

Seiko took her cards.

Turbo Granny looked at her across the table.

Seiko looked back.

Momo told Okarun, very quietly, that they should probably move, and Okarun said he wasn't going anywhere, he wanted to see this, and Momo stayed too, because so did she.

Two hands later, Mai Zenin sat down without asking.

She had been watching from the doorway.

She had apparently been watching closely.

She did not say anything for three rounds.

On the fourth, she won.

On the fifth, she won.

Turbo Granny stared at her.

"Who taught you to play like that?"

Mai arranged her cards.

"Nobody. I grew up in a house where you learned to watch people's hands."

Across the room, Maki laughed so hard she had to put her head down on the arm of the sofa.

Yuta, beside her, looked from Maki to Mai and back.

"Was that a joke?"

"No," said Maki, still laughing. "That's what's funny."

Mai did not smile.

She did keep playing.

And when Seiko eventually beat her, Mai looked at the old woman across the table for a long moment, and said, "Again," and Seiko said, "Hm," in a tone that meant she liked her.



Stark fell asleep sitting up against the wall around the middle of the afternoon.

He did it the way he did everything that required no decision: completely, and without noticing.

Fern noticed.

She went to the blanket pile, which was now mostly a fort, negotiated with Umaru for one blanket, lost the negotiation, and took one anyway.

She put it over him.

Himmel watched her from the armchair by the fire.

"Eisen slept like that," he said, when she came back. "Sitting up. As if he might be needed."

Fern looked at Stark.

"He might be."

Himmel waited.

Fern did not explain.

She went back to the wall, after a while, and sat down beside Stark.

Not touching.

Then, when the fire burned low and the room got colder, slightly closer.

Himmel smiled into his tea and did not say a single word, which for Himmel was an act of real heroism.



Kessoku had planned to practice.

The music room had other ideas.

Rain on the music room roof was louder than Ryo's bass, and Ryo, after one song, said that she refused to compete with weather, and lay down on the floor.

So they came into the common room instead.

Bocchi sat in the corner with her guitar and played very softly, nothing in particular, the kind of music that made a room easier to be in without asking anyone to listen.

Nijika tapped along on her knee.

Kita sat beside Bocchi and, at some point, put her head on Bocchi's shoulder.

Bocchi missed one note.

Then two.

Then she found the song again and kept playing with her shoulder very, very still, as if Kita were a bird that might leave if she moved.

Kita did not leave.

Marin, passing with a tray, saw them and pressed her free hand to her heart.

"Don't," said Kita, without opening her eyes.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

Marin went away to say it to Wakana instead.



Nazuna woke at noon.

The rain had made the afternoon so dark that she came downstairs convinced it was night, full of energy, ready for anything, and found forty tired people and a fort.

"Why is everyone asleep?" she demanded. "It's night!"

"It's lunch," said Anko.

Nazuna looked out of the window.

Then at Anko.

"It's dark."

"It's raining."

Nazuna considered this.

Then she was recruited into the fort as its dragon, because Frieren had refused the position an hour before, and she took the role extremely seriously, and roared at Milim, and Milim roared back, and the fort fell down for the third time.

Anko laughed until she had to sit on the floor.



Rem did not dislike noise.

Not exactly.

She liked the common room.

She liked that it was full.

But sometimes there was a limit to how much of other people she could hold at once, and on a day like this, with nowhere to walk to, the limit came early.

She went looking for somewhere quieter.

Down the family corridor.

Past doors she knew she should not open.

At the turn of the corridor, where Yuta's door and Rimuru's door and the door to Mau and Frieren's room made their careful little triangle, there was a fourth door.

Narrow.

Half-hung.

Wakana's chalk on the frame said: STORAGE (LATER).

So far it stored one broom and one crate of nails.

It had a window over the courtyard.

The rain ran down it in long grey lines.

Rem sat down on the crate of nails.

It was very quiet.

She stayed.



Kaneki found her there about an hour later.

He stopped in the doorway with a book in his hand.

"Sorry. I didn't—"

"It isn't mine," Rem said.

Kaneki looked at the room.

At the broom.

"It isn't anyone's," he agreed.

He sat down on the floor against the other wall.

He opened the book.

They did not talk.

She knew, roughly, what he was.

He knew, roughly, what she was.

Neither of them needed to bring it into the room.

The rain went on.

It was, Rem thought, the most comfortable silence she had shared with anyone in this world who was not Mau or Frieren or Ori.

She did not know what to do with that.

She did not do anything with it.

She let it be.



Frieren came next.

She had a grimoire under her arm and the expression of someone who had been asked to be a dragon twice.

She looked at Rem on the crate and Kaneki on the floor.

She sat on the windowsill.

She opened the grimoire.

Ten minutes passed.

Then Frieren said, without looking up:

"What are you reading?"

Kaneki held up the cover.

It was one of the books from the old inn's shelf, which he had been reading in order, left to right, since the move.

"Is it good?" Frieren asked.

"No," said Kaneki.

Frieren looked at him.

"Then why are you reading it?"

Kaneki thought about it.

"Because somebody wrote it," he said. "Somebody sat down and decided this was worth putting on paper. I like finding out what people thought was worth keeping."

Frieren was quiet for a while.

Then she held up her grimoire.

"This is a spell for turning bad tea into slightly less bad tea," she said. "It took me forty years to find."

Kaneki looked at it.

Then at her.

Something in his face eased that Rem had not known was tight.

"Is it good?"

"No," said Frieren.

And, very faintly, Kaneki laughed.



Ori found them when she came looking for Rem.

She looked into the narrow room, at three people in it who were not talking, and understood immediately.

She did not say anything either.

She sat down on the floor with her back against Rem's crate, and after a while, without discussion, Rem moved the blanket she had brought from her shoulders so that half of it went over Ori's.

Ori leaned her head back against Rem's knee.

That was all.

That was a great deal.



Mau found them last.

He had been looking for Frieren.

He usually was.

He stopped in the doorway and looked at the room.

Rem on a crate of nails.

Kaneki on the floor with a bad book.

Frieren on the windowsill with a worse spell.

Ori half-asleep against Rem's knee.

"Is this a meeting?"

"No," said Frieren.

Mau sat down on the floor beneath the windowsill, where Frieren's feet hung down, and leaned his head back against the wall, and closed his eyes.

At some point he dozed.

At some point after that, Kaneki looked up from his bad book and saw that Mau was no longer quite sitting on the floor. He was a finger's width above it.

Kaneki looked at Frieren.

Frieren, without taking her eyes off her grimoire, hooked one foot over Mau's ankle and drew him gently back down.

"Rule eight," Ori murmured, half asleep against Rem's knee.

"Later," said Frieren. "He's resting."

Twelve days since the last one, Ciel noted, to nobody.

The rain went on.

From the far end of the house, very faintly, came the sound of a fort falling down for the fourth time and Milim declaring war on gravity.

"This room is good," Mau said after a long while.

"Yes," said Rem.

"What is it?"

"Storage," said Ori, without opening her eyes.

"Later," said Kaneki.



The rain stopped a little after dark.

Rem found Fern in the kitchen with the plan of the family wing spread out on the table, already marking what needed to be fixed tomorrow.

Rem stood beside her for a while before she said anything.

Fern let her.

"The narrow room," Rem said finally. "At the end of the corridor."

Fern looked at the plan.

"Storage. Later."

"Could it be—" Rem stopped. Started again. "When it rains. Could I use it. Only when it rains."

Fern looked at her.

She looked at her for long enough that Rem started to take it back.

Then Fern picked up the pencil, drew one line through STORAGE (LATER), and wrote above it, small and neat:

Rem.

She did not write anything about rain.

Rem looked at the word.

"When it rains," she said again.

"It rains a lot here," said Fern.

Rem was quiet.

"...Yes," she said.

Fern went back to her list.

Rem went back down the corridor, moved the broom into the hall, and the crate of nails after it, and left the blanket folded on the windowsill.

Then she went to sleep in the common room with everyone else, because the fort had finally been declared finished and Umaru would not let anyone go to bed until they had been inside it.

But the blanket stayed where she had left it.

In the morning it was still there.
