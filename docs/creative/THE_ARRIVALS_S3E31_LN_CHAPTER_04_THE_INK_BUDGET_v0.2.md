# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 16
## Chapter 11 — The Ink Budget

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 16  
**Reading order:** 11  
**Same time as:** V16 #8–#23 (the redesign weeks: each chapter follows one thread through the same weeks)  
**Continuity state:** Settlement weeks | Witch Hat ink counted and spent once: the shadowless light | The seed has sprouted; nothing is built inside its circle

While the water circles were still being argued over, Qifrey did the count nobody wanted to do.

Qifrey counted the ink on the atelier floor, with all four of his students watching.

It did not take long.

That was the problem.

He laid it out on a clean cloth in a row: every bottle, every stoppered vial, every small sealed pot that still held anything at all. Some were full. Most were not. One was barely a stain at the bottom of the glass, and he set it down very gently at the end of the row, as if it might be offended by its own emptiness.

Coco counted with him under her breath.

Agott did not count. Agott had already counted, three days ago, without being asked, and had written the total on the back of her hand so she could not lose it.

Richeh looked at the row and said, "Oh."

Tetia did not say anything at all.

"This," said Qifrey, "is everything."

Nobody asked everything of what. They knew. Everything that had come across from their world. Everything that had survived the field at the old inn, the one drawn to stop a monster, which had cost more than any of them had wanted to know at the time. Everything that would not come back.

"How long does it last?" said Richeh.

"That depends entirely," said Qifrey, "on what we spend it on."



Everybody, it turned out, wanted to spend it on something.

They had not known that until they started asking. Then the requests came in a flood.

Momo wanted a warming glyph under the kitchen floor, so that bread would rise in winter. Maomao wanted a cold glyph in the medicine store. Senku wanted a glyph that would tell him when the drainage was failing before it failed, which Qifrey said was not how glyphs worked and Senku said was how they should work. Marin wanted the washing court's water warm. Seiko wanted the old inn's roof to stop leaking, which was not, strictly speaking, in the Muslin at all. Kaneki asked, very quietly, whether there was anything that could make the smell of cooking meat travel less far. Aira wanted something defensive, and would not say what.

And Coco, sitting on the atelier floor beside the row of bottles, said:

"Light."

Everyone looked at her.

"In the common room," said Coco. "Over the scratches."

The atelier went quiet.

Since the night of the shadow, the common room had five lamps. Six on bad nights. Oil, every one of them, burning low from dusk to dawn so that nobody's shadow in that room ever fell very long. It worked. It also cost oil, and oil cost money, and every lamp was one more thing that could tip, or gutter, or go out at two in the morning when nobody was watching.

"A glyph light doesn't come from one place," said Coco. "It comes from everywhere at once. The whole circle glows. If we put one on the ceiling over the scratches, nobody standing under it would have a shadow at all. Not a long one. Not a hole." She looked down at the row of bottles. "I know it's expensive."

Qifrey looked at her for a long time.

"It's very expensive," he said.

"I know."

"Light that strong, held all night, every night." He touched the second-fullest bottle with one fingertip. "That is most of this one."

Coco nodded.

She did not take it back.



They argued for two days.

Not angrily. Qifrey would not allow angry. He made them sit on the atelier floor and argue properly, the way he had made them argue about circles back in their own world: every request written on its own slip of paper, laid out on the floor around the row of bottles, and every slip had to be defended out loud by someone who had not asked for it.

Agott defended Momo's warming glyph. She did it well, with numbers, and then voted against it, because bread could rise by the stove.

Richeh defended Senku's drainage glyph and accidentally convinced herself that something like it might actually be possible. She spent the next hour drawing it in water on the atelier floor while the others went on arguing around her.

Tetia defended Kaneki's request, very gently. Nobody voted against it. It turned out to be cheap, a small glyph that turned the air in one direction, and it went on the kitchen wall on the side nearest his room. Kaneki, when told, did not say anything. But he came to the atelier that evening with a plate of something he had cooked himself, with no meat in it, and left it on the floor by the door, and went away again before anyone could thank him.

And Coco's light.

Coco did not defend her own request. That was the rule. So Agott did.

She stood up in the middle of the atelier floor with the number on the back of her hand and her arms folded and her face set in its usual expression of mild contempt for everyone present.

"It's the most expensive thing on this floor," said Agott. "It does nothing that oil doesn't do. Oil works. We have oil. If it were my ink I'd say no." She paused. "It isn't my ink. It's everyone's. And everyone in this house walks past those scratches every day and pretends not to look at them, and when the lamps are low at two in the morning, they look." She unfolded her arms. "If there's a light that means nobody in that room ever has to look at their own shadow and wonder, then that's what the ink is for. Not bread."

She sat down.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Coco was looking at Agott as if she had never seen her before.

"What," said Agott.

"Nothing," said Coco. "Nothing."

The light passed.



Coco drew it herself, on the common-room ceiling, standing on Wakana's tallest ladder with Qifrey holding it steady and the whole household watching from below in a silence so complete that Bocchi, in the music room, stopped tuning to listen to it.

It was a small circle, no wider than a cartwheel. She drew it slowly and perfectly. The ink went on silver and dried pale.

When she finished the last line, it lit.

Not like a lamp. Not from a point. The whole circle glowed, softly and evenly, a light like an overcast noon, and it fell on the common room from everywhere at once. Under it, on the floor, the four varnished scratches lay in plain sight, clean and pale. Mau, standing right over them, looked down at his own feet and saw no shadow there at all. Just the floor, and the scratches, and his own two feet.

He looked up at Coco on the ladder.

She was crying a little. She was also grinning so hard that the ladder shook.

That night, for the first time since the Move, the common-room lamps went out at midnight, and nobody lit them again.



"That leaves," said Qifrey afterward, on the atelier floor, "almost nothing."

He had laid the bottles out again. The row was shorter now. The second-fullest bottle was not second-fullest anymore.

"Then we need more," said Coco.

"There isn't any more."

"There will be."

Coco went to the door of the atelier and pointed out of it. Across the yard, past the end of the Muslin, at the edge of the garden she had marked on the first day before anybody had a house, a stake stood with a faded rag tied to it.

Beside the stake, there was a shoot.

It had come up nine days ago. Coco had found it at dawn and had not told anyone for a whole day, out of a superstition she could not explain. It had two leaves. They were a color nobody had a name for, somewhere between green and silver. Every morning since, Coco had gone out and measured it with a strip of paper, and every morning it was a hair taller than the day before.

"The tree I came from," said Qifrey slowly, "took a hundred years before anyone could draw ink from it."

"So we plan for a hundred years," said Coco.

Agott snorted.

"None of us will be here in a hundred years."

There was a short silence.

Then, from the doorway behind them, where she had apparently been standing for some time, Frieren said:

"I will."

Everyone turned.

She was leaning against the doorframe with a book under her arm and her eyes on the small silver-green shoot at the edge of the garden.

"I can wait a hundred years," she said. "I've waited longer for worse things." She looked at Coco. "Tell me what it needs."



Senku and Coco marked the circle that afternoon.

They worked out from the stake together, Senku with his measuring cord and his notes on soil and roots, Coco with her strip of paper and her absolute certainty, and they drove a ring of small stakes into the ground around the shoot. It was much wider than anyone expected. Wide enough that the ring ran right up against the end of the Muslin's second section, and curved around it.

"This is where the roots will go," said Senku. "Eventually. If it's anything like the trees I know, and it isn't, so double it."

"So nothing gets built inside the circle," said Coco.

"Nothing gets built inside the circle."

"Ever."

Senku looked at her.

"Ever's a long time."

"Frieren's going to be here," said Coco. "She can check."

Wakana came out to look. He walked all the way around the ring of stakes with his chalk in his hand. Then he went back to the board in the common room, where the real house was still only a list of things the Muslin had taught them, and under *More bells*, he wrote:

The house goes around the tree. Not on it.

Then he went and found Mau, who was sitting on the step outside the common room in the shadowless light coming through the open door. He told him, because he thought someone should, that Frieren had said she would wait a hundred years for a tree.

Mau did not say anything.

But that night, lying awake in the room with the east window, with Rimuru snoring softly in the loft, he thought about a hundred years. About a woman standing in a doorway saying *I will*, and about how he would very much like to be standing beside her when the tree was finally tall enough to sit under.

He did not say that either.

Not yet.
