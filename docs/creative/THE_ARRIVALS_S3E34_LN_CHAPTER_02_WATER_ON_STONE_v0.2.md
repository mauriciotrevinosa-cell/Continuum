# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 16
## Chapter 18 — Water on Stone

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 16  
**Reading order:** 18  
**Continuity state:** Ordinary day | Coco teaches Frieren | Frieren mirrors Mau's care for Coco

The invitation came at breakfast, and it was not for Mau.

Frieren put down her cup, looked across the table at Coco and said:

"Are you busy today?"

Coco froze with a spoon halfway to her mouth.

Mau, beside Frieren, also froze.

So did Agott, Richeh, Tetia, Qifrey, and most of the people in earshot who knew Frieren well enough to know that she did not, as a rule, invite anyone anywhere.

"...No?" said Coco.

"Good," said Frieren. "Show me how you draw."

The spoon finally reached Coco's mouth.

She forgot to eat what was on it.



"She asked me," Coco said, at the door, for the third time, in the tone of someone who was still checking.

"She did," said Mau.

"Not you."

"Not me."

"Me."

"You."

Coco looked at him, suddenly anxious.

"Is that okay?"

Mau thought about Coco dragging him out to plant a seed. Coco with a colored schedule, wanting a memory whose purpose was not recovery.

"Coco," he said. "Go."

She went.

Frieren was waiting at the end of the courtyard with her staff and nothing else.

No food.

No water.

No cloak, though the morning was cool.

Coco looked at her.

Then ran back inside, and came out with a bag, and Mau, watching from the doorway, saw Frieren look at the bag and then at Coco with an expression he could not quite read.

They left together.

Mau stood in the doorway long after they had gone.

Qifrey came and stood beside him.

"She's been preparing for a week," Qifrey said.

Mau looked at him.

"Coco?"

"Frieren." Qifrey's mouth moved slightly. "She has been asking me questions. Very specific ones. How Coco learns. What Coco finds difficult. Whether Coco takes correction better before lunch or after."

"Which is it?"

"After," said Qifrey. "Obviously."

Mau laughed.

Then stopped.

"Why didn't she tell me?"

Qifrey thought about it.

"Because it isn't about you," he said, not unkindly. "Which I think, for both of them, is the point."



Fern found Stark in the yard twenty minutes later and handed him a basket.

"Lunch," she said. "For Frieren and Coco. They went to the river."

Stark looked at the basket.

"Didn't Coco take a bag?"

"Coco took a bag for Coco," Fern said. "Frieren will have taken nothing. Coco will try to share. Coco will not have brought enough for two. Go."

Stark went.

He had been carrying things for Frieren for years.

He did not even have to think about it anymore.

He was halfway across the yard before it occurred to him that this was the first time he had been sent to carry them for someone else as well, and that he did not mind.



The river ran broad and slow past the bridge, and below the bridge there was a shelf of flat grey stone, smooth from years of water.

That was where Coco took her.

She knelt on the stone and took a small brush out of her bag, and a little jar of plain river water, and drew.

The line went down wet and dark on the grey.

A circle.

A shape inside the circle.

Three small marks.

"It doesn't do anything," Coco explained. "It's just water. In the sun it's gone in a minute. That's how Qifrey taught us to practice. You draw it until your hand knows it. Then you're allowed to use ink."

Frieren crouched beside her and watched the line fade.

It went pale.

Then it was gone.

"Again," Frieren said.

Coco drew it again.

Frieren watched where her wrist turned.

How she held the brush.

How she drew the three small marks last, and always from the same side.

"Why from the left?"

Coco blinked.

Nobody had ever asked her that.

"...Because that's how Qifrey does it?"

"Does it work from the right?"

Coco thought.

"I don't know."

"Find out," said Frieren, and Coco drew it from the right, and nothing happened, because it was water, and they both bent over the stone to watch it fade as if something might.

Then Coco held out the brush.

Frieren looked at it.

"Your turn," said Coco.



A thousand years of magic did not help.

That was the first thing Frieren learned.

Her circle was perfect.

Her inner shape was perfect.

Her three small marks were in entirely the wrong place, because she had been looking at the shape and not at the hand, and Coco, without thinking, reached over and moved Frieren's wrist by a finger's width, the way Qifrey moved hers.

Then she realized what she had done.

She took her hand back very fast.

"Sorry—"

"Why?"

"You're—" Coco gestured, helplessly, at all of Frieren. "You're Frieren."

"I'm bad at this," said Frieren. "You aren't. Do it again."

Coco did it again.

Frieren's second glyph was better.

Her fourth was nearly right.

Her seventh, Coco said, would have done something, if it had been ink.

Frieren sat back on her heels and looked at the fading line.

She looked pleased.

Not the faint pleased of a good spell found in a bad grimoire.

Something more open.

Coco had seen that look before.

She had seen it on Frieren's face when Mau was explaining something he loved.



Stark found them an hour after noon, and by then Coco was cold.

She had not said so.

She had been kneeling on wet stone all morning, and the wind came up the river with the water, and her hands had started to go pale at the knuckles.

Stark noticed it from twenty steps away.

But Frieren had already noticed.

She took off her own coat, the only layer she had brought, and put it around Coco's shoulders without discussion.

She took the brush out of Coco's hand.

She put both of Coco's hands between her own, briefly, the way you would warm a cup.

Then she reached up and fixed the brim of Coco's hat, which had been knocked crooked by the wind, and settled it straight.

Coco went very still.

Stark, who had stopped walking, also went very still.

He knew that gesture.

Not from Frieren.

He had seen Mau do exactly that, to exactly that hat, a dozen times at least. At the old inn. On the road. In the new common room. Coco forgetting her own hat was crooked, and Mau straightening it on his way past without breaking stride.

Coco knew it too.

"Did Mau tell you to—"

"No," said Frieren.

"Then how—"

Frieren let go of the hat brim.

She looked down the river, toward the settlement, toward the bridge, and beyond it the roofs.

"I watched him," she said.

Coco looked at her.

"For a long time," Frieren said, "I didn't watch people properly. I thought I had time. I'd look later. Then one day there wasn't a later, and I had to go around asking everyone what he had been like, because I hadn't paid attention when he was standing right in front of me."

She did not say Himmel's name.

She did not need to.

Himmel was alive now, by some arithmetic of this world neither of them understood, and sat by the same fire as her most evenings. It had not given her back the years she had not watched.

"I'm not doing that again," she said. "So I watch Mau. What he does. How he does it. Who he does it for." She looked back at Coco. "He does that with your hat."

Coco's eyes had gone very bright.

"So now I do it too," said Frieren. "In case."

She did not say in case of what.

Coco did not ask.

She leaned into the borrowed coat and into Frieren's side, slightly, the way she did with Mau, and Frieren let her, and did not look as though she knew quite what to do about it, and did not move away either.



Stark came the rest of the way, very loudly, so they would hear him coming.

"Lunch," he said. "From Fern."

Frieren looked at the basket.

"I didn't bring food."

"She knew."

"Of course she did."

They ate on the stone.

Coco had brought exactly enough for one person and tried to share it anyway, and Stark watched Fern's prediction come true and decided never to tell Fern, because she would be insufferable, and then decided he would tell her immediately, because she would be pleased.

After lunch Coco tried to teach Stark too.

Stark was very bad at it.

He was bad at it in a different way from Frieren, though.

Frieren had been perfect in the wrong places.

Stark was wrong everywhere, but all his lines were steady, and his circle did not shake even once.

"Your hands are good," Coco told him, surprised.

Stark looked at his hands.

"They shake when I'm scared," he said.

Coco considered this.

"Are you scared now?"

Stark thought about it honestly.

"No."

"Then they're good for circles," said Coco, and went back to correcting his marks.

Stark laughed, and Frieren looked up from the stone at the sound, and something in her face said she had not heard that laugh in a while and had missed it.



They walked back in the late afternoon.

On the way, Coco stopped, and opened her bag, and took out a tiny stoppered bottle.

Ink.

Real ink.

Her own, from the small supply Qifrey had divided between his students when they first realized it would have to last.

She looked at it for a long moment.

Then she knelt by the path and drew, in ink, very small, on a flat stone the size of her palm, the first glyph Frieren had learned that morning.

Seven tries' worth of practice.

One stroke of ink.

When she finished the last small mark, from the left, a light came on in the stone.

Tiny.

Warm.

The size of a candle flame, sitting quietly on grey.

Coco held it up.

"For your grimoire," she said. "It doesn't do anything useful."

Frieren looked at the little light for a long time.

Then she took the stone in both hands, carefully, as if it might spill.

"Those are the best ones," she said.



Mau was at the end of the courtyard when they came back.

He had been there, according to Qifrey, for most of the last hour, pretending to repair a gatepost that did not need repairing.

He saw Coco first, wrapped in Frieren's coat.

He saw her hat.

Perfectly straight.

He looked at Frieren.

Frieren looked back at him and said nothing.

Coco ran ahead.

"She's terrible at it," Coco announced. "Her circles are perfect and her marks are in completely the wrong place."

"I improved," said Frieren.

"She improved," Coco agreed. "And Stark has good hands when he isn't scared, and Fern knew we'd forget lunch."

Stark, behind them, said, "She did," with deep feeling.

Mau looked at the three of them.

At the coat.

At the hat.

At Frieren holding a small glowing stone against her chest like a found thing.

He did not ask what had happened.

He could tell it had not been his to ask about.

That night, Frieren wrote a new page in her grimoire, in her small, careful hand.

Light, drawn.

Useless.

Taught by Coco.

And beneath it, smaller, a note nobody else would read for a long time:

She leans in when she's cold. He's right. You just have to notice.
