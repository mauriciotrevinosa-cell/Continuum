# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 16
## Chapter 26 — Pencil

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 16  
**Reading order:** 26  
**Continuity state:** Ordinary afternoon | Rem writes her name on the narrow room at the turn, in pencil

Rem came to the Muslin on a Thursday with a basket of travelers' notes from the old inn and found the house empty.

Not empty. Never empty. But empty the way a house is in the middle of a working afternoon: everyone out at the worksite, or the washing court, or the water point, or the atelier, or asleep in a loft behind a black curtain. Momo humming faintly in the kitchen. The second bell swinging slightly on its cord where somebody had brushed past it. The common-room ceiling glowing softly over four scratches in the floor, in a light that threw no shadows.

She put the basket on the long table that was not the long table.

She meant to leave.

She went down the family corridor instead.

She did not decide to. Her feet decided, the way they had decided, for weeks now, to take the long way back to the road, past the turn, past the narrow room.

The narrow room was still empty.

It had been empty for weeks. She knew because she checked. She had never admitted to checking. She checked every time.

There was a folded blanket on its shelf. She had put it there herself, a month ago, the day Ori showed her the room and said *you could use it* and Rem said *not yet*. *For guests*, she had said. The blanket was still exactly where she had put it. Nobody had moved it. Nobody had used the room. Nobody had put a marker on its door.

There was a slip of paper pinned to the doorframe, the same as every other room in the Muslin, for a name.

It was blank.

Rem stood in front of it.

She thought about Mau, who had walked her to the cart on the third day of the Move and been so careful not to look at this door that he had nearly walked into the wall. Mau, who had never once asked her. Not when she brought supplies. Not when Momo gave her the second blue-rimmed cup. Not when she stayed late, and later, and once, during the bad rain, overnight on the common-room couch. He wanted to. She could see it on him every single time. He wanted to ask so badly that he went pale with not asking.

He never asked.

She had thought, at first, that it was because he did not want her to stay.

It had taken her a long time to understand that it was because he did.



Fern found her there twenty minutes later, on her way past with a ledger.

Rem was still standing in front of the door.

Fern stopped.

She looked at Rem, and the door, and the blank slip of paper, and she did not say anything, because Fern had learned a long time ago, from a very patient elf, that the most useful thing you could do for someone deciding was to stand nearby and be ordinary.

"Fern," said Rem.

"Mm."

"Whose room is this?"

Fern considered the question honestly.

"Nobody's," she said.

Rem nodded slowly.

Then she held out her hand.

Fern looked at it for a moment. Then she took the pencil from behind her own ear, the one she used for the ledger, and put it in Rem's palm.

Rem wrote her name on the slip of paper.

Small. In pencil. Not in ink. Something that could be rubbed out, if she changed her mind, without anyone having to know.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she handed the pencil back.

"Don't tell him," she said.

"I won't," said Fern.

"He'll see it."

"Probably."

"I just don't want him to make a face."

Fern almost smiled.

"He'll make a face," she said. "He makes faces about everything. You'll have to live with it."

Rem looked at the small pencilled name on the door of the narrow room at the turn of the corridor.

"Yes," she said. "I suppose I will."



Mau saw it that evening, on his way back from the worksite.

He stopped dead in the corridor.

He stood there in front of the narrow room for a long time, reading one small word in pencil, over and over, as if it might change.

Ori, coming the other way, stopped beside him.

She looked at the name. Then at his face.

"You're making a face," said Ori.

"I'm not."

"You are."

Mau put one hand flat against the wall beside the door, very lightly, the way you would touch something that might startle.

"She said yes," he said.

"She said yes," said Ori. "For herself."

Mau nodded.

He did not trust his voice for anything else.

They walked down the corridor together to dinner. Behind them the narrow room stood as empty as it had always been, with a folded blanket on its shelf and a name on its door, waiting for the night someone would finally sleep in it.
