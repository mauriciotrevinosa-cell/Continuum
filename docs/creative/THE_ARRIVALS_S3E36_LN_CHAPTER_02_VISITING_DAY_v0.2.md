# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 16
## Chapter 33 — Visiting Day

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 16  
**Reading order:** 33  
**Continuity state:** Quiet midday, whole household home, visitors from the second community and the native town | Ciel: imminent failure, three patterns adapting together | Seal evacuation protocol | Wheel, Blue/Red orbit, domains begin

It was the quietest day the settlement had had in a month.

That was the first thing everyone would say afterward, the same way they had said *it had been weeks* about the old inn. As if quiet should have counted for something.

No refuge wave. The last of the families from the corridor had moved on to the second community or settled into Arrival House, and the spare bunks stood empty for the first time since the propaganda started. No Searchers out. No construction emergency. No one sick. The whole household was home: the first five who had ever slept under the old inn's roof, and everyone who had come after them, all of them in one place on one ordinary midday for no particular reason.

And there were visitors.

Four of them from the second community, the camp across the river that had survived by staying small and had decided, carefully, that it might be safe to come and look at a settlement that hadn't. With them came an elder from the native town down the valley, a woman Mau had met twice at the corridor talks. She walked with a stick she did not need, and she looked at everything as if she were deciding what to tell her grandchildren about it.

They had come to see the plaza.

That was all. Not a negotiation. Not a treaty. Just *we heard you built something that isn't for anything; can we see it*. Rimuru had said yes before anyone could make it a meeting.



Mau was showing them the water point.

He was not the right person to show anyone the water point. Senku was the right person, or Maomao, or Kusuri, or anyone who could explain the separation stages without pointing vaguely at them and saying *and then it gets cleaner*. But Senku was buried in the workshop and Maomao was with a patient. The visitors had asked, specifically, to meet the person everyone in the corridor kept talking about.

So Mau showed them the water point, and Seiko came along, because the native elder had taken one look at Seiko and Seiko at her and the two of them had become, within about four minutes, the kind of friends who insult each other's walking sticks.

"And this," said Mau, pointing at a pipe, "is a pipe."

"Fascinating," said Seiko.

The elder laughed.

The tallest of the four visitors did not. He was a broad, quiet man who had walked out of his own world with a sword on his back and had not, as far as anyone could tell, been afraid of anything since. He was looking around the plaza with the expression of someone measuring distances to exits, which Mau recognized, because Mikasa wore it every day.

The plaza was full of ordinary things. Children on the play boards. Bocchi in her corner, playing something quiet. Milim losing a game she had invented. A native merchant at the edge of the shade cloth arguing prices with Momo. Rimuru, across the plaza, crouched with Wakana over a bench that wobbled.

Frieren on the bench under the tree, reading.

Mau looked at her, and she looked up at exactly the same moment, the way she sometimes did, as if she had felt him look.

He smiled.

Mau. Master.

Ciel's voice. In both their heads at once. Mau saw Rimuru's head come up on the other side of the plaza.

It was the voice from the old inn kitchen. The one he had heard once, over a jar of salt, and had hoped never to hear again.

Imminent failure.

Mau stopped breathing.

Three things are adapting to the seals. At the same time. They have found the shape of the walls together. A pause, very small. Imminent failure. Thirty seconds. Approximately.



Seiko was standing beside him.

She did not hear Ciel. Nobody but Rimuru and Mau could hear Ciel. But Seiko had lived a very long time among things other people could not hear, and she was looking at Mau's face, and what she saw there she had seen once before, on the morning he had run out of the old inn.

She did not ask.

She turned around, and in a voice that carried across the whole plaza like a slap, she said:

"Emergency evacuation."

Rimuru was already moving.

He had started before she spoke. He was across the plaza, pulling the cord on the defense signal by the tree, and the bell that Mikasa had insisted on and Coco had made sure did not look military rang out over the settlement, three long strokes and one short. The pattern every person in the house had drilled until they could do it asleep. The pattern that meant one thing and one thing only.

Not monsters. Not fire. Not raiders.

Mau.

Everyone moved.

That was what the visitors would remember most afterward, the ones who were able to talk about it at all. Not the thing that came. The way everyone moved. There was no screaming and no running in circles. Fern had the children off the play boards before the third stroke of the bell. Mikasa was at the north route with her arms out, counting. Momo dropped the merchant's goods and took the merchant by the elbow and walked him toward the refuge without a word. Aira scooped up two toddlers. Bocchi stood up from her corner with her guitar in one hand and Kita's hand in the other and went where the drills said to go. Nobody looked back at Mau.

They had promised.

After the shadow, the household had written the shadow protocol. After the shadow protocol, they had written the next one, quietly, over three bad evenings, without telling the visitors or the refuge or anyone outside Ring One. If the seal ever failed, everyone went. Everyone. The only ones who came back were Rimuru, Yuta and Milim, and only after every other person was safe.

Everyone had agreed.

Frieren had agreed.

She had hated agreeing more than she had hated anything in a long time, and she had agreed anyway, because Mau had asked her to, at the old kitchen table, with his hand over hers.

She was not moving now.

She was standing beside the bench under the tree with her book fallen on the ground and her staff already in her hand, looking at him across the plaza.

Milim got to her first.

"Frieren."

"No."

"You promised."

"I know what I promised."

Milim took her by the wrist.

Not gently. Not cruelly either. Simply with the absolute, immovable strength of a being who had once been asked to stop a war with her bare hands and had done it. And she began, step by step, to pull.

Frieren dug in her heels. It made no difference at all.

"Let go of me."

"No," said Milim, and her voice cracked, which Frieren had never heard it do. "You promised him. So I promised you. Walk, or I'll carry you."

Frieren walked.

She walked backward, the whole way, so that she could see him.



Twenty seconds.

The visitors had not moved.

They did not know the bell. They did not know the drill. They stood in the middle of the plaza in a loose knot, the elder with her stick, the three others, and the tall man with the sword, and they watched the most powerful household anyone in the valley had ever heard of scatter in perfect order away from one ordinary-looking man standing next to a water pipe.

Seiko grabbed the elder's arm.

"Move," said Seiko. "Now. Don't look at him. Move."

The elder looked at Seiko's face. Then she moved, faster than her stick would have suggested, and took the two smaller visitors with her.

The tall man did not.

He was looking at Mau's shadow.

It was noon. The sun was nearly straight overhead. Mau's shadow should have been a small dark puddle around his feet.

It was spreading.

It went out across the paving stones of the new plaza, black and flat and wrong, the color of a hole, and from the middle of it, slowly, something rose.

A wheel.

Eight-spoked. Heavy. Pale as old bone, and taller than a man, and turning very slowly on nothing at all. It came up out of the dark and stopped, upright, half above the shadow and half still inside it, and *strained*. Every spoke of it strained to complete the turn that would let it finish rising.

Mau put up his hands.

He did not decide to. His body decided, the way it had decided on the floor of the common room with a dog's muzzle breaking the surface of his shadow. He put up his hands against the wheel without touching it, and the wheel stopped, held, shaking, an eighth of a turn from completing.

Ten seconds.

Above him, the air went blue.

Not the sky. A point. A point of blue, very small and very deep, hanging in the air about a body's length over his head, and the air of the plaza began, very gently, to lean toward it. Leaves from the tree. Dust. The fallen book from Frieren's bench, sliding across the stones.

Then, beside it, red.

Mau knew them both. He had known them since the worst day of his life, when he had stood inside Rimuru and held these two apart for eleven minutes by refusing, and refusing, and refusing. He knew what they wanted. They wanted to touch.

He did not think. There was no time to think.

He made them turn.

He did not know how he did it. He imagined it, the way he had once imagined a room so precisely that his body went to look at it. Two lights circling each other. Not touching. Never touching. Going around and around one shared empty center like two people dancing who must never be allowed to hold hands.

The blue and the red began to orbit.

Slowly at first. Then faster. A ring of blue and red light spinning over his head, too fast now for the eye to separate, so that from below it looked like a single slow-burning wheel of violet.

And Mau stood there in the middle of the new plaza, with one wheel rising out of his shadow and another of light spinning over his head, and held them both.



The tall man with the sword had not moved.

He was looking at Mau the way a man looks at something he has seen in his own world only once, at the end of a long road, in the heart of a mountain everyone else had fled from.

"How," he said, in a voice that did not sound like his own, "can you *live* with a monster like that?"

Nobody answered him.

Nobody was listening. Yuta came past him at a run, going toward the refuge with a child under each arm, and did not even turn his head. Mikasa took the tall man by the back of his collar and simply walked him away, and he let her, because he had forgotten how not to.

Five seconds.

The air around Mau began to cut.

It started at the edge of his shadow and spread outward: thin dark lines appearing in the air from nowhere, crossing and recrossing. Where they crossed, things came apart. A bench was sliced into four clean pieces. The corner of the shade cloth fell, cut, fluttering. A paving stone split in a line so straight it might have been ruled.

And around Mau himself, close, closing, a darkness began to gather. Not the shadow. Something rounder. The beginning of a sphere.

Two domains, coming out of him at once, from opposite sides of the seal, beginning to grind against each other.

Hold, said Ciel. Her voice was perfectly level. Hold a little longer. I am fixing the breach. I am rebuilding the wall around them while you keep them apart. I need time.

"How long?"

I do not know yet.

The sphere was closing around him. The plaza was going dark at the edges. Through the last gap, he saw Frieren at the far edge, at the mouth of the north route, Milim's hand still around her wrist, looking back.

"Nineteen minutes," said Mau. "Remember? At the clearing. Nineteen minutes, and then I faint."

A pause.

We might cut it close, said Ciel.

"I don't know if I can hold everything for nineteen minutes."

Another pause.

Understood, said Ciel.

The sphere closed.
