# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 16
## Chapter 35 — What the Visitors Saw

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 16  
**Reading order:** 35  
**Continuity state:** Aftermath, days 1-3 and the weeks after | Visitors leave; second community moves visits to the bridge | First distancing of the outer ring (Ring Four after V17) | The household grows closer

Mau slept for two days.

Not a coma. Maomao was very firm about that, to everyone who asked, which was everyone. His body had done in nineteen minutes what it had taken weeks to learn to do in the clearing, and then a great deal more, and it was simply refusing to do anything else until it had been paid back. His fingertips firmed by the first evening. The eye closed on the second morning. The fever never came at all.

Frieren did not leave the room.

Nobody asked her to.



The visitors left on the first day.

They had spent the night in the refuge with everyone else, because nobody had been willing to let anyone walk the river road in the dark after that. In the morning they stood at the edge of the plaza with Rimuru and looked at it.

The paving stones crossed with straight fine cuts, as if someone had drawn on them with a ruler made of nothing. The benches in pieces. The shade cloth hanging in ribbons. The tree in a hundred neat sections on the ground, with its stump in the middle sliced flat as a table.

Somebody had already left flowers on the stump. Nobody knew who.

The native elder looked at all of it for a long time, leaning on the stick she did not need.

"In my grandmother's time," she said at last, "there was a man in the next valley who could call lightning. Not much. A little. Enough to light a fire. Then one summer it came out of him wrong and burned his house and his wife's house and half the grain." She looked at Rimuru. "His village sent him away. That's what the story says. That they sent him up the mountain with food and told him not to come back."

Rimuru did not say anything.

"My grandmother said they were wrong," said the elder. "She said the lightning was already in him when they loved him. Sending him up the mountain didn't take the lightning out. It only took them away from it." She turned her stick in the dirt. "She also said she would not have wanted to live next to him."

"Both of those are true," said Rimuru quietly.

"Yes," said the elder. "That's the trouble with true things."

She went home to tell her grandchildren what she had seen. Nobody ever found out exactly what she told them.



The tall man with the sword did not say goodbye.

He stood with the second community's delegation at the river road and did not look back at the settlement once. Yuta, who had come to see them off because somebody had to, walked with them as far as the bridge.

At the bridge, the tall man stopped.

"I saw a thing like that once," he said, without turning around. "In my world. At the end of everything. Everyone fled from it. I was the one they sent to kill it." He was quiet for a moment. "I didn't."

"Why not?" said Yuta.

"Because it was asleep," said the tall man. "And I was a coward." He looked at the water. "Your friend wasn't asleep. He was holding it. All of it. With his hands." He finally turned his head and looked at Yuta, and his face was grey. "I asked how you could live with a monster. I meant him. I want you to know I meant him. I still don't know if I was wrong."

Yuta looked at him for a long time.

"He's not the monster," he said. "He's the one holding it."

"Then what's the difference," said the tall man, "if he ever lets go?"

He crossed the bridge without waiting for an answer.



The second community sent a letter three days later. It was polite and careful, and it thanked the settlement for its hospitality. It said the corridor partnership would continue.

It also said that, for the time being, future visits would be held at the bridge.

Fern read it twice and put it in the ledger without comment.



In the settlement, things did not change all at once.

That was almost worse.

The plaza was rebuilt within a week. Wakana and Agott laid new stones, and Coco scratched a small mark into the corner of each one with a nail. Not a glyph; there was no ink to spare. It did nothing except make them hers. A new tree was planted beside the stump, which nobody had the heart to pull up. Children came back to the play boards within two days, because children always did.

But a family from Arrival House who had been talking about settling for good took the bridge road to the second community instead, and did not explain why.

A native merchant who used to sell at the edge of the shade cloth started setting up his stall at the far end of the washing court instead. Further from the east end of the shell. He was perfectly friendly when Momo went to buy from him. He simply did not come to the plaza anymore.

A mother called her son back from the play boards one afternoon, when Mau, still pale, still walking slowly, came and sat on the new bench to watch. She did not do it rudely. She did it the way you call a child back from a road. Then she saw that Mau had seen, and her face went red, and she did not look at him again.

In the second community, and the native town, and along the river road, the story went around. It did not get worse as it went, not yet. But it did not get better either. *He's the one holding it*, some people said. *What's the difference*, said others, *if he ever lets go?*

And somewhere much farther away, in a town whose walls had posters about Otherworlders on them, somebody heard a version of it from a traveler and wrote it down. They put it in a drawer with other things they had written down, and waited.



Mau woke on the third morning to Frieren asleep in the chair beside the bed, Rimuru asleep in the loft above it, and the east window full of light.

He lay there for a while, not moving.

"Ciel," he said finally.

I'm here.

"The voice."

Yes.

"At the end. Before I fell. You heard it too."

Yes. A pause. Rule five. I am telling you. I heard it. I do not know whose it was. It was not the dog. It was not the wheel. It was not anything I counted when I counted the seal. I have checked every pattern under the wall three times since. Nothing in there has a voice.

Mau stared at the ceiling.

"Then what said it?"

I do not know, said Ciel. I have told Master that I do not know. I will tell you both when I do.

"And the wall?"

It holds, said Ciel. It is stronger than it was. A pause, longer. It has also learned that it can be breached. So have they. I would like you to know that, and not be afraid of it, and I know that is two things that do not fit together.

Mau almost laughed.

"Yeah," he said. "That's every day now."

Beside the bed, Frieren opened her eyes.

She did not say anything. She looked at him for a long time, to make sure. Then she reached over and took his hand, the one that had held a wheel, and turned it over and looked at the fingertips, one at a time, as if counting them.

All there.

She put his hand down on the blanket and kept hers over it.

"You made it spin," she said.

"I imagined it."

"I know," said Frieren. "I watched you."

Above them, in the loft, Rimuru had woken and was pretending very hard that he had not.



The household got better after that.

That was the strange part. That was the part none of them would have predicted. For weeks afterward, the house was closer than it had ever been. More people at roof night. More people at breakfast. Fern relaxed her rules about the common room. Umaru taught Milim three new games. The seal held, and held, and held. Mau's fingertips stayed fingertips, and the second elf trial, when they finally risked it, ran longer than the first.

It was good. It was real, and it was good.

The cuts in the plaza stones never quite came out, no matter how many times Wakana sanded them.

And the merchant never came back to the shade cloth.
