# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 16
## Chapter 41 — Story Night, Again

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 16  
**Reading order:** 41  
**Continuity state:** Storm, day 1 | Marin forces story night | Mau has stories now (the roof, the deer) | The Sein story; Himmel survived | Frieren starts a plan

The storm came in off the hills on a Tuesday and announced, through the roof, that it intended to stay.

By noon the river road was mud. By two the worksite was a lake. By three Marin had walked into the common room, looked at forty people pretending to be busy with nothing, and climbed onto a chair.

"Get down," said Fern, without looking up.

Marin did not get down.

"Nobody works today," Marin announced. "And nobody leaves this room. It's raining, so we're doing stories."

"We did this," said Momo. "At the old inn. It was a disaster."

"It was a disaster because we didn't commit," said Marin. "Today we commit. Everybody tells one. Nobody leaves until everybody's told one. I'll go first, so nobody can say I made them do something I wouldn't."

She told the story of the first costume she had ever made, which had fallen apart on stage in front of two hundred people. She told it so well that Wakana, who had been there, put his face in his hands and laughed until he wheezed.

Then it went around the room.



Rimuru told the one about the time a goblin village had tried to name him king and he had panicked and agreed to everything. Bocchi told one about a live house where she had played the entire set facing the wall, and the crowd had cheered anyway, and she had never found out why. Okarun told one, and Momo corrected every detail of it, and by the end they were telling it together, shouting over each other. Seiko told one about a ghost who had haunted a public bath for forty years because nobody had ever thanked it for keeping the water warm. Nobody could tell whether she was joking. Suika told one about Senku that Senku denied in its entirety, which made it better.

Mau sat on the couch with Frieren's feet in his lap and listened.

He had done this before.

At the old inn, a long time ago, the first time the house had told stories at dinner. Everyone had had something: a city, a band, ten years with a hero. Every story had brought another person with it, another place, another life. And when it came round to him, there had been nothing. No story waiting. Facts with nothing behind them. He had gone outside into the dark and not come back for an hour.

The circle came round to him.

The room went a little quiet. Some of them remembered. Bocchi did. Yuta did. Frieren's foot went still in his lap.

Mau thought about it.

"Okay," he said. "The roof."

Rimuru groaned.

"No."

"The roof with the hole," said Mau. "At the old inn. The first month. Before most of you were here." He leaned back. "Rimuru said he could fix it in an afternoon."

"I could have fixed it in an afternoon."

"He went up with one board. One. He said, 'the trick is to not overthink it,' and stepped right through the hole and landed on the kitchen table in front of Frieren, and Frieren—"

"Didn't look up," said Yuta, grinning.

"Didn't look up from her book. She turned a page. And then she said—"

"'The roof is up there,'" said Frieren, from the other end of the couch, in exactly the same flat voice, without lifting her eyes from her current book.

The whole room lost it.

Mau told the rest. Yuta fixed the hole in the end, with Mau holding the ladder and Rimuru, in slime form, offended, supervising from a bucket. They found a bird's nest in the rafters and spent two days moving it rather than throw it out. Frieren pretended not to care about the birds and fed them every morning.

"Tell the deer one," said Bocchi from the floor.

Yuta put his face in his hands.

"No."

"The deer," said Mau, with enormous seriousness, "is still out there."

Rimuru looked around.

"What deer?"

That made it worse. It had made it worse the first time too, at the old inn, a year and a half and a whole other building ago. Yuta explained, with the exhausted patience of a man who had been explaining for a year and a half: there had been a deer, once, in the first month. Mau had seen it twice in the trees on the north trail and had decided, on no evidence whatsoever, that it was following him.

"It was," said Mau.

"It ran away from you."

"That's what it wants you to think."

Bocchi was crying with laughter. Frieren, at the far end of the couch, did not look up from her book, but the corner of her mouth moved.

When he finished, the room was warm with it.

He looked down and found Bocchi watching him from the floor with an expression he could not read. Then she looked away, very fast, and wiped her face on her sleeve, and pretended she had not.

He had a story now.

He had a lot of them. He had not noticed when that happened.



Fern and Stark told the last one, because Marin made them.

It was about a priest.

"He drank," said Fern.

"He smoked," said Stark.

"He gambled."

"He was good, though," said Stark. "At being a priest. When he wanted to be."

"His name was Sein," said Fern. "He traveled with us for a while. He was looking for a friend."

"And he had," said Stark, very carefully, "opinions. About women."

"Older women," said Fern.

"Specifically older women," said Stark. "He said so. A lot."

There was a short, thoughtful pause.

Then, slowly, every head in the common room turned to look at Frieren, who was more than a thousand years old, and who was reading.

Frieren turned a page.

"It didn't work," she said.

The room exploded.

"He didn't look at me twice," said Frieren, over the noise, perfectly calm. "Not once. He said I was like a grandmother who had forgotten how to be one." She turned another page. "I've decided it was his loss."

"It was," said Stark loyally.

"With Himmel, though," Frieren went on, as if continuing a thought about weather, "it worked. He nearly died."

At the back of the room, by the window that faced the road, Himmel put down his cup.

Every head turned the other way.

Himmel considered the room for a long moment, with great dignity.

"I'd like it noted," he said, "that I survived."

"Barely," said Frieren.

"Barely," Himmel agreed, and smiled. It was the kind of smile that had been at peace with a great many things for a very long time. The room laughed until Momo had to sit down on the floor.



The storm went on all evening.

It went on into the night, hammering the roof of the Muslin like it had a grudge. It was still going when Frieren lay in bed under the east window, beside Mau, who had fallen asleep the way he always did after a good day: all at once, mid-sentence, with one arm across her.

Above them, in the loft, Rimuru was snoring softly, like the sea.

Frieren did not sleep.

She lay there and thought about Sein, which she had not done in a very long time. *Like a grandmother who had forgotten how to be one.* It had not bothered her then. She had not cared what he thought, or what anyone thought, about how she looked. It had not been a thing she had ever needed to know.

She thought about Himmel, a long time ago, turning red to the ears every time she so much as handed him a cup.

And she thought about Mau.

Mau loved her. She knew that the way she knew her own spells. He told her. He kissed her. He looked for her in every room, and when he found her he stayed. He held her hand at the table as if it were a thing hands were for. He made flowers in a clearing with his own mana because he wanted, once, to be the one who did.

All of that was love.

She was very old. She had been a great many places and seen a great many people. She was not a fool. She knew there was another thing, separate from love and next to it. A thing that made people look at someone across a room and forget what they were saying.

Himmel had had it, with her. It had nearly killed him.

She had never once, in a thousand years, wondered whether she could cause it on purpose. She had never needed to.

She wondered now.

She looked at Mau asleep beside her, one arm across her, his mouth slightly open, and wondered whether he had ever looked at her across a room and forgotten what he was saying.

She did not know.

That was intolerable.

Frieren lay awake in the storm and, for the first time in a very long life, began to plan something that was not a spell.
