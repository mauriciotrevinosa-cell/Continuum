# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 16
## Chapter 42 — The Super Duper Ultra Archi-Re-Que-Contra Mega Important Meeting

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 16  
**Reading order:** 42  
**Continuity state:** Storm, day 2 | Frieren calls every woman in the house | 'Is Mau attracted to me?' becomes a plan | Senku's cup telephones | Rimuru kidnapped for Ciel

On the second morning of the storm, Diablo was standing outside the common room door with his hands folded behind his back and an expression of serene, total authority.

"I am afraid," he told Senku, "that I cannot let you pass."

"I left my notebook on the table."

"I am deeply sorry for your loss."

"It's not lost. It's on the table. I can see it."

"Nobody may enter," said Diablo, "until the Super Duper Ultra Archi-Re-Que-Contra Mega Important Meeting has concluded."

Senku stared at him.

"The what."

"The sisters chose the name," said Diablo, with deep respect. "They felt it needed to communicate the importance."

The phrase *archi-re-que-contra* had been stolen from Mau, who had once said something was archi-re-que-contra important and refused, ever since, to explain what it meant. The younger half of the household had adopted it with the fervor of people who had found a weapon.

Senku looked past Diablo at the closed door. Then at the corridor. Then back at Diablo, who was smiling at him with the patience of a demon who had already outlasted several civilizations and was happy to outlast Senku too.

Senku went to get a different notebook.



Inside, the common room was full of women.

Not some of them. All of them. Every girl and woman in the household, and several who were only sort of in the household, crammed onto the couch and the floor and the long table and every chair in the room, because Frieren had gone door to door at dawn and told each one, individually and without explanation, that she was required.

Fern. Ori. Coco, Agott and Tetia, and Richeh, who was still technically a guest and had come anyway. Umaru and Suika, sharing one cushion. Milim, upside down on the arm of the couch. Bocchi, Kita, Nijika, and Ryo, who had come for the snacks. Momo and Aira and Vamola. Seiko, who had brought tea. Turbo Granny, who had brought opinions. Maki, Mai, Mikasa. Marin, practically vibrating. Anko with a notebook open and a pen already moving. Rem, by the window. Hakari, Karane, Shizuka, Nano, and Kusuri, in one heap. Maomao, sitting at the end of the long table with her arms folded, radiating the specific fury of a woman who had been about to start a culture and had been physically escorted here by Milim.

And Nazuna.

Nazuna was awake.

At eight in the morning, in daylight, with the storm going outside. Nazuna, who did not wake for anything before sundown, who had slept through an entire day once and laughed about it, was sitting on the floor with a blanket over her head like a hood, blinking at the room.

"This had better be good," said Nazuna.

The last time the girls of the house had held a meeting this serious, it had ended with Mau being carried somewhere against his will. Everyone remembered. Several people looked at the door, as if he might be brought in.

He was not.

Nobody had kidnapped anybody. Frieren had called this one herself.

She was standing at the head of the long table. She had her grimoire under one arm and a roll of paper under the other, and her face was doing nothing at all.

"Thank you for coming," said Frieren.

"Was it optional?" said Maomao.

"No."

Frieren put the roll of paper down on the table. She did not unroll it.

"I have a question," she said.

The room went quiet. Even Milim stopped swinging her legs.

Frieren looked at the window, at the rain, for a moment. Then back at all of them.

"How do I know," she said, "whether Mau thinks I'm attractive?"



There was a silence so complete that the rain on the roof sounded like applause.

Then about nine people started talking at once.

"He WHAT—"

"Is he blind—"

"Did he SAY something—"

"I will end him," said Mikasa, very calmly, and started to stand up. Aira pulled her back down by the belt.

"You're beautiful," said Kita, with total conviction. "You're so beautiful. Have you seen you?"

"He's an idiot," said Momo. "All men are idiots. This is known."

"Let her finish," said Fern sharply, and the noise dropped.

Frieren had not moved.

"He hasn't said anything wrong," she said. "That's not the problem. He tells me he loves me. He kisses me. He looks for me in every room. When he finds me, he stays." She paused. "I know he loves me. I don't need help with that."

"Then what—" said Ori.

"I don't know," said Frieren, "if he finds me attractive."

She said it plainly, the way she would have described a spell that was not working.

"Those are different things," said Frieren. "I'm old. I'm not stupid. I've seen a lot of people, and I know they're different things. Himmel nearly died of the second one, and I never noticed until it was too late to matter." She looked down at the table. "I never needed to know if I could do it. I never wanted to. Now I want to know, and I don't know how to find out, and I've never not known how to find something out."

The room was very quiet.

"Also," said Fern, after a moment, slowly, "you once told me the best birthday present for Stark would be a spell that dissolves clothes."

Several people choked.

"That was practical," said Frieren.

"It was not practical."

"It was a little practical."

"The point," said Fern, red to the ears, "is that you understand the concept. You've always understood the concept. You just never cared."

"Yes," said Frieren. "And now I care. It's very inconvenient."

Maki, on the couch, had started smiling, slowly. She was a woman who had lived an entire life with someone, and had grandchildren in another world, and had nothing at all to learn about this subject from anyone in this room.

"Okay," said Maki. "So what do you need? Reassurance?"

Everyone leaned forward. Fifteen women took a breath, ready to tell Frieren exactly how attractive she was and exactly how blind Mau would have to be not to notice.



Frieren's face changed.

It was very small. Most people would not have seen it. But Fern had known her a long time, and Fern saw it, and Fern's stomach dropped. She knew that face. It was the face Frieren wore right before she did something for which there was no adequate apology.

It was smug.

"I didn't come here for reassurance," said Frieren. "I didn't come here to be told things."

She unrolled the paper on the long table.

It went on for some time.

It went off the end of the table and onto the floor, and Milim, delighted, kept unrolling it, until it reached halfway to the door. It was covered from end to end in Frieren's small precise handwriting, with diagrams, and timings, and arrows, and in one place what appeared to be a map of the Muslin with routes marked in red.

"I came here," said Frieren, "because I need assistance with a plan."



She explained the plan.

She began by telling a story. Most of them had not heard it, because most of them had not been at the old inn in the first months. It was the story of the day Maomao had made a chocolate in the workroom: mild, carefully dosed, meant to lower inhibition a little and nothing else. It did not create wants that were not there; it only took away the part of you that stopped you from acting on them. And somebody, through no fault of her own, had eaten it by mistake.

Frieren had.

She told the whole thing. The hoodie. The cap. Sitting closer than there was any reason to sit. Fern walking in at exactly the wrong moment. Frieren had never once told this story in her own voice, and she told it now perfectly evenly, as if it had happened to somebody else.

At the end of the long table, Maomao had begun, very quietly, to laugh.

She tried to stop. She put her hand over her mouth. It did not help. It came out of her in small helpless bursts, and then bigger ones, until she had her forehead down on the table and her shoulders were shaking.

"I remember," Maomao managed. "I remember. Your face. Fern's face—"

"Yes," said Frieren. "I remember too. I've been remembering it for two years."

She put one finger on the very first line of the plan, where it said, in her neat hand, at the top:

PHASE ONE.

"So," said Frieren. "I think it's his turn."



After that, the meeting became a meeting.

It was, everyone agreed afterward, the most productive meeting the household had ever held. It was more productive than the council, or the water argument, or any of the six evenings it would one day take to draw the real house. Everyone had an opinion. For once, every opinion was useful.

Maomao, once she had stopped laughing, said she would not do it. Then she said she would do it on three conditions. Then she said it would be a smaller dose than last time, because Mau had a sealed pattern in him and nobody was going to find out what an uninhibited Construct beat did. Then she wrote the dose down, and underlined it twice.

Umaru said it had to be a covert operation. Coco said that meant costumes. Marin said she would handle costumes, and then said Wakana would handle costumes, and went to find Wakana.

Momo said the chocolate had to come from someone Mau would never suspect. Everyone looked at Umaru. Umaru looked insulted, then flattered, then accepted.

Anko took minutes in a code nobody else could read.

Mikasa planned the routes, because she could not help it. It turned out the Muslin had eleven ways to get from the kitchen to the common room without being seen, and she had already known about nine of them.

Kita said somebody had to get Mau out of the house for the afternoon, so Maomao would have time. Bocchi, Coco and Umaru volunteered as the sisters at once, before anyone else could. They said they would take him on a quest. Nobody asked what kind of quest. You did not ask Umaru about her quests.

Then Nano said, in her small flat voice:

"We need walkie-talkies."

Everyone looked at her.

"For coordination," said Nano. "The house is long. Signals must be passed."

"We don't have walkie-talkies," said Ori.

"We need them," said Nano, "urgently."



Outside the door, Senku had come back with his other notebook and was explaining to Diablo, at length, why the first notebook was scientifically necessary.

The door opened.

Frieren looked at him.

Senku looked back.

He saw the room behind her: thirty women, a map, a roll of paper on the floor that went halfway to the door, Milim upside down, and Nazuna awake in daylight. Every face in the room had turned toward him with the same expression.

Frieren took him by the sleeve.

"Walkie-talkies," she said.

Senku opened his mouth.

He shut it again.

His face went through, in rapid succession, the expressions of a scientist realizing that a battle has not only been lost but was lost before he arrived, and that the correct course is acceptance.

"I don't have the parts," he said finally.

"Make them," said Frieren.

He made them out of cups and string.

It took him twenty minutes. Paper cups from the kitchen, waxed against the damp, with long lines of waxed thread strung between them along the ceilings of the Muslin's corridors on little hooks. It was a whole network, tin-can telephones run end to end through the house, so that someone at the turn of the corridor could whisper into a cup and be heard in the common room. It was crude. It was absurd. It worked perfectly. Senku looked at it afterward with an expression that suggested he did not know whether to be proud or ashamed, and had decided to be both.

He was not allowed to leave the meeting after that. He knew too much.



There was one piece left.

"The seal," said Maomao. "If he's uninhibited, everything in him is a little less held. I want someone watching."

Everyone knew who could watch.

Nobody wanted to say it, because saying it meant bringing Rimuru into the meeting, and Rimuru would ask why, and the whole point was that—

Milim got up off the arm of the couch, walked out of the common room, past Diablo, and came back thirty seconds later carrying Rimuru under one arm like a sack of flour.

Rimuru was in slime form, and very confused.

"Milim," he was saying, "we have talked about this. You can't just pick people up and carry them places. It's called kidnapping. It's—"

He stopped.

He had seen the room.

Milim set him down on the long table, on top of the plan. He looked around, slowly, at thirty women, a demon at the door, a defeated scientist holding a paper cup on a string, and Nazuna awake in daylight.

"...What is this," said Rimuru.

"We don't need you," said Frieren.

Rimuru blinked.

"Then why am I—"

"We need Ciel."

Rimuru looked at Frieren. He looked at the plan under him, which he was sitting on. He looked at Senku, who met his eyes and shook his head very slightly, in the manner of one prisoner to another.

Then he looked at the room, which had turned toward him with thirty identical expressions of *please*.

"...What," said Rimuru carefully, "do you need Ciel for?"

"We're going to do something tomorrow," said Frieren. "To Mau. Nothing bad. Nothing dangerous. But he's going to be busy. A little more open than usual. We need Ciel watching the walls the whole time, extra carefully, so that nothing in him is, either."

Rimuru was quiet for a long moment.

Inside him, very dryly, Ciel said something.

Rimuru closed his eyes.

"She says," he reported, "that she would like it noted she was not consulted about the meeting. And that she is in."

The room cheered.

Diablo, outside, smiled with deep satisfaction and did not move from the door.

Senku put the paper cup to his ear, listened to the noise coming down the string from the common room, and sighed the sigh of a man who would be telling no one about this, ever, for the rest of his life.

The storm went on.

It was going to last at least two more days. Frieren had checked.

She had planned for that too.
