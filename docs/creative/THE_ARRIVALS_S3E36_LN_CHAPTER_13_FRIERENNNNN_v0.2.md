# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 16
## Chapter 44 — Frierennnnn

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 16  
**Reading order:** 44  
**Continuity state:** Storm, day 3, afternoon to midnight | Golden-retriever Mau | Lovebird Hours | The deer was her | Frieren flirts, badly

Mau looked at Frieren in the green dress for a long, long moment.

Thirty people leaned forward.

Frieren lifted her chin a quarter of an inch, which for her was the equivalent of a fanfare.

And Mau's whole face lit up like a lamp.

"*Frierennnnnn*," he said.

He crossed the room in about three steps. He did not look at the dress. He did not look at the hair. He did not seem to see anything about her at all, except that she was there. He dropped onto the couch beside her so hard the cushions bounced, put both arms around her, and buried his face in her shoulder with a sound of pure, uncomplicated contentment, like a dog that has been let in out of the rain.

Frieren sat very still inside the hug.

Her face did nothing.

"...He didn't notice the dress," she said, to the room, flatly.

"He noticed you," said Kita, at once, from the floor.

"That's not the experiment."

Mau, with his face in her shoulder, said something muffled that sounded very much like *you smell like the library*.

Frieren's ears went pink at the tips.



It did not stop.

That was the thing everyone would remember afterward. The rest of the day, and the evening, and well into the night, it did not stop for a single minute.

Wherever Frieren went, Mau went. She got up to put her book back on the shelf, and he got up with her and stood behind her with his chin on top of her head while she chose another one. She went to the kitchen for tea, and he followed her to the kitchen and stood so close that Momo had to cook around them. She sat back down, and he sat down so close that there was no room at all between them, and then a little closer than that.

He told her things. He had never stopped telling her things, exactly, but now he said all of them, one after another, with nothing in between to stop them. That he liked the way she frowned at a spell before she cast it. That she hummed when she read and did not know she did it. That the first morning he could remember, in the clearing, before he knew his own name, he had looked up and seen white hair and thought, *oh*. Just that. *Oh.* And that he had been thinking it, in one way or another, every day since.

Frieren listened to all of it with her face doing nothing at all, and her ears getting steadily pinker.

At one point she turned her head away to say something to Fern.

Mau kissed her cheek while she was not looking.

She turned back.

He was looking at the ceiling, extremely innocently.

"Did you just—"

"No."

"You did."

"Prove it."

Across the room, Rimuru put his slime-face into his slime-hands.



At around six, Frieren gave up pretending to have a plan for the evening. She picked up her book again and actually tried to read it.

Mau lay down across her.

Not beside her. Across. His head in her lap and the rest of him spread along the couch, entirely without dignity, the way a large dog arranges itself across the person it has chosen, looking up at her with an expression of complete peace.

He did not say anything. He just lay there and looked at her while she read.

The whole common room watched to see what Frieren would do.

Frieren did not complain.

Frieren did not move him.

Frieren held her book a little higher, so that her face was mostly behind it. From behind the book, quietly at first and then not quietly at all, she began to smile. It was not her small, sideways smile, the one that barely moved her mouth. It was a real one, wide and helpless. It spread until it reached her ears, and her ears went red, and under the book her feet began, without her permission, to kick. Little kicks, against the arm of the couch, like a girl who had been given exactly what she wanted for her birthday and was trying very hard to be dignified about it.

Fern, by the window, stared.

In all the years she had known Frieren, she had never once seen her do anything with her feet.



"Nobody," said Umaru, standing up on the long table, "comment."

"Get down," said Fern automatically, without looking away from Frieren.

Umaru did not get down.

She had her AGENT HAMSTER coat on, and the collar turned up, and an expression of terrible authority. Behind her, on the floor, stood Milim in her AGENT DRAGON coat, arms folded, nodding seriously at everything Umaru said.

"These," said Umaru, "are Lovebird Hours."

"Lovebird Hours," repeated Milim, gravely.

"During Lovebird Hours there will be no commenting. There will be no teasing. There will be no noises."

Several people had been about to make a noise. They did not make it.

"Anyone who interrupts Lovebird Hours," said Umaru, "will be removed by Agent Dragon."

Milim cracked her knuckles.

Diablo, by the door, closed it softly and stood in front of it, smiling the smile of a demon who had been given a clear mission and intended to enjoy every second of it. When Okarun, a little later, opened his mouth with the clear intention of saying something about it, Diablo simply looked at him. Okarun closed his mouth and went to help in the kitchen for the rest of the evening.

Every so often, at the edge of things, something quiet checked in.

Walls steady, said Ciel, inside Rimuru. He is very happy. That is not a guardrail matter. A pause. Neither is the kicking. I have been asked about the kicking.

"Who asked about the kicking?"

Ciel did not answer that.



It was late in the evening, with the storm still going and the lamps low, when Mau, still on the couch with his head in her lap, said, out of nowhere:

"You said maybe it followed me home."

Frieren lowered her book.

"What?"

"The deer. At the old inn. The first month. I kept talking about the deer, and Yuta kept telling me to stop, and you said, 'Maybe it followed you home.'"

Frieren was quiet for a moment.

"I remember," she said.

"I think it did," said Mau. He looked up at her, perfectly serious, the way he had been perfectly serious about the deer for two years. "I think it was you."

Frieren stared at him.

"I'm not a deer."

"You're better than a deer."

"That's not a compliment."

"It's the best compliment I have," said Mau. "That deer was the most important thing that ever happened to me. Until you."

Frieren put the book back up in front of her face.

Her feet started kicking again.



They went to bed a little before midnight. Mau was still holding her hand, and still talking, and walked into the door frame on the way because he was looking at her instead of the door.

In their room, under the east window, with the storm on the roof and Rimuru tactfully pretending to be asleep in the loft, Frieren tried one more thing.

She had a plan for this part too. It was the last page of the very long roll of paper. She had been saving it.

She sat on the edge of the bed in the green dress. She took the silver pins out of her hair, one at a time, slowly, and let it fall. She looked at him sideways, through it, the way she had seen people look at each other across rooms for a thousand years, people she had never understood and never wanted to.

"Mau," she said, and made her voice lower than usual.

Mau looked at her.

"Do you think," said Frieren, "that I'm—"

"Cute," said Mau, instantly, with total sincerity. "So cute. You're so cute right now. You're doing a thing with your voice."

Frieren stopped.

"I'm not doing a thing with my voice."

"You're doing a thing. It's very cute."

"It's not supposed to be *cute*."

"What's it supposed to be?"

Frieren opened her mouth.

She had read about this. She had watched other people do it for centuries. She knew the shape of it perfectly.

She had never once done it herself. She had no idea how it was supposed to go.

She leaned closer and took hold of his collar, the way she had seen someone do once in a tavern a very long time ago, and pulled him a little toward her, and said, as low as she could:

"Come here."

Mau came there, very happily.

Then he said, delighted, from about an inch away:

"Are you *flirting* with me?"

Frieren held very still.

"...Yes."

"You're terrible at it."

"I've been doing it for one day."

"I love it," said Mau. "You're so bad at it. Do it again."

Frieren let go of his collar.

She looked at him for a long time, with her hair down and her ears red and her plan in pieces. He beamed at her. He clearly thought this was the best night of his life, and had clearly not understood the question at all.

"I'm going to change," said Frieren, with great dignity, and went to the closet.
