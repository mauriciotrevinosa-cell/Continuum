# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 16
## Chapter 45 — Green Eyes

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 16  
**Reading order:** 45  
**Continuity state:** Storm, night 3 | The hoodie, like the first time | 'The most beautiful person, place or thing' | 'Idiot' | The tree promise

She came back from the closet defeated.

She had taken off the green dress and folded it back into its paper, the way she had folded it for however many centuries it had lived at the bottom of a bag. She had taken the last pin out of her hair. She had stood in front of the closet for a while, looking at the shelf of clothes that were hers and the much larger shelf of clothes that were his, and then, out of habit, out of comfort, out of the specific tiredness of a plan that has failed at the very end, she had put on his hoodie.

The black-and-orange one. Too big in the shoulders, sleeves past her fingers. And her own short sleeping shorts, and nothing on her feet.

The same clothes she had been wearing the first time.

The day of the first chocolate, at the old inn, two years ago, when she had come downstairs in his hoodie and his cap without stopping to think whether it would look strange, and the whole table had gone quiet.

She did not think about that now. She was too tired. She walked back across the room, past the shelf that was in the wrong place, with her hair loose and the sleeves hanging, climbed onto the bed under the east window, and lay down with her back to him.

"It didn't work," she said into the pillow.

Mau had been lying on his side, waiting for her.

He was very tired now too. Whatever Maomao's chocolate was doing to him had worn him out over ten hours of following a person around a house. His eyes were half shut.

They opened.

He looked at her.

He looked at her for a long time. Frieren in his hoodie, with her hair spread across the pillow and the sleeves over her hands, lying with her back to him and her ears drooping. Something happened in his face that nobody was there to see. Rimuru, in the loft, would describe it later as *like watching someone fall down a well, except the well was nice*.

Frieren felt him looking.

She turned her head.

"What," she said. "What are you looking at."

Mau did not answer right away.

When he did, he said it slowly. Not the way he had been saying things all day, tumbling out one after another. Carefully. As if he had been carrying it for a long time and had only just found the place to set it down.

"I don't know if I tell you often enough," he said. "I don't think I do. I think I say *I love you* and I think that covers it, and it doesn't. So I'm going to say it now, while I can."

Frieren went very still.

"You're the most beautiful person I've ever seen," said Mau. "Or place. Or thing. Anything. From the very first second. I opened my eyes in that clearing, and I didn't know my name, I didn't know anything, and you were the first thing there was. And I've never seen anything more beautiful since. Not once. Nothing has even come close." His voice went quiet. "Your eyes. That green. I have never in my life seen anything as beautiful as your eyes."

Frieren did not say anything.

She could not.

Her face, which had been doing nothing for a thousand years, went red. Not her ears. All of it. Her cheeks and her forehead and her neck, under the collar of his hoodie, a slow, total, helpless red. It spread until she was the color of the red light that had burst over the plaza like a firework.

She opened her mouth.

She closed it.

She opened it again.

"*Idiot*," said Frieren.

Mau smiled, and closed his eyes, and was asleep about three seconds later.



Frieren lay there.

She lay there for a long time, red to the roots of her hair, with her heart doing something it had not done in a thousand years. Not even with Himmel. With Himmel she had never noticed in time.

She had noticed this time.

She did not know what to do with it, so eventually she did the only thing that occurred to her. She turned over, very carefully, and curled up small against his chest, inside his arm, with her face in his shirt.

In his sleep, Mau's arm closed around her.

And then, mostly asleep, in a voice that was hardly a voice, he said into her hair:

"You said you'd be there for the tree."

Frieren held very still.

"In a hundred years," said Mau. "When it's big enough to sit under. You said, *I will*. In the atelier door." He was barely awake. "I'm going to find a way to be there too. With you. I promise."

Frieren did not answer.

She did not trust anything she might say.

"You can't take it back now," murmured Mau. "I heard you. I'm going to be there."

Then he was properly asleep. His breathing slowed, and his arm was heavy and warm around her, and the storm went on and on over the roof of the Muslin.

Frieren lay awake against his chest in his hoodie for a very long time.

She had come to bed thinking her plan had failed.

She went to sleep, eventually, with something in her chest she did not have a name for. It was not like anything she had felt before, in a thousand years of feeling very little. It was not quite happiness. It was larger than that, and more frightening. It felt like standing at the edge of the clearing, very early in the morning, with the frost lifting and something about to begin.

Up in the loft, Rimuru lay in slime form with his eyes wide open in the dark, not making a sound.

Mau's heart rate, said Ciel softly, inside him, is normal. Hers is not. I am not noting it. A pause. I am noting it a little.
