# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 17
## Chapter 10 — The Big One

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 17  
**Reading order:** 10  
**Continuity state:** Eren guest status day 2 | Stark's birthday | Household ordinary life seen by a newcomer

Fern had known the date for weeks.

She had not told anyone.

That was not unusual. Fern knew a great many dates. Market days, review dates, the day the western drain was due to be cleared, the day a guest's three-day status ran out. She kept them all in the same small book, in the same neat hand.

This one had been circled since before the move.

Two days before it, she came to Mau in the kitchen, closed the door behind her, and said:

"I need meat ground very fine."

Mau looked up from the onion he was failing to cut properly.

"How fine?"

"Finer than a knife."

"For what?"

Fern was quiet for a moment.

"Stark's birthday," she said. "On the road, once, Frieren and I made him a hamburg steak. A big one. His brother used to make him one, when he'd done something brave. It's—" She stopped. "It's what he gets."

Mau put the knife down.

Something in him had gone very still.

"Hamburg," he said.

"You know it?"

He did not know it.

He did not remember ever eating one, or where, or with whom.

But the word had landed somewhere under his memory, the same place the coffee had landed, and his mouth knew exactly what it should taste like, and his hands knew exactly the shape.

"I think I know it," he said. "I think it's from my world too."

Fern looked at him for a long moment.

"Then you're helping," she said.



Grinding meat finer than a knife turned out to be an engineering problem.

That meant Senku.

Senku listened to the request with the expression of a man who had been asked to apply his life's work to a sandwich.

"You want a meat grinder."

"Yes."

"For one meal."

"For one person."

"I'm rebuilding the water system."

"Yes," said Fern. "And you'll finish it. This is a day."

Senku looked at her.

Then at Mau.

Then at the small circled date in Fern's book, which she had left open on the table, perhaps not by accident.

He sighed.

"Give me an afternoon," he said. "And don't tell Suika, or she'll want to crank it."

By evening there was a grinder bolted to the end of the kitchen table, made from a screw, a plate with holes drilled in it, a crank and a great deal of muttering.

Suika had found out within the hour.

Suika cranked it.



Momo took over the kitchen side, because the kitchen was Momo's, and anyone who thought otherwise could argue with her and lose.

Seiko supervised Momo.

Kaneki came in to make coffee and stayed.

He could not eat any of it.

Everyone knew that.

He chopped onions anyway, finely and fast, so much better than Mau that Mau quietly gave up and went to grate bread instead.

"You know you can't have any," said Momo, not unkindly.

"I know," said Kaneki.

"Then why are you helping?"

Kaneki thought about it.

"Because it's a birthday," he said, and kept chopping.

Momo looked at him for a second.

Then she slid a cup of his own coffee across to him, without a word, the way she put food in front of people she had decided were hers.



The hardest part was Stark.

Stark could not know.

Stark, unfortunately, could smell onions from the other side of the settlement, and had noticed that the kitchen door kept closing whenever he came near it.

He was beginning to look worried.

"Is something wrong?" he asked Fern, at lunch.

"No."

"The kitchen's shut."

"Yes."

"Did I do something?"

Fern looked at him.

"Stark," she said. "Go train."

"With who?"

"Me," said Himmel, from down the table, standing up and stretching. "Come on. I've been wanting to see what Eisen taught you."

Stark looked at Himmel.

He did not look worried anymore.

He looked terrified, which, Fern noted with satisfaction, would keep him busy for hours.



They sparred in the yard all afternoon.

Himmel was not as strong as Stark.

Nobody was.

But Himmel had fought beside Eisen for ten years, and knew every habit Eisen had ever had, and could see them in Stark like a family resemblance.

"He drops his left shoulder before he swings," Himmel said, after the third bout. "So do you."

Stark lowered his axe.

"He does?"

"He used to. When I knew him." Himmel leaned on his sword. "He was afraid, you know. Every fight. He never let it show where anyone could see, but I stood next to him for ten years. I could see."

Stark stared at him.

"Eisen?"

"Eisen." Himmel smiled. "He never told you?"

Stark looked down at his own hands.

"He told me fear was nothing to be ashamed of," he said slowly. "I thought he was just being kind."

"He was being kind," said Himmel. "He was also telling you the truth. He just didn't mention it was about himself."

Stark did not say anything for a long time.

Then he picked his axe back up.

"Again," he said.

His hands were steady.



Eren was watching from the edge of the yard.

He was two days into a three-day guest status, and he had spent most of it watching. Mikasa had brought him out to see the sparring. She was standing beside him now, arms folded, saying nothing.

Eren watched Stark fight.

He watched him win.

He watched him, afterward, sit down on a crate and shake.

"That's the one they call their warrior?" Eren said.

Mikasa nodded.

"He's scared."

"He's scared of almost everything," said Mikasa. "Ask him."

Eren did not ask him.

But a little later, when Himmel had gone in and the yard was empty, Stark came over to the fence with his axe on his shoulder and stood beside them, because he had no idea who Eren was and had decided he looked lonely.

"You're new," Stark said.

"Guest."

"Me too, once." Stark looked at the yard. "First week here, I jumped every time a door slammed."

Eren looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because I'm a coward," said Stark simply. "I'm scared all the time. Of monsters. Of fights. Of messing things up." He thought about it. "Of Fern."

"Then why fight?"

Stark shrugged.

"Because the people behind me are scarier to lose."

Eren was quiet.

Mikasa was very quiet beside him.

Stark nodded to them both, awkwardly, the way he did everything that was not swinging an axe, and went inside to see if the kitchen was still shut.

Eren watched him go.

"He doesn't have walls," Eren said.

"No," said Mikasa. "He has people."



The hamburg was enormous.

It was, Momo said, the largest single piece of food she had ever cooked, and she had cooked for a household that included Milim.

It took both Momo and Mau to carry it out on the board.

The common room was full.

Fern had invited everyone.

Fern had told no one why, and everyone had come anyway, because Fern had asked.

Stark came in last, from the yard, still sweaty, still wary, and stopped dead in the doorway.

He looked at the table.

At the board.

At the thing on the board.

At the circle of people around it.

At Fern, standing at the end of the table with her hands folded, looking at him with her usual expression, which was the expression of someone who had done something very large and would like no attention for it whatsoever.

"Happy birthday," said Fern.

Stark did not say anything.

He could not.

His face had done something complicated and young, and his eyes had gone very bright, and he stood there in the doorway like a man who had been hit by something he had not seen coming.

"You remembered," he said finally.

"Obviously," said Fern.

"Nobody told you."

"You told me. On the road. Once."

Stark opened his mouth.

He closed it.

Then he laughed, a wet, broken, astonished laugh, and wiped his face with the back of his arm, and came in.



Everybody had brought something.

Frieren gave him a spell for removing rust from armor, which was the most useful thing she had given anyone in a decade and which she seemed faintly ashamed of.

Himmel gave him one of Eisen's old habits, written down, which Stark read three times and put inside his shirt.

Mau gave him a small bowl of something red.

"What is it?"

"I think it's supposed to go on the hamburg," Mau said. "From my world. I don't remember it. I just know it's supposed to be there." He looked at the bowl doubtfully. "It's probably wrong."

Stark tasted it.

It was wrong.

It was sweet where it should have been sharp and lumpy where it should have been smooth.

Stark put an enormous amount of it on his plate.

"It's good," he said.

"It's wrong."

"It's good," Stark said again, firmly, and Mau gave up.



Stark cut the first piece himself.

He did it carefully, with the concentration he gave to axes and very little else.

Then, instead of putting it on his own plate, he leaned across the table and put it on Fern's.

The whole room saw.

Nobody said anything.

Marin put her hand over her mouth so hard it made a sound.

Fern looked down at the piece on her plate.

Then at Stark.

Her ears had gone faintly pink.

"It's your birthday," she said.

"I know."

"It's yours. The first piece is yours."

"I know," Stark said. "That's why I get to pick."

Fern looked at him for a long moment.

Then she picked up her fork and ate it, very precisely, without a word, and Stark watched her do it with an expression that made Himmel look away and smile at the ceiling.

Across the table, Frieren leaned over to Mau.

"They're going to be like that for a hundred years," she said.

"Is that bad?"

Frieren thought about it.

"No," she said. "It's slow."

She stole a piece of hamburg off his plate.



Later, Mai, who had come only because Maki had, challenged Stark to arm-wrestle for the last slice.

She lost.

She demanded a rematch.

She lost again, and looked at Stark with new and grudging respect, and Okarun, who had bet on Mai, paid Momo three coins and complained about it for the rest of the night.

Kessoku played after dinner. Nothing in particular. Ryo claimed it was a birthday song from her world. Nijika said it was not. Ryo said it was now.

Stark sat at the end of the table with his plate empty and his face still pink and listened to all of it as if he were afraid it might stop.



Mikasa saved a slice for Anko, who was on watch, and wrapped it in a cloth, and went out to the road to bring it to her.

Eren stayed.

He sat at the end of the long table, in the noise, with a plate he had not asked for in front of him.

Suika had put it there.

Nobody had asked him anything.

Nobody had asked him to stay.

He ate the hamburg, slowly, and watched Stark laugh with his mouth full, and watched Fern pretend not to watch Stark, and watched Kaneki drink coffee at a feast he could not eat because it was a birthday, and watched an old woman scold a girl about salt.

When Mikasa came back, he was still there.

"You stayed," she said.

Eren looked at the table.

"It's a birthday," he said, as if that explained it.

Mikasa sat down beside him.

It did.
