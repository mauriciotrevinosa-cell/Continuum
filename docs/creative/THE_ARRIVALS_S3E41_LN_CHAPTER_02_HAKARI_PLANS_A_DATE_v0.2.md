# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 17
## Chapter 17 — Hakari Plans a Date

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 17  
**Reading order:** 17  
**Continuity state:** Ordinary day between hearings | Household plans a second date for Mau and Frieren

The crisis began, as several crises had, with Hakari overhearing something she was not supposed to.

"One?"

The common room went quiet.

Hakari was standing in the doorway with a basket of laundry she had clearly forgotten she was holding.

Mau, who had been answering a question from Kita about something else entirely, turned around.

"One what?"

"You've been on one date," said Hakari. "With Frieren. One. I heard you. Kita asked if you'd taken her anywhere since the bread and you said no."

"We've been busy."

"One date," said Hakari, louder, setting down the basket, "in how long?"

Mau opened his mouth.

Then he made the mistake of actually counting.

Hakari watched his face while he did it.

"Oh no," she said. "Oh no, no, no. Karane."

Karane, on the sofa, did not look up.

"I'm not getting involved."

"Karane, he's counting in months."

Karane looked up.

"...How many months?"

Mau, very wisely, said nothing at all.



Within the hour, there was a committee.

Mau was not invited to it.

Neither was Frieren.

They found out about it the way everyone in the household found out about everything, which was that Nano walked up to Mau after lunch and handed him a schedule.

To the minute.

For a date.

Tomorrow.

"I have had a draft of this for eleven weeks," Nano said. "I was waiting for a sufficiently motivated committee."

Mau looked at the schedule.

It had seven stops.

It had travel times.

It had a contingency column.

"Nano," he said. "Why have you had a date plan for me and Frieren for eleven weeks?"

Nano considered the question.

"Your last date had a measurable positive effect on household stability," she said. "I concluded that a second one was statistically overdue." A pause. "Also, Hakari asked me to."

"Eleven weeks ago?"

"She asks me every week."

Across the room, Hakari waved.



Frieren read the schedule at dinner.

She read it twice.

Then she looked down the table at five people who were all very carefully not looking back at her.

"No," she said.

Hakari put her hands together.

"Please."

"I don't need a committee to go on a date."

"You haven't been on one in months!"

"I can count."

"Then why haven't you?"

Frieren opened her mouth.

She closed it again.

Mau, beside her, also had no answer.

There had been the move. The refuge. The road closures. The audit. The hearing. Every day a reason. Every day tomorrow.

Shizuka held up her book.

The page read:

Tomorrow is a word for days that never come.

Karane read it over her shoulder.

"That's bleak."

Shizuka turned the page.

So we picked a day.

Frieren looked at the book for a long moment.

Then at Mau.

"Fine," she said. "One stop."

"Seven," said Nano.

"Two."

"Five," said Hakari.

"Three," said Frieren, "and nobody follows us."

There was a short, intense silence along the committee side of the table.

"Agreed," said Hakari.

Mau knew, with complete certainty, that they would be followed.



The first stop was Nano's.

The bridge at exactly mid-morning, because the light came through the supports at an angle she had measured, and for eleven minutes it fell across the river in long bars.

Mau and Frieren stood on the bridge.

The light did exactly what Nano had said.

"It's nice," Mau admitted.

"It's measured," said Frieren.

"It's measured and nice."

Frieren looked at the bars of light on the water.

"Yes," she said.

On the near bank, behind a stack of timber, something rustled.

Mau did not turn around.

"How many?"

Frieren did not turn around either.

"Five," she said. "And Milim."

"Why Milim?"

"Milim heard there was a secret."



The second stop was the music room.

Shizuka had arranged it.

Bocchi had lent it without being asked twice.

She knew about needing a quiet room.

It was very quiet.

Mau and Frieren sat on the floor and did not talk and listened to the walls swallow every sound from outside, and it was, both of them would admit later, very pleasant, right up until Frieren tilted her head and said:

"Someone is breathing against the door."

The breathing stopped.

Then a voice, very muffled, said, "That's Karane," and another voice said, "IT IS NOT," and then the door was silent again, and Mau put his face in his hands and laughed without making any noise, and Frieren watched him laugh and looked, for a moment, entirely content.



There was meant to be a third stop.

Kusuri had prepared something for it.

She met them on the path with a small corked bottle and the bright expression of a scientist who had been waiting for a test subject.

"For the date," she said. "It's a tonic. Mild. It enhances mood, warmth, emotional openness—"

A hand came over her shoulder and took the bottle.

Maomao uncorked it, sniffed it once, recorked it, and put it in her pocket.

"No."

"It's mild!"

"It's mild," Maomao agreed, "and it's going in my cabinet, and you're going to tell me what's in it, in writing, and then we're going to have a very long conversation about consent."

Kusuri looked offended.

Then she looked thoughtful.

"...Fair."

Maomao walked her away by the elbow.

Over her shoulder, she said to Mau and Frieren, without slowing down:

"Enjoy your date. Nothing from her."

"I didn't—"

"Nothing from her," Maomao said again, and was gone.

Frieren looked at the empty path.

"That was the third stop?"

"I think the third stop just got confiscated," said Mau.



The last stop on the schedule was Karane's.

It said only:

SUNSET. LAKE. THE SPOT BY THE FLAT ROCK. (DON'T ASK.)

Mau found Karane before they went.

She was standing behind the washing court, pretending to be there by accident, arms crossed so tightly she looked like a knot.

"Thank you," Mau said.

"For what?"

"For giving us your spot."

Karane went red from collar to hairline.

"It's not my spot."

"Okay."

"And I'm not giving it to you."

"Okay."

"I'm lending it." She glared at the ground. "You'd better not make it weird."

"I won't."

"And you'd better actually be happy there, because if you aren't, then I gave it up for nothing, and I'll—"

"Karane."

She stopped.

"I know the spot," said Mau. "Frieren sits there sometimes. She says you complain about the insects the whole time."

Karane looked at him in total betrayal.

"She knew?"

"She's quieter than you."

"Everyone is quieter than me!"

She stormed off.

Then she came back, and said very fast, without looking at him, "It's nicer when someone's there," and stormed off again for real.



Hakari had saved the finale for herself.

She had planned it for days.

Lanterns on poles around the flat rock. Paper flowers she had begged off Marin. A little cloth laid out for two. A speech, written down, which she had practiced in front of Nano until Nano reported that further practice had stopped improving it.

The wind came up off the lake ten minutes before sunset.

It blew out every lantern.

It took the paper flowers off across the water like small boats.

It flipped the cloth into the reeds.

From behind a bush, Hakari made a sound like a wounded animal.

Mau and Frieren stood on the shore and looked at the wreckage.

"We should tell her it's fine," said Mau.

"Yes," said Frieren.

Neither of them moved.

Then Frieren reached into her coat and took out a loaf of bread.

Mau looked at it.

He knew that bread.

He knew the crust.

He knew that it was terrible.

"You went to the village," he said.

"This morning," said Frieren. "Before Nano's schedule started."

"Same stall?"

"It was closest."

Mau started laughing.

He sat down on the flat rock, in the wind, in the wreckage of Hakari's beautiful finale, and Frieren sat down beside him, and broke the loaf in half, and handed him his side.



The committee watched from the bushes.

Five of them, Kusuri having been released from Maomao's custody on what Maomao called parole, and Milim, and by now also Umaru, who had heard there was a secret, and Coco, who had heard Umaru.

Nobody spoke.

On the rock, Frieren reached over and tore a piece off Mau's half.

"You have your own side," Mau said.

"Yours is better."

"It's the same loaf."

"Yes."

Mau stopped.

He knew this.

He knew this exchange.

He knew exactly where it had happened the first time, and what the water had looked like there, and that it had been a stone wall and not a rock, and that it was real.

He did not need to check.

That was new.

He sat with it for a moment, quietly, while Frieren ate his bread.

Then he leaned his shoulder against hers.



In the bushes, Hakari was crying.

"It's not about the plan," she whispered. "It was never about the plan. Nano, it was never about the plan."

"Your plan was structurally sound," said Nano. "The wind was not on the schedule."

"It's about the bread."

"The bread is of poor quality."

"That's what makes it about the bread!"

Karane was wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist and saying, to nobody, that this was stupid, that this was the stupidest thing she had ever crouched in a bush for, and that if anyone told anyone she was crying she would kill them.

Hakari put an arm around her shoulders.

Karane let her.

Shizuka held up her book.

The page said:

♪

Coco whispered, "Should we leave?"

"Yes," said Umaru, and did not move.

Nobody moved.



On the rock, with the lanterns dark and the sun nearly gone, Mau said:

"We're on a date."

Frieren considered this.

"We're being observed."

"We're on a date that's being observed."

Frieren looked over her shoulder at the bushes, which had gone very still, and at the eight sets of eyes inside them, and at the paper flowers still sailing out across the lake.

"Fine," she said.

And stole another piece.
