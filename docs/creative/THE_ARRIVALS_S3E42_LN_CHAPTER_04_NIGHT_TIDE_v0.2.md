# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 17
## Chapter 23 — Night Tide

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 17  
**Reading order:** 23  
**Continuity state:** Family trip, night 1 | 'It's the people standing next to it that change' | Frieren's sea-shining spell, never shown to anyone | 'Second most'

After dinner, the fire burned down and the household burned down with it.

People fell asleep where they sat. Umaru and Suika slept inside the walls of their own fortress. Milim, who had fought the sea for six hours, slept face-down on the sand with one fist still clenched. Yuta and Maki slept on their towel. Bocchi had fallen asleep against Kita, and Kita, rather than move her, had fallen asleep too. Seiko had been asleep in her folding chair for an hour and would deny it until her death. Out in the dark water, a small blue dome rose and fell on the swell, very peacefully, and did not come in.

Mau could not sleep.

He was too full of the day. He lay on his back on the sand by the embers and looked up at more stars than he had ever seen over the settlement, and listened to the sea.

Beside him, Frieren was not asleep either.

She was sitting up with her knees drawn in, looking out at the water. She had been looking at it for a long time.

"You've seen the sea before," said Mau.

"Many times."

"With Himmel?"

"With Himmel. And Heiter. And Eisen." She was quiet. "And alone, after. More times alone than with anyone."

"Does it look different?"

Frieren considered the question honestly, the way she considered everything.

"No," she said. "The sea doesn't change. I came back to the same beaches after a hundred years, and two hundred, and they were exactly the same. The same rocks. The same sound." She paused. "It's the people standing next to it that change."

Mau sat up.

He looked at her in the dark, with the starlight on her hair and her face doing nothing at all.

"Then I'm going to stand here a long time," he said.

Frieren did not answer.

But she leaned, very slightly, until her shoulder was against his.



They walked down to the water.

The tide was going out. The wet sand at the edge was flat and shining, and the small waves came in black and silver and slid away again. Mau rolled up his trousers and walked in the shallows. Frieren walked beside him with her boots in her hand, letting the foam run over her feet.

"The sea's beautiful," said Mau.

Frieren opened her mouth.

"No," said Mau, before she could say anything at all. "Not more than you. Stop asking."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

Frieren closed her mouth.

She looked out at the dark water, perfectly composed.

Her ears went red.

Mau grinned at the sea and did not say anything else.



They had walked a long way down the beach, past the dark rocks at the south end where the household's fire was only a small orange point behind them, when Frieren stopped.

"I want to show you something," she said.

Mau stopped too.

"Okay."

Frieren put her boots down on the sand. She waded a little way out, until the water was around her ankles, and held out her hand over the dark sea.

She did not say anything. She did not draw anything in the air. She just stood there, the way she had stood in the clearing a lifetime ago with her hand held out over the frost, and something went out of her palm into the water.

The sea lit up.

Not all of it. The patch in front of her, first: a soft blue-green glow, rising up out of the black water as though the sea had been holding light in it all along and had just been waiting for someone to ask. Then it spread. It ran out along the line of the waves, up and down the beach, every breaking wave edged with cold living light. When the water ran up around Mau's ankles, it glowed where it touched him. When it slid away, it left the wet sand sparkling, and the sparkles went out one by one, slowly, like embers.

Mau stood in the middle of it and could not speak.

"It's a spell for making the sea shine at night," said Frieren.

She was looking at the water, not at him.

"I found it a long time ago. It took me sixty years. It doesn't do anything. You can't fight with it. It doesn't help you travel. It just makes the sea shine." She paused. "I used it once, when I first learned it. On a beach like this one. By myself."

"Why?"

"To see if it worked." Frieren was quiet for a long moment. "I never used it again. There was never anyone to show it to."

The waves came in glowing and went out again.

Mau thought about a clearing, and frost, and a woman who did not know what was wrong with him lifting her hand over the grass anyway, so that the first thing in the world he ever saw would be something beautiful.

He thought about making flowers there himself, fourteen minutes into something that might have killed him, because he had wanted, once, to be the one who made something.

She had been carrying this spell for a thousand years. She had found it alone and used it once, and never again, because there had never been anyone she wanted to show it to.

He walked out to her through the shining water.

He did not say anything clever. He did not say anything at all. He just put his arms around her from behind and rested his chin on top of her head, the way he had in the storm while she chose a book. They stood together in the light she had made, up to their ankles in it, and watched the sea shine.

"It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen," said Mau, eventually.

Frieren went very still in his arms.

"...More than—"

"No," said Mau. "Second most."

Frieren did not say anything.

But she put her hands over his where they were crossed in front of her, and held on, and they stayed in the water until the last of the light went out.
