# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 17
## Chapter 24 — Tidepools

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 17  
**Reading order:** 24  
**Continuity state:** Family trip, dawn day 2 and the ride home | Mau and Ori at the tide pools | 'Tell me first. Before you go' | 'Dad,' asleep in the cart

Ori woke him before dawn by poking him in the shoulder, repeatedly, with one finger.

"Mau."

Poke.

"Mau."

Poke.

"The tide's out."

Mau opened one eye. The sky over the sea was the dark grey that comes just before the first light. Frieren was asleep against his side with her hair full of sand. Somewhere down the beach, Milim was snoring like a small, contented thunderstorm.

Ori was crouched beside him, fully dressed, with her shoes on and a bucket in her hand and an expression of contained urgency he had never seen on her before.

"The rocks at the south end," she whispered. "There are pools. Maomao said. When the tide goes out, things live in them." She paused. "I've never seen a tide pool."

Mau looked at her.

Ori had come into the world strange, and old, and careful. She had spent most of the time he had known her being the one who noticed things, held things, and quietly made sure everyone else was all right. He could not remember her ever once waking anyone up because she wanted to see something.

He very carefully lifted Frieren's arm off his chest and put it down on the warm place he had left.

"Show me," he said.



The pools were at the foot of the south rocks, where the night's tide had pulled back and left the stone wet and black and full of hollows. Every hollow held its own small, still sea.

Ori knelt at the first one and did not move for five minutes.

There were anemones in it, red and green, closed tight like small fists. There were tiny grey fish that flicked away from her shadow. There was a crab, and a star, and a snail grazing slowly across the stone, and on the bottom, perfectly still, something with too many legs that Ori named immediately and with great confidence, though neither of them had any idea what it was.

"It's a whole world," said Ori. "In a hole."

"Yeah."

"It doesn't know about the sea. It thinks this is everything." She touched the surface of the water with one fingertip, very gently, so the anemone closest to her closed even tighter. "And then twice a day the sea comes back and it's part of something huge. And then it goes away again."

Mau did not say anything.

He knelt beside her on the wet rock and watched her watch the pool, and understood that she was not only talking about the pool.



They went from pool to pool as the sky got lighter.

Ori found three more stars, a shell shaped like a horn, and a small crab that she named Senku because it kept trying to measure the edges of its pool. She kept the horn shell. She said it was for Frieren. She put it very carefully in the bucket.

Somewhere around the sixth pool, without looking up from it, she said:

"I was scared. In the plaza."

Mau looked at her.

"When the wheel came up," said Ori. "Milim had Frieren. Everyone was running. I went where the drill said to go. I did everything right." She was quiet. "And then I stood in the refuge for eleven minutes and counted, and I couldn't stop thinking that the drill was the last thing you'd taught me and I'd done it perfectly and that was going to be it."

Mau did not say anything for a moment.

"I was scared too," he said.

"I know."

"The whole time. Every minute of it."

"I know," said Ori. "I could tell. Afterward. You look different when you've been scared. Your shoulders." She touched the water again. "You did it anyway."

"So did you."

Ori looked up at him.

She did not say anything. But something in her face, which was usually so careful, came open for a moment, the way it had in the narrow room in the rain with Rem.



The sun came up over the sea.

They sat on the highest rock with the bucket between them and watched it come. Neither of them talked for a long while.

Then Ori said:

"Can I ask you for something?"

"Anything."

"Don't say anything." She was looking at the sea, not at him. "Not until you've heard it."

Mau waited.

"When something happens to you," said Ori. "Something big. Something bad. The kind of thing where you'd want to go somewhere alone and deal with it by yourself so nobody else gets hurt." She paused. "Tell me first. Before you go."

Mau was very still.

"You always go," said Ori quietly. "The first time. The road. The second time. Even in the inn kitchen, with the salt, when Ciel told you to run. You go first and you tell us after, if you can. And I know why. I know you think it's the safe thing." She looked at him at last. "It's not safe for me. I'm the one standing in the refuge counting."

Mau looked at her for a long time.

At this girl who had come into the world strange and old and careful, who had chosen a room across a muddy courtyard because the plan would not let her be closer, who had told him not to ask Rem to stay, who had sat in the rain and watched someone read a note on a wall and said nothing.

"Okay," said Mau.

"Promise."

"I promise," said Mau. "Before I go anywhere. You first."

Ori nodded once.

She looked back at the sea. Her shoulders, which she always held so carefully, came down a little.

"Okay," she said.

Neither of them knew yet how hard that promise would be to keep.



They drove home that afternoon in four carts, sunburned and sandy and exhausted. Umaru had a jar of sand from her fortress and refused to say what it was for. Senku had a notebook he swore did not contain tide data. Maomao had a crab that had apparently decided to live with her now. Eren drove the lead cart, and when Rimuru offered to take the reins Eren said "no" so fast that Mikasa laughed out loud, and everyone in the cart turned around to look at her, because hardly anybody had ever heard Mikasa laugh out loud.

Ori sat in the back of the second cart, wedged between Mau and the side, with the bucket of shells on her knees.

Somewhere past the halfway point, the rocking of the cart got her.

She fell asleep sitting up, the way children do. She tipped slowly sideways until her head was on Mau's shoulder, and stayed there. Mau did not move. He sat very still for an hour so as not to wake her, with his arm going numb, watching the trees go by.

Just before they reached the bridge, Ori stirred in her sleep.

"Dad," she murmured, into his shoulder. "Look. The sea."

Then she was asleep again.

Mau did not say anything.

He sat in the back of the cart with Ori asleep against him and the shell for Frieren rattling in the bucket on her knees. He looked straight ahead at the road, and did not move, and did not trust his face to do anything at all.

Across the cart, Frieren had seen.

She did not say anything either.

She reached over and put her hand on the back of his, where it lay on the seat between them, and kept it there all the way home.
