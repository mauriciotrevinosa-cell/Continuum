# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 17
## Chapter 33 — What Tempest Cost

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 17  
**Reading order:** 33  
**Continuity state:** Same night | Rimuru tells Tempest: Falmuth, twenty thousand, the eastern empire, Yuuki | 'A power people walk into, not one that walks out'

"What are we becoming," said Fern, and stepped back from the board.

For a while, nobody answered.

It was a harder question than the first one, and everyone in the room knew it. The first question had been about who. This one was about what. What were they, the long ridiculous house and the settlement growing around it and the people coming and going over the bridge? What were they turning into, whether they meant to or not?

Anko answered first, because Anko always started with evidence.

She put the document on the table, the one with the phrase in it. *Uncontrolled concentration of foreign capability.* Three regions, three authorities, the same words in the same order.

"This is what they think we're becoming," said Anko. "Somebody is teaching the world to be afraid of us, in a standard format. It's going to get worse." She looked around. "So the question isn't really what we want to become. It's what we want to be when they come."

"When," said Maomao.

"When," said Anko.



"We could stay small," said Kita.

She said it quietly, from the floor beside Bocchi. Several people turned to look at her.

"The other camp did," said Kita. "Across the river. They survived because nobody noticed them. They kept their heads down and their numbers low and nobody ever wrote them on a form." She looked at her hands. "Maybe we could do that. Stop growing. Stop building. Just be a house."

"Too late," said Senku, not unkindly. "We built a bridge. You can't un-build a bridge. People know where we are."

"We could stop letting people come."

"No," said Frieren.

It was the first thing she had said since the faces. Everyone turned.

"We are not closing the door," said Frieren. "Everyone in this room came through a door somebody else held open. I am not going to be the one who closes it on the next person."

Kita nodded slowly. She did not look like she had wanted to win the argument. She looked like she had wanted someone to say exactly that.



"Then we become strong," said Aira. "Strong enough that nobody comes."

"Strong enough for what?" said Mikasa.

"For them to leave us alone."

"Nobody is ever strong enough for that," said Mikasa. "I come from a place that built walls fifty meters high so the world would leave it alone. It didn't."

At the window, Eren had gone very still.



Rimuru had not said anything since the first question.

He was sitting on the couch with his elbows on his knees, the way he had sat through the faces. He was looking at the floor. Mau, beside him, could feel something coming off him that he had not felt in a long time: the particular stillness of someone deciding whether to say something he had never said in this room.

"Can I tell you about Tempest?" said Rimuru.

The room went quiet.

Everyone in the house knew Rimuru had a city. They had heard the stories at dinner, the funny ones, the goblin village that tried to make him king, the dwarves, the dragon he had kept in his stomach and argued with about manga. They had all heard those.

They had not heard this one.

"It started small," said Rimuru. "The way this did. A few goblins who needed help. Then some wolves. Then some dwarves who were in trouble. Then everyone who heard there was a place where monsters and people could live together without killing each other. It got big because people kept arriving and I kept saying yes." He smiled a little. "Sound familiar?"

Some people laughed. Not many.

"We made friends," said Rimuru. "That was the plan. Be useful to everyone, be a threat to no one, trade, help, build roads. Make it so that hurting us would cost everyone around us something they wanted. I thought that was enough. I thought if we were kind enough and useful enough, nobody would ever bother."

He stopped.

"There was a kingdom," he said. "Falmuth. They wanted what we had. They put up a barrier around the city that made magic stop working, and while I was away they came in with soldiers, and they killed my people."

The room had gone completely silent.

"Not all of them," said Rimuru. "A lot of them. People I'd named. People who'd trusted me because I said this place would be safe." He swallowed. "Shion. She was my secretary. She was terrible at cooking and the most loyal person I've ever known. She died protecting children. And I wasn't there."

Diablo, outside the door, did not move, but Mau thought he heard the door frame creak.

"There was a way to bring them back," said Rimuru. "One way. Become a Demon Lord. To do that, I needed souls. A lot of them." He looked at his left hand, the one that remembered being gone. "Twenty thousand."

Nobody breathed.

"So I went to where the army was camped," said Rimuru, very quietly, "and I took them."

He did not explain any more than that. He did not need to.

"It worked," he said. "They came back. Shion came back. Everyone came back." He was quiet for a moment. "And I was a person who had done that. I still am. It doesn't go away because it worked."

By the window, Eren had unfolded his arms.

He was looking at Rimuru with an expression nobody in the room had seen on him before. It was not fear or judgment. Mau thought, later, that it had been recognition. The face of someone looking at the only other person in the room who might understand.

"Did you regret it?" said Eren. His voice was rough.

"Every day," said Rimuru. "And I'd do it again. Both of those."

Eren looked at him for a long time.

Then Mikasa's hand found his arm, and he looked down, and did not say anything else.



"After that," said Rimuru, "I did it right. Or I tried to. Alliances. Treaties. Councils. I sat down with every king and every Demon Lord and every church I could find, and I made us so connected to everyone that hurting us would hurt the whole world." He was quiet for a moment. "And it still wasn't enough."

"What happened?" said Kita.

Rimuru looked at the floor.

"There was an empire," he said. "In the east. And there were—" He stopped and chose his words carefully. "There were versions of things. Times when it went the other way. When the empire came, and all the friends in the world didn't stop it, and I died."

Mau went still.

"I'm not going to explain how I know that," said Rimuru. "I'm not sure I could. But someone fixed it. Someone carried it, alone, over and over, so that it didn't happen to me. So that I'm here." He breathed out. "I didn't even know until it was over."

Mau did not look at him.

He could not. There was a number in him, sitting very still, which he had asked Ciel never to say to Rimuru. And Rimuru was sitting beside him, talking about someone who had carried something over and over, alone, so that it didn't happen to someone else.

Mau held very still and looked at the board.

Inside his head, very quietly, Ciel said nothing at all.



"And then there was Yuuki," said Rimuru.

He said the name differently, as if it still tasted of something.

"He was from my world. Japanese, like me. He'd been a student of someone I loved. He ran the guild. He was funny and smart and helpful, and he was my friend. I trusted him more than almost anyone." Rimuru's mouth twisted. "He was working with a group of clowns the whole time. Literally clowns. They wore masks and called themselves jesters. Every bad thing that happened to us for years, he'd had a hand in. And he smiled at me through all of it."

"What did you learn?" said Jinshi.

It was a very quiet question, from a man who had spent his whole life in a palace learning exactly that lesson.

Rimuru thought about it.

"That being kind isn't protection," he said. "Being small isn't protection. Being useful isn't protection. Being connected isn't protection. Trusting someone isn't protection." He looked around the room. "And being strong isn't protection either. I was the strongest thing in my world, and they still killed my people while I was away."

"Then what is?" said Kita.

Rimuru was quiet for a long time.

"Nothing," he said. "Nothing is protection. There's only how you decide to be, knowing that." He looked at the board. "That's the real question. Not what we become so they leave us alone. They won't. What do we want to be when they don't."



After that, everybody talked.

It went on for two hours, and it was the best two hours of argument the household had ever had.

Senku said that capability was not the same as aggression, and that a settlement could be impossible to erase without ever threatening anyone. Mikasa said that was true right up until the moment it wasn't, and that she had seen what happened when a people decided the only way to be safe was for no one else to be. Eren did not speak at all through that part, but he did not leave either. Maki said her clan had been powerful for a thousand years and had used every year of it to crush its own daughters, and that power was not the problem; what you let it make you into was. Kaneki said, very quietly, that he had lived his whole life as something people hunted, and he did not want to live anywhere that taught its children to hunt back. Himmel said that in his whole life as a hero he had never once protected anyone by conquering anything, only by standing in the road.

And Frieren, near the end, said the thing that settled it.

"I've watched a lot of kingdoms," she said. "Hundreds. I've watched them rise and I've watched them fall. Most of them I don't remember." She was looking at the window, at the dark and the lamps of the settlement beyond it. "The ones I remember were never the strongest. They were the ones people walked to. Roads full of people going there. Gates open. That's what lasts. Not walls. A place people want to come to, that's strong enough that nobody can take it away from them."

The room was quiet.

"A power people walk into," said Mau slowly. "Not one that walks out."

Frieren looked at him.

"Yes," she said.

Fern picked up the chalk.

She wrote it under the second line, in her neat hand:

A POWER PEOPLE WALK INTO. NOT ONE THAT WALKS OUT.

Then, underneath, smaller, because Senku insisted on precision:

Strong enough that nobody can erase us. Open enough that anyone can leave.

Then she drew a line through the second question.



"One more thing," said Rimuru, before Fern could tap the third line.

Everyone looked at him.

"If we do this," said Rimuru, "if we decide out loud to be something that matters, somebody is going to test it." He looked at the document on the table, at the phrase repeated across three regions in the same order. "Soon. Probably sooner than we want. That's how it went for me. The moment you decide to be something, someone comes to find out if you meant it."

"Then we'll mean it," said Mau.

Rimuru looked at him.

For a moment, something passed across his face that Mau could not read. It might have been pride. It might have been fear. On Rimuru, they had always looked very much alike.

"Yeah," said Rimuru. "I guess we will."

Fern tapped the third line on the board.
