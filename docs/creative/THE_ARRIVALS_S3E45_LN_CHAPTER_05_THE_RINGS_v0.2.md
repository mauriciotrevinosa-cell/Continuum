# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 17
## Chapter 33 — The Rings

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 17  
**Reading order:** 33  
**Continuity state:** Same night, to 3 a.m. | Rings One to Four written down; Eren voted into Ring Two | Secrecy contract signed | Mau's shadow: 'a summon he is still training'

"Who gets told things," said Fern.

It was past one in the morning. The fire had burned low. Nazuna, who had arrived awake and stayed that way out of sheer interest, was the only person in the room who looked fresher than she had at ten. Momo had brought out bread and the second-best pot of soup at midnight without being asked, and the bowls were scattered across every surface.

Nobody had gone to bed.

"There are things in this house," said Fern, "that the people in this room know, and the people outside it don't."

She did not say what. She did not have to. Everyone in the room was thinking of the same things. The list of rules pinned inside Mau's door. The voice only Rimuru could hear. Four varnished scratches in the common-room floor and a glyph on the ceiling above them. A number of patterns, held behind a wall, that nobody outside the family had ever been told the size of.

"Anko made a rule, after the containment," said Fern. "Rule seven. Nobody outside the household learns any of this." She looked around the room. "It was a good rule. It was also written for a house. We're not a house anymore. We're a settlement with a bridge, and the people who come and go across it are not all the same. So who is the household now? Who counts as inside?"



It was the hardest of the four, because it was about people.

"Everyone in this room," said Momo at once. "Obviously."

"Yes," said Fern. "And then?"

"Richeh," said Tetia.

Richeh, who was in the room, looked startled to be named.

"Richeh's in the room," said Fern. "Richeh's inside."

Richeh opened her mouth to say she was a guest. She looked at Tetia. She closed her mouth again and did not say it.

"The witches," said Mau.

The room went quiet.

"They've kept every promise they ever made me," said Mau. "Every second week. They've never once asked something I said wasn't theirs to ask. I trust them."

"I know you do," said Fern gently. "Does everyone?"

Mau looked around the room.

He saw it at once. Not hostility. Just hesitation. Frieren, who had once nearly fought them in a forest. Rimuru, who had sat with Ciel's readings of them. Mikasa, who trusted nothing she could not see the exits of. Ori, who had sat in the room for the first session and watched them write things down.

"No," said Mau quietly.

"That's the problem," said Maki.

Everyone looked at her.

"It's not about whether one of us trusts someone," said Maki. "Any one of us could trust anyone. Mau trusts the witches. Rimuru trusted a man who smiled at him for years while he burned his city down. That's not a criticism. That's what trust is. It's one person's, and sometimes it's wrong." She looked around the room. "So it can't be one person who decides. Not for the inside."



They drew the rings that night.

Not new ones. The rings had existed for a long time, in the way people spoke about each other. Ring One and Ring Two, the family and the ones who had come in through the family. They had never been written down. That night, for the first time, Fern wrote them on the board. Everybody argued over every word, and by the end every word was right.

**Ring One.** Family. The people in this room who are family, in whatever word they use for it.

**Ring Two.** Close. The people in this room who came in through someone already here and stayed, all the way through the week Mau first went missing, up to the last of them. *(G5 closed with Rem, Kaneki and Aira,* Anko added, in her code, and then wrote it again in plain letters so everyone could read it.)

Then there was a long argument about Eren.

Eren sat through it with his arms folded and did not say a word in his own defense, which Mau thought was the most Eren thing he had ever seen. In the end it was Mikasa who settled it.

"He's in the room," said Mikasa. "He came because I asked. He sat through Rimuru's story and didn't leave. I vouch for him."

"One person's vouch," said Maki, "isn't enough. That's what we just said."

"Then vote," said Mikasa.

They voted. It was not unanimous. It was close enough.

"Ring Two," said Fern, and wrote Eren's name, and looked at him.

Eren looked at the name on the board for a long time.

"...I'll think about it," he said.

"You can think about it inside," said Fern, and that was that.

**Ring Three.** People some of us trust and not all of us yet. To come inside, someone in Ring One or Two has to vouch for them, *and* Rings One and Two together have to agree. One person's trust gets you to the door. It takes all of us to open it.

"The witches," said Fern, and wrote them under Ring Three.

Mau did not argue. He looked at the word on the board for a moment. Then he nodded, because it was right, even if he did not like it, and because he understood that being written down at all was something.

**Ring Four.** Everyone else. The people who come and go across the bridge. Refuge families. Travelers. Arrival House. The second community. The merchants at the washing court. People who stay a night, a month, a year.

"Their right to be here doesn't depend on any of this," said Fern, and underlined it twice. "They get shelter. They get food. They get protected. They get exactly the same roof as us when it rains. They just don't get told what's behind the door."

"That sounds cold," said Kita, unhappily.

"It's not cold," said Seiko, from her armchair. "It's a front door. Every house has one. You let people into the hall before you let them into the kitchen. That's not unkindness. That's having a kitchen."



"And for the inside," said Anko, "we need a contract."

She had one ready. Of course she did. It was the same kind of contract the household had made at the very beginning, in the first weeks after the old inn filled up: a promise, written down, that what was said inside stayed inside. Anko had simply rewritten it for a settlement instead of a house, longer and more careful, with a line at the bottom for every name in the room.

Nothing confidential leaves Rings One and Two. Not the seal. Not Ciel. Not the rules. Not the number of anything. Not what is behind the door. If you want to know, you sign. If you sign, you keep it.

She put it on the long table with a pen beside it.

Nobody hesitated.

That was what Mau would remember. Nobody hesitated at all. Fern signed first, because Fern always signed first. Then Frieren, without reading it, because she had already decided. Then Rimuru. Then Yuta and Maki together. Then Bocchi and Kita. Then everyone, one after another, in a long line around the table, Milim signing with a flourish that took up three lines, Nazuna yawning in the middle of hers, Richeh signing slowly and carefully and then looking at her own name for a long time, as if it surprised her.

Eren was last.

He stood in front of the paper with the pen in his hand for a long moment.

Then he signed it.

He did not say anything. He went back to the window and stood beside Mikasa, and Mikasa did not say anything either. But she looked at him the way a person looks at someone who has just walked through a door they were not sure he would walk through.

Mau signed too, at the very end, below everyone else. He noticed as he did it that it was the second time in his life he had signed a list of rules that other people had written about him. This time he had been in the room for every word.



"The last one," said Fern.

She tapped the board.

MAU'S SHADOW.

Mau, who had been beginning to relax, stopped relaxing.

"The plan," said Fern, "was that nobody outside would ever know. That was the whole point of rule seven." She looked at him, not unkindly. "That plan is over. Thirty people saw a wheel come out of the ground in the middle of the plaza. Some of them were from the second community. One of them was a man with a sword who has told everyone in two valleys about it."

"So we announce it," said Aira. "Get ahead of it. Tell them the truth before they make something worse."

"Which truth?" said Jinshi.

The room looked at him.

"There are a great many truths about Mau's shadow," said Jinshi, in his calm palace voice. "Which one do you want two valleys to know? That he carries a hundred and forty-three patterns behind a wall that has been breached once? That one of them is a thing that adapts to anything that hurts it, and was trying to adapt to the end of everything when it was stopped? Those are all true." He spread his hands. "None of them make the people across the river sleep better."

"Then we mitigate," said Anko. "We don't lie. We choose what to say."

"What do they actually know?" said Maomao.

Anko consulted her notes.

"They know his shadow can make a wheel," she said. "They know it frightened everyone. They know he held it and it went away. They know there were lights over his head. That's it. Nobody outside this room knows what the wheel is. Nobody knows what the lights are. Nobody knows about the wall."

"So they know almost nothing," said Senku.

"They know enough to be afraid," said Anko. "Which is the same thing, to most people."

There was a silence.

Then Yuta said, slowly:

"It's a shikigami."

Everyone turned to him.

"The wheel," said Yuta. "That's what it is, where it comes from. Ten Shadows is a summoning technique. The dog in the common room was a shikigami. The wheel belongs to one. It came up through the shadow medium." He looked at Mau. "If somebody in the river towns asked a sorcerer from my world what they'd seen, they'd say: a summon. In his shadow. One he can't fully control yet."

"That's—" Mau stopped. "That's technically true."

"It's completely true," said Yuta. "It's just not all of it."

"In my world," said Seiko, "people who can summon things are respected. Feared a little. But respected. A boy with a spirit in his shadow that he's still learning to handle?" She sniffed. "Half the shrines in my prefecture would have hired you."

"It's a better story than *monster*," said Anko. "And it's one they've already got most of. We don't have to invent anything. We just have to finish the sentence for them, before someone worse does."

"*There is a summon in Mau's shadow,*" said Fern slowly, trying the words out. "*He is still learning to control it. It frightened people once. It is being trained.*"

"I hate it," said Mau.

The room looked at him.

"I hate it," he said again. "It's true and it's a lie at the same time. I don't want to be a story. I don't want to be something people manage."

"You're already a story," said Rimuru quietly. "You're just not the one telling it. That's the only thing we're changing."

Mau was quiet for a long time.

"There's one more thing," said Maomao. "If we tell that story, it has to keep being true. In public. Which means he has to keep being ordinary. In public." She looked at Mau. "You can't hide behind the family wing. You have to keep going to the washing court. Keep eating in the plaza. Keep talking to Ring Four people as if nothing is wrong. Let them see a man with a summon he's training, not a monster behind a door. The moment you stop being seen, the other story wins."

"And if more comes out?" said Mikasa.

"Then the story breaks," said Maomao. "And we tell the next truth, and the next one. But not before we have to."

Mau looked at the board.

At the four lines, three of them crossed out. At his own name, under the last one.

Then he looked at Frieren, beside him, who was looking at him.

"You'll hate it," she said.

"Yeah."

"Do it anyway?"

He breathed out.

"Okay," he said. "A summon. I'm training it."

"I can help train it," said Milim at once, sitting up. "In public. Every day. In the plaza. It'll be great. People will love it."

"*No*," said the entire room.

Milim lay back down on the floor, deeply wounded, and Umaru patted her head.

Fern drew a line through the last question.



It was nearly three in the morning when the meeting ended.

People went to bed in twos and threes, yawning, arguing, still talking. Momo collected bowls. Marin fell asleep on the couch and Wakana put a blanket over her. Nazuna, at last, began to look tired, which meant it was almost dawn somewhere. Richeh stood in front of the board for a long time, looking at her name under Ring Two. Eren and Mikasa went out together onto the step outside, and did not come back in for a while.

Mau stayed.

He sat on the couch in the glyph light and looked at the board. Four lines, all crossed out. Two names in a box that had once held a question mark. Four rings, drawn in chalk, the innermost ones small and the outermost wide enough to hold everyone who would ever cross the bridge.

Rimuru sat down beside him.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Twenty thousand," said Mau finally, very quietly.

"Yeah," said Rimuru.

"You never told us."

"I never told anyone. Here." Rimuru looked at his hands. "I didn't want it to be the first thing anyone knew about me."

Mau thought about a number he was carrying that he had not told Rimuru. He thought about it for a long time, and did not say it.

"Thank you for telling us," he said instead.

Rimuru bumped his shoulder against Mau's.

"Two," said Rimuru.

"Two," said Mau.

Somewhere behind the wall, very deep down, something was quiet. It might have been listening. It might not.

Volume Seventeen ended with three things on the same table: a defense system designed not to become a cage; a crossed-out equation that had tried to turn Mau into a civic resource; and a board in the common room with four rings drawn on it, two names where a question mark had been, and one true sentence about a shadow that was not the whole truth.

The city had become worth protecting. It had chosen who would speak for it, what it wanted to be, and who would be told what.

Now it had to prove it could protect itself without consuming the two people it had just asked to stand in front.
