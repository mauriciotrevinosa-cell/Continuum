# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 18
## Chapter 3 — Look at Me

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 3  
**Continuity state:** Same night | Frieren's first panic attack | Maki, Milim, Seiko | Rimuru asks her to trust the bay

Maki got there seconds after they had gone.

She had heard Rimuru's voice and known it, and come down the corridor at a dead run. She passed the line of dark drops on the boards going the other way, toward the bay, and did not follow them. She understood at once where they led, and that Rimuru and Yuta were already at the other end. She had stood beside Yuta through more than one war. She knew what he was like with his hands on someone. Nobody in any world was going to make him let go.

So she went the other way. Toward the room the line came from.

Frieren was on the floor beside the bed.

She was kneeling where Rimuru had put her down. She had not moved. The bed behind her was soaked dark from the middle outward. The front of the shirt she was wearing, which was Mau's, was dark. Her hands were dark to the wrist and held out a little from her body, palms up. She was looking at them as if they belonged to somebody else.

She was breathing very fast. Too fast. Short, high breaths that did not go anywhere.

Maki knew that breathing.

She had seen it on battlefields, on younger sorcerers, the first time. She had felt it in her own chest, once or twice, a long time ago, before she had learned to put it somewhere. She had never in her life expected to see it on Frieren, who had lived a thousand years and never once, as far as anyone knew, been afraid of anything in front of anybody.

Maki crossed the room and knelt down in front of her, close, between Frieren and the bed.

"Frieren," she said. "Look at me."

Frieren did not look at her.

"Not at your hands. At me. Look at me."

Frieren's eyes moved. Slowly. Off her hands and up. They found Maki's face and did not seem to know what it was.

"Good," said Maki. "Now breathe with me. In. Slowly. Like this." She breathed in, slow and deep, and held it. "Out."

Frieren tried. It caught halfway.

"Again," said Maki. "In."

"I can't—"

"You can. You've done it for a thousand years. You're just doing it too fast. In."

Frieren breathed in.

"Out."

Out.

"Again."



Milim arrived next.

She had come from the end bay, from the other side of Mau and Frieren's wall. She had heard everything through it: Rimuru's voice, the running, her brother's door. She burst into the room with her hands already in fists and her whole body lit up, ready to fight. Ready to hit something, anything, whatever had done this, until it stopped.

She stopped in the doorway.

She saw the bed.

She saw the floor, and the line of drops going out of the door, and Frieren on her knees with dark hands, breathing in and out at Maki's count.

Milim's fists did not open, but they stopped shaking.

"Where's Mau?" Her voice came out very small. "Where's my— where's Rimuru's—"

"The bay," said Maki, without turning around. "Yuta has him. Rimuru has him. Maomao has him."

"I'll go. I'll help, I can—"

"Milim."

Rimuru was in the doorway behind her. He had come back. Only for a second, as a slime, flowing in low along the floor, and he spoke fast.

"Stay with Maki. Do whatever she asks you. That's helping. That's the most helpful thing anyone can do right now."

Milim looked at him. Then at Frieren on the floor, still counting her breaths with Maki.

Something went across Milim's face. Something very old.

"Okay," she said.

Rimuru was gone again.

Milim came across the room and knelt down on Frieren's other side, very carefully, the way someone kneels who has spent thousands of years being too strong for every room she was in. She did not touch Frieren. She just knelt there, close, the way you stay near someone so they know you are there.

"I know," said Milim quietly, not to anyone in particular. "When someone you love is bleeding and you can't do anything. I know."



Frieren got as far as her feet.

The breathing had slowed. It was not right yet, but it was going somewhere now. As soon as it was, she stood up, too fast, and swayed, and Maki caught her elbow. Frieren turned toward the door and the line of drops on the floor.

"I'm going to the bay."

"Frieren—"

"I'm going. I need to be there. I need to—"

"No," said Rimuru.

He was in the doorway again, in his human form this time, and he had not come back for long. He looked worse than Frieren had ever seen him. There was blood on his hands too, and on the front of his shirt where he had held Mau's head.

"You can't," said Rimuru. "Not yet."

"He's *mine*—"

"I know he is." Rimuru's voice cracked. "I know. That's why. Listen to me. Right now, in that room, there are four people doing the thing they're best at in the world. Yuta's holding him together. Maomao's closing what she can. Senku's getting blood into him. Kusuri's doing anything anybody asks, faster than they can ask it. And Ciel's inside him, fighting the thing that did this." He held Frieren's eyes. "If you go in there like this, they'll stop to look at you. Every one of them. Even for a second. And we don't have a second." He paused. "You have to trust them. I know you've never had to before. You have to now."

Frieren looked at him.

Her mouth opened. Nothing came out.

"I'll come and get you," said Rimuru. "The moment anything changes. Good or bad. I promise. You'll be the first."

He was gone again.

Frieren stood in the middle of the room and shook.



Seiko had been standing in the corridor through all of it.

She had come out of her room at the first shout, slower than the young ones, and walked down the corridor at her own pace, past the line of drops, and stopped in the doorway behind Milim. She had watched all of it without saying a word. Maki on her knees counting breaths. Milim kneeling. Rimuru coming and going. Frieren standing up and trying to leave.

Now she came into the room.

She looked at the bed for a moment, without any expression at all. Then she looked at Frieren, all of her, from her bare feet to her hair, the way she looked at a ghost before she decided what to do about it.

Then she took Maki by one elbow and Milim by the other and moved them aside, and planted herself in front of Frieren with her arms folded.

"You," said Seiko, "are going to wash."

Frieren stared at her.

"What?"

"You're going to wash," said Seiko, "and you're going to put on clean clothes, and you're going to do something with that hair." She jerked her chin at Frieren's hands, at the shirt. "Because when that boy wakes up, and he is going to wake up, I've decided, the first thing he's going to see is you. Do you want him to have a heart attack and die all over again? Seeing you like this? Thinking it's *yours*?"

Frieren looked down.

She had not looked at herself. Not once. She had looked at her hands, but only her hands, the way you look at a thing that has happened. She had not seen the rest.

She saw it now.

The shirt. Mau's shirt, the soft grey one she wore to sleep in. It was dark from the collar to the hem, stiffening already. Her arms. Her knees, where she had knelt in the bed. The ends of her hair, which had been spread across both of them when she fell asleep, were dark too, clumped together, heavy.

She looked like something that had walked off a battlefield.

She looked like the thing she had been, a long time ago. Before him.

Frieren made a sound. It was the first sound she had made since she put her hand down on the wet bed. It was very small. It was not a word.

"That's right," said Seiko, much more gently. "There you are. Come on."



They took her to the washroom at the end of the family wing, the one with the big tub Wakana had built after the bathroom wars. Seiko led. Maki held one arm. Milim followed behind, very quietly, carrying a lamp, because nobody had told her not to and somebody needed to.

Seiko ran the water hot. Nobody else would have known how to make the Muslin's pipes give hot water at two in the morning, and Seiko did it in about a minute.

Frieren stood in the middle of the room and did not move.

So Maki undid the buttons of the shirt for her, one at a time, the way you undo them for a child who is too tired. She took it off and folded it, carefully, and put it on the floor by the door. Nobody said what would happen to it. Everyone knew it was not going to be worn again.

They got her into the water.

It went red. Not all at once. In slow clouds, from her hands first, then her arms, then her knees, spreading out and thinning until the whole tub was faintly pink and the lamp on the edge made it look like the inside of a shell.

Frieren sat in it with her knees drawn up and looked at the color.

She did not cry. Not yet. She was past where crying was. She sat in the water with her arms around her knees and shook, steadily, from the inside out, and did not seem to notice she was doing it.

Maki washed her hands.

She did it the way she would have washed a wound. Thoroughly, without hurry, each finger, then the backs, then the palms, then under the nails. That was where it stayed longest, and Maki worked it out patiently with her thumbnail, one nail at a time. Frieren watched her do it as if from very far away.

Milim washed her hair.

Nobody had asked her to. She simply put the lamp down on the edge of the tub and knelt behind Frieren and gathered up the long white hair, the ends dark and stiff, and lowered it into the water. Then she began to work the dark out of it with her fingers. She was terrible at it. She had never washed anyone's hair in her life. She pulled, and tangled it, and said *sorry, sorry* under her breath every time. But she did not stop, and her hands did not shake. Slowly, strand by strand, the white came back.

Seiko sat on a stool by the door with her arms folded and talked.

She talked about nothing. About the weather in her prefecture when she was a girl. About a ghost she had once known who had haunted a public bath for forty years because nobody thanked it for keeping the water warm. About her husband, who had been a fool, and whom she had loved for fifty years in spite of it, and who had once fallen off a roof trying to fix a gutter and lain in a hospital for nine days. She had sat beside him for every one of the nine, she said, and on the tenth he had woken up and complained about the food.

"They wake up," said Seiko. "That's the thing nobody tells you. The idiots wake up and complain about the food." She sniffed. "Then you have to decide whether to hit them."

Frieren made a sound.

It might have been the beginning of a laugh. It might have been the beginning of something else. Whatever it was, it cracked something open, and what came out after it was crying.

It came out of her all at once, the way the water had gone red. Great, ugly, helpless sobs, the kind she had never in a thousand years let anyone see, the kind she had perhaps never had at all. She folded forward over her knees in the pink water and cried. Milim kept washing her hair. Maki kept holding her hand. Seiko kept talking, quietly, about nothing.



They dressed her afterward in clean clothes.

Maki went back to the room for them. She did not look at the bed. She went straight to the closet in the inner corner, turned a quarter so a treaty boardwalk could reach the landing, and stood in front of the shelves. On one side were Frieren's things. On the other side, much larger, were his.

She took down his hoodie.

The black-and-orange one. Too big in the shoulders. Sleeves past the fingers.

She brought it back to the washroom, along with Frieren's own soft shorts, and helped her into both. When Frieren's head came out through the neck of the hoodie and the too-long sleeves fell down over her hands, she stood very still for a moment, the way someone stands when they have been handed something they did not know they needed.

"So he can find you," said Maki. "When he wakes up. So the first thing he sees is you, looking like you."

Frieren did not say anything.

She pulled the sleeves down further over her hands, and held on to them.

Seiko stood up from her stool.

"Now," she said. "You're going to sit outside that bay door. You're not going to go in. You're going to sit outside it, where you're near and you're not in anybody's way. And Milim is going to sit with you, because she's too strong to be any use to anyone else tonight and she knows it. And when the boy wakes up and complains about the food, you're going to be clean."

Milim nodded seriously.

Frieren looked at Seiko for a long time.

"Thank you," she said. Her voice was wrecked.

"Don't," said Seiko. "I'm old. I've done this before. It's the only use I am." She turned toward the door. "Come on. Before I decide to hit somebody myself."
