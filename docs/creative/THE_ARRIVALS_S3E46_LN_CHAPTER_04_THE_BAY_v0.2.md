# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 18
## Chapter 4 — The Bay

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 4  
**Continuity state:** Same night | Maomao, Senku, Kusuri, Yuta and Ciel work | Stark, Mikasa and Momo give blood; Senku refuses Eren, who stands with Kaneki | Diablo guards the room | Qifrey and the sisters hold the fort

The medical bay was the room at the far end of the Muslin, as far from the kitchen as Maomao's rules could put it. It had one table, two lamps, a shelf of labeled jars, and a basin. Tonight it had five people in it, and one of them was dying.

Maomao had the table cleared and the lamps turned up before Yuta came through the door. She did not look at Mau's face. She looked at the hole.

She had read about wounds like this. She had never seen one on a living person. Nobody had ever told her what it looked like when a body tried to die and somebody else would not let it.

That was what she was seeing now. Yuta laid Mau on the table without once taking his hands off him. The hole in the middle of Mau was trying to bleed, and wanting to be a hole, and wanting to be open to the air. Where Yuta's palms pressed on either side of it, the edges were trying to close. Flesh crawled toward flesh under that fierce warm pressure and then fell back, and crawled toward it again. Yuta was not healing it. Not yet. He was holding it at the edge, refusing, the way you hold a door shut against a wind that will not stop.

"How long can you do that?" said Maomao.

"As long as it takes," said Yuta.

He did not say it like a hero. He said it like a fact.

Maomao looked at him for one second longer. Then she rolled up her sleeves.



They worked.

It was not like the stories. There was no single moment where everything turned. There was only work. Small things, one after another, each one done as fast and well as the person doing it knew how, for hours.

Yuta held the edges and poured.

Maomao worked inside what he was holding. She cleaned what could be cleaned. She tied off what was spilling that his technique was not reaching fast enough. She packed what was open. She worked with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder and her hair tied back with a strip of cloth torn off her own nightgown, because there had not been time to find a ribbon. She talked the whole time, in a low steady voice, to nobody, naming each thing as she did it, so that if her hands slipped someone else would know what she had been doing.

Senku did the blood.

He had never done it on a person. He had done it in his head a hundred times, on paper, in the long nights of the audit. He had once, half joking, put a line in his notebook headed WHAT WE DO IF SOMEONE LOSES TOO MUCH, under a list of things the settlement could not yet do. Then he had crossed out *cannot* and spent two weeks making sure it could. It had needles, which Kusuri had made. Tubing, from an animal gut cleaned until Maomao had stopped making faces at it. A cross-test that took four minutes and that he had checked against seventeen volunteers, out of curiosity. Nobody had ever thought it would be needed.

He went out into the corridor.

There were already people there. Of course there were. Half the house had followed the line on the floor.

"I need blood," said Senku. "Volunteers. Now. I'll test you. It takes four minutes and most of you won't match. Don't be offended."

They lined up.

He would remember that, afterward, more than anything else about the night. How fast they lined up. Mikasa first, rolling up her sleeve before he had finished the sentence. Then Stark. Then Okarun, then Momo, then Wakana, then Kita, then Ori, white-faced, holding out her arm. Rimuru tried. Senku had to tell him, very gently, that whatever was in a slime was not going to help. Kaneki stood at the back of the line for a long time. Then he quietly took himself out of it, because he knew what was in his own blood and would not risk it, and went and stood against the wall instead with his hands over his face.

Eren came out of his room, saw the line, and got in it without asking what it was for.

Senku tested them in the order they stood. Most of them did not match. He turned them away one after another with *thank you, no, thank you, no*. Every single one of them looked as if he had told them something terrible about themselves.

Stark matched.

So did Mikasa.

So did Momo, who said "Good," as if she had been personally expecting it, and rolled up her sleeve.

Then Senku got to Eren, and did not test him at all.

"Not you," said Senku.

Eren looked at him. "You haven't checked."

"I don't need to." Senku's voice was not unkind. It was just very tired and very certain. "Whatever makes you what you are lives in you somewhere, and I don't know where. Neither do you. Not here. Not in this world." He glanced at the door of the bay. "And there's something in him that reads everything it touches. I'm not putting a Titan where it can read it."

Eren stood very still.

For a moment the corridor thought he would argue. He had the face for it. Mikasa, ahead of him in the line, half turned around.

Then Eren looked down the corridor at Kaneki. Kaneki was against the wall with his hands over his face. He had taken himself out of the line for the same reason, and nobody had needed to tell him.

Eren stepped out of the line. He went and stood against the wall beside Kaneki and did not say anything. After a while, without looking up, Kaneki moved over an inch to make room.

Eren had been in the house for less than two months. A week ago, the people in this corridor had voted to let him through a door. He and Kaneki stood there all night, the only two people in the corridor whose blood was too dangerous to give. Both of them had got in line anyway.

Senku looked at the three who had matched.

"Sit down," he said. "One at a time. Stark first."

Stark sat down in the corridor with his back against the wall and his arm out. Senku put the needle in, and the line ran dark into the bay, under the door. Stark watched it go and did not say anything at all. When he was done, he did not stand up. He stayed sitting on the floor by the door, very pale, until Fern came and sat beside him and held his other hand.

Then Mikasa. She sat down where she could see the stretch of wall where Eren was standing, held out her arm, and watched him the whole time instead of the needle.

Then Momo, last. She gave hers with her jaw set and her free hand clenched in the hem of her nightshirt. When it was done, she stood up much too fast, sat down again much too fast, and announced that she was going to make soup for everybody and that nobody was to argue. Nobody argued. She went, a little unsteadily. Twenty minutes later the whole corridor smelled of soup.



Kusuri did everything.

That was the only way to describe it. She did whatever anybody asked, the instant they asked it, and most of the time before they had finished asking. She boiled water. She fetched cloth. She held lamps. She held a jar steady while Maomao poured. She held Yuta's cup of water to his mouth so he could drink without taking his hands off Mau. She ran to the store and back eleven times.

She did not touch her own bench once all night.

There was nothing on it that would help. She knew that. She had known it the moment she came through the door and saw the hole. Somewhere in the long run of the night she found, to her own surprise, that she did not mind. She did not need to be the clever one tonight. She did not need to make anything. She needed to be fast, and quiet, and exactly where Maomao's hand reached out next. For once in her life, that was enough.

At some point Maomao, without looking up, said, "Good." Kusuri did not know what she had done that was good. She decided it did not matter.



And inside him, where no one in the room could see, Ciel was fighting.

Rimuru stood at the head of the table with one hand on Mau's forehead, because that was what let Ciel reach furthest. Every so often, without being asked, he said aloud what she said. It came in fragments.

"She's found it. The pattern. It's still running."

"It thinks the walls are an injury. That's the thing. It's been looking at them for weeks. Analyzing them. It doesn't understand what they are. It just sees something in him that wasn't there when he arrived, something that holds things back, something—" He stopped, listening. "—something that wasn't there before. So it decided they're damage. And it's trying to heal the damage the only way it knows. By going back."

"She's trying to make it stop."

"It won't stop. It can't hear her. It's not a person. It's just doing what it does. Observe, analyze, understand." He swallowed. "It understood the wrong thing."

"Why now?" said Maomao. "Why tonight?"

Rimuru listened.

"Because it finished," he said slowly. "It's the same thing that was fixing his memories. All those months. Rethreading. Ciel says it stopped a few weeks ago, because there was nothing left it could fix." His mouth twisted. "So it went looking for something else that was broken. And the only thing in him that wasn't there when he arrived is the walls."

"She has to teach it."

Maomao, her hands inside Mau, glanced up for one second.

"Teach it *what*?"

Rimuru was quiet for a long time, listening.

"That the walls aren't a wound," he said. "That they're part of him now. That they're not something wrong. They're something that happened to him, that he lived through. That they belong." He closed his eyes. "She's trying to show it everything since the containment. The flowers. The house. The storm. Every day he's lived with the walls. To prove they're not damage. That he's been *alive*, with them. That this is what he looks like now."

"Is it working?"

Rimuru did not answer for a while.

"She doesn't know yet," he said.



Diablo stood outside the door of the room with the east window.

Rimuru had asked him to, on his way past, in about four words: *Nobody goes in. Nobody looks.* Diablo had bowed and taken up his position. He had not moved since.

He could not keep the whole house from knowing. Nobody could have. Too many people had opened their eyes to Rimuru's shout. Too many had seen Yuta running with something in his arms, or heard the word *bay*, or stepped out of their doors and seen the line of dark drops that led from this door all the way down the corridor to that one.

But he could keep them from seeing the room.

So when the sisters came, he was ready.

They came together, the way they did everything. Umaru in front, in her hamster hood, with her face set. Coco beside her, already crying, trying not to. Suika, small and fierce. Bocchi behind them, white as paper, her guitar case forgotten in her room for the first time anyone could remember. They had followed the drops backward. They had worked out, the way children always work things out, exactly which room they came from.

"Let us in," said Umaru.

"No," said Diablo.

"We want to see. We want to help. Is he— is Mau—"

"Rimuru-sama has asked that no one enter this room," said Diablo. He said it very gently, which was not a voice the sisters had ever heard him use. "It is not a room to see tonight."

"We're not babies—"

"No," Diablo agreed. "You are not."

He stepped forward into the corridor. He drew the door shut behind him, all the way, until it clicked. Then he looked down at the four of them in the lamplight. In the faces of the older ones, Mau's sisters in every way that counted, he saw that they had already understood far more than he could take away from them.

"Go back to sleep, little ones," said Diablo softly. "Some things are not for you to carry yet. You are allowed to keep a little innocence. Let me keep this one for you."

Umaru's chin was shaking.

She opened her mouth to argue.



Qifrey came down the corridor before she could.

He had been at the bay door. He had stood there long enough to understand that there was nothing in that room he could do that Maomao was not already doing better. Then he had turned around and come looking for the children. He had known, without anyone telling him, that this was where they would be.

He took in the four of them, and Diablo, and the closed door. He understood all of it at once.

"Fort," said Qifrey.

Umaru turned.

"There's a fort," said Qifrey. "In the common room. From the war. It's still standing, isn't it? I was told it was a monument."

"...It is a monument," said Umaru, uncertainly.

"Then it needs defending," said Qifrey. "Tonight. Somebody has to hold it. All night." He looked at each of them in turn, gravely, the way he looked at an apprentice about to draw a difficult circle. "I can't do it alone. Coco. Bocchi. Suika. Umaru. Will you hold it with me?"

They all knew what he was doing.

Every one of them. Even Suika. They knew it was a distraction, and he knew they knew, and they knew he knew. For a long moment nobody moved.

Then Coco, crying, took Qifrey's hand.

"Okay," she said.

They went to the common room. They crawled inside the fort that Umaru had built during the war. It was still standing because nobody had ever had the heart to take it down: cushions, crates, a folding screen, a toll booth with snacks in it. Qifrey went in with them, crouched almost double, and sat in the middle of them. They told stories. They played cards. Umaru won, as she always did, and did not cheer.

And every few minutes, without anyone saying anything, one of them would put her head out of the fort and look down the long corridor at the lamp burning under the door of the bay. Then she would come back in, and nobody would ask what she had seen.

They held the fort all night.

Every one of them stayed awake.
