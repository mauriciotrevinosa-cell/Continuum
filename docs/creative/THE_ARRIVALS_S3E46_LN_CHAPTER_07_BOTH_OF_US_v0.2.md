# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 18
## Chapter 7 — Both of Us

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 7  
**Continuity state:** Morning of Day 3 | Mau wakes with no memory of the night | the house's one unanimous rule: that night is not spoken of unless he or she speaks first

Mau woke up a little after dawn, and the first thing he knew was that he did not know anything.

He remembered falling asleep. He remembered that very clearly. Frieren on top of him, heavy and warm, her hair everywhere. Her making him say things twice and correcting him. *That green.* Kissing her so she would stop talking. The promise about the tree, and *idiot*, and her arms around his neck, and falling asleep.

That was the last thing.

Now he was lying on his back, and something was wrong with his body.

Not wrong in a way he could point to. Wrong everywhere at once. He felt the way you feel after a fever, or a fall, or a day of hauling stone. Hollowed out, heavy, aching in places he did not have names for. There was something across the middle of him, tight and strange, a bandage or a dressing. When he breathed in too deep, something under it pulled.

He did not say anything about it.

He turned his head.

The bed was different. The sheets were the good ones from the closet, the ones Frieren liked. The mattress was firmer than he remembered.

And Frieren was not on him.

Frieren was in her reading chair, which was not where her reading chair lived. It had been pulled across the room to the side of the bed. She was asleep in it, curled up small with her knees drawn up, wearing his black-and-orange hoodie with the sleeves over her hands. In her lap, held tight in both arms the way a child holds a toy in the dark, was Rimuru. In slime form. Also asleep.

Mau looked at this for a long time.

He did not understand any of it.

He shifted, trying to sit up a little. The thing across his middle pulled, sharply. He made a small sound before he could stop himself.

Frieren's eyes opened.

So did Rimuru's.

Both of them, at exactly the same moment, as if they had been sleeping with one ear open for just that sound. They stared at him.

Mau looked from one to the other. From Frieren in the chair, to Rimuru in her lap, held tight in both her arms.

"Frieren," he said. His voice came out rough. "Are you cheating on me?"



Frieren launched herself out of the chair.

Rimuru went flying. He landed on the floor with a small wet sound. She was halfway onto the bed, arms already out, already reaching, before something caught her in mid-air.

She stopped.

She froze there, half on the bed, her arms out, an inch from him. Mau saw her face. He had never seen it look like that, not in the clearing, not on the road, not anywhere. It was terrified. Terrified of *him*, or of touching him, or of what might happen if she did, as if he were made of something that might come apart under her hands.

"Frieren," he said. "Hey."

She did not move.

"It's okay," said Mau. He did not know what was okay. He did not know what had happened. He knew that she was afraid to touch him. He knew he could not stand it. "Come here. It's okay."

Something went out of her face.

The fear did not go. But something stronger than it came up underneath it and moved it aside.

She came the last inch.

She did not throw herself on him. She put her arms around him very carefully, from the side, around his shoulders and his neck. Nowhere near the thing across his middle. She lay her head down against his, cheek to cheek, and held on, and he felt her shaking.

He put his arm around her.

From the floor, a small blue voice said, "Ow."



Rimuru told him.

Not all of it. Not the room as it had looked, and not the floor, and not the line on the boards. Rimuru had cleaned those things, and they did not need to exist anymore. But enough. That the pattern in him had looked at the walls for weeks and decided they were an injury. That it had tried to heal him by going back to before the walls. That it did not understand time, and had reached for the last moment his body had been rebuilt from nothing.

"After Sukuna," said Mau slowly.

"After Sukuna."

Mau lifted his hand off Frieren's back and touched the dressing across his middle, very lightly.

"The hole."

"The hole," said Rimuru. "For two days. Yuta held it. Maomao worked inside it. Senku put three people's blood in you." He paused. "Stark. Mikasa. Eren."

Mau stared at him.

"*Eren?*"

"He got in line," said Rimuru. "Didn't ask what it was for. Just got in line." He breathed out. "And Ciel spent two days teaching the thing in you that the walls aren't a wound. That they're you now. She showed it everything. Every day. Until it understood." He looked at the dressing. "And then your body helped. The pathway, from last time. It woke up. It's closed now. Not healed. You've got weeks of lying in this bed ahead of you and Maomao's going to be unbearable about it."

Mau did not say anything for a long time.

He thought about all of it. Two days. A hole. Three people's blood. Yuta holding something shut with his hands for two days. Ciel inside him, showing something that could not hear her every day he had lived since the walls went up, over and over, until it understood.

And Frieren.

Frieren had been lying on him. When it started. She would have been the one to—

He looked at her, cheek against his, shaking.

"Oh," said Mau, very quietly. "Oh, no."



Rimuru went to the door.

"I'll tell Maomao you're awake," he said. "In a while. Not yet." He paused in the doorway, a small blue shape, and looked back at the two of them on the bed. "Take your time."

He shut the door.



Frieren cried.

She had not cried in two days. Not in the corridor, not when Rimuru came out and said it stopped, not in the chair in the dark. She had cried once in the washroom, in the pink water, with Seiko talking about nothing. Then she had stopped, because there had been a door to sit outside, and you could not sit outside a door properly if you were crying.

Now there was no door.

She cried into his shoulder until his shirt was wet through. She cried the way she had cried in the pink water, and then past it, into something that had been waiting a great deal longer than two days. When she could speak at all, the first thing she said was:

"You promised."

Mau held her.

"You promised. You said you'd be there. In a hundred years. When the tree's big enough. You said I couldn't take it back." Her voice broke completely. "You *promised*, and two days later you were—you had a—"

"I know," said Mau. "I know." He turned his face into her hair. "That's why I'm still here. Isn't it?"

Frieren made a sound that was half sob and half something else.

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's not."

"Okay," said Mau. "It's not."

She cried harder.



And then, slowly, she started to talk.

It came out of her in pieces, between the crying, not in any order. It was not about him. That was the thing Mau would understand, lying there, listening. It was not *you did this to me*, or *you scared me*, or *never again*. It was about her.

About lying on him and thinking about herself. About discovering, that night, that she liked who she was with him. Liked the feet, and the kicking, and being someone who made him say things twice. About putting her hand down and finding it wet. About not screaming, because she could not find the scream. About sitting outside a door for two days, listening for a breath she could not hear.

"I don't know how to do it anymore," said Frieren into his shoulder.

"Do what?"

"Be alive. Without you." She was not dramatic about it. She said it the way she said true things, flatly, as if describing a spell that would not work. "I used to know. For a thousand years I knew how. I lost everyone. I walked away from funerals. I went on. I was good at it." Her hands tightened in his shirt. "I don't know how anymore. You made me forget how. If you die, I don't—"

She stopped.

"Not like the stories," she said. "Not dying of a broken heart, or poison, or a dagger. That's stupid. That's for plays." She pressed her face harder into his neck. "I mean the part of me that learned how to be alive. With you. I don't think it knows how to be alive without you. I don't think it would. I think it would just stop, and the rest of me would go on walking, for another thousand years, and it would be like it was before. Before you. And I don't want to go back to that. I *can't* go back to that."

Mau was quiet for a long time.



He was thinking about Sukuna.

About the field. About the line he had stepped into on purpose, with his eyes open, because he had already known what was coming. Frieren saying *no*. Him going anyway. And the one clear thought he had had in the half second before the hand. It had not been a thought about himself at all.

*She'll be alive.*

That had been the whole of it. He had accepted the rest, as long as she was safe. He had thought that was love. He had thought it was the most anyone could give.

Afterward, in a room with the door shut, she had told him what it really was. *You decided I would survive losing you.* He had promised her then never to hide something that large again, never to decide everything alone, and he had meant it. But underneath the promise, he understood now, he had gone on believing the first thing. That she would survive it. That she was the one who walked away from funerals and kept walking. That if it ever came to that again, her being alive would be enough.

She had just told him it wasn't. That she wouldn't.

"I was wrong," said Mau.

Frieren went still against him.

"With Sukuna," said Mau. "When I stepped in. The last thing I thought was *she'll be alive*. That was all of it. I thought if you lived, it didn't matter if I didn't." He breathed carefully, around the dressing. "You told me afterward that I'd decided you would survive losing me. And I said sorry for deciding alone. But I never said sorry for the other part. Because I still thought you would." He shook his head slowly against the pillow. "I was wrong. That's not enough. That was never enough."

He turned his head so he could see her face.

"If I live," said Mau, "and you live. Both of us. That's when I'm alive. Not one. Both." He swallowed. "That's the only way it counts."

Frieren looked at him.

Her face was wet and red and wrecked, and her ears were drooping. She did not look like a thousand-year-old mage. She looked like someone who had very nearly lost the only thing she had ever wanted, and was holding it again, and did not quite believe it yet.

"Promise," said Frieren.

"I already promised."

"Promise again."

"I promise," said Mau. "Both of us. A hundred years. The tree. I'm going to be there."

Frieren put her face back in his neck.

"You can't take it back," she said.

"I know."

"You said that last time."

"I meant it last time."

They lay there.

After a long while, the crying slowed and slowed and finally stopped. There was only her breathing against his neck, and his, and the east window full of morning.

Then Mau said, very seriously:

"I'm hungry. Is that weird? I feel like it's weird that I'm hungry."

Frieren went completely still.

Then she laughed. Wet and cracked and helpless, into his shoulder. It shook her whole body, and he had to hold on to her so she would not jostle the dressing.

"What?" said Mau. "What's funny?"

"Seiko," said Frieren, laughing and crying at once. "Seiko said. She said you'd wake up and complain about the food."

"I'm not complaining. I just said I'm hungry."

"That's what she said you'd say."

"What does Seiko have to do with anything?"

"I'll tell you later," said Frieren, and laughed until she cried again, and held on.



The house found out he was awake within ten minutes. Rimuru told Maomao, and Maomao told nobody, which in that house was the same as telling everybody.

They did not come in. They were not allowed. But they stood in the corridor outside, in a long quiet crowd, until Maomao came out and said he was awake and talking and hungry and fine. Then the corridor did something nobody would ever describe afterward, because nobody could. Several people sat down on the floor. Umaru cried so hard she had to be carried back to the fort. Yuta, woken by Maki, came and stood in the doorway for a full minute without saying a word. Then he went back to bed and slept for another day.

That evening, in the common room, Fern opened the ledger to a clean page and wrote one line.

Nobody had asked her to. Nobody had proposed it. But when she turned the ledger around on the long table, every person in the room read it, and every person nodded. Not one of them said a word. One at a time, without being asked, they came and wrote their names under it. The whole of Ring One and Ring Two. Over the next few days, the witches and the others who had been in the house that night asked quietly to see the page, and signed it too.

The line said:

*That night is not spoken of. Not to him, not to her, not to anyone. Unless he speaks of it first, or she does.*

It was the only rule the house ever made without a single argument.

None of them ever broke it.
