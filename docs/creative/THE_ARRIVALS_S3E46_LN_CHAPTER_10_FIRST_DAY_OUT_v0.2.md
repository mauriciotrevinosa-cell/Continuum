# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 18
## Chapter 10 — First Day Out

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 10  
**Continuity state:** Day 29 after Mau wakes | Maomao's slip: OUTSIDE. SLOWLY. | the fort escorts him | three rumors, 'dead' winning | washing court, the merchant's pears | Seiko's fan | thanks in one line each | the ink tree has nine leaves

On the twenty-ninth morning, Maomao signed a slip of paper and handed it to Mau without a word.

It said: OUTSIDE. SLOWLY. HOME BY THE SECOND BELL. — M.

Mau read it twice. Then he looked up at her.

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"I can just go?"

"You can go slowly," said Maomao. "If I hear that you ran, climbed, lifted, carried, fought or argued with Milim, the slip is void, and you go back to bed for a week." She paused. "Arguing with Milim counts as lifting."

He had meant to go out quietly. He had a plan for it. He would walk out of the front door of the Muslin in the middle of the morning, when the house was busy, cross the yard, go as far as the washing court and come back, and nobody would make a fuss.

The plan lasted as far as the front door.

The fort was waiting outside it.

Not some of the fort. All of it. They were arranged on either side of the step in two lines, the way you line a road for a procession. Umaru stood at the front in her hood with a clipboard. Behind her, in no particular order, were Coco, Suika, Bocchi, Ori, the Witch Hat girls, Kessoku, Hakari's group, Marin, Mai, Rem with a basket, and Anko with her notebook. Milim was not in the line. She was on the roof of the Muslin, sitting on the edge with her legs dangling, because Maomao had made her promise not to touch him and she did not trust herself at ground level.

"How long have you been out here?" said Mau.

"Since the first bell," said Umaru.

"How did you know?"

"Nano," said Umaru.

"I calculated it," said Nano. "From the schedule."

"The fort has been planning this for nine days," said Coco.

Mau looked at the two lines of them. Then he looked behind him at Frieren, who was standing in the doorway wearing an expression of complete innocence that did not fool anybody.

"You knew," he said.

"I'm escorting you," said Frieren. "That's different."



Frieren escorted him the way an elf escorts a person she has very nearly lost, which is to say badly.

She walked on his left, close, with one hand hovering an inch from his elbow the whole time, not touching it. Every time he took a step that was slightly longer than the step before, the hand came a little closer. Every time he stopped, it stopped too. When he turned to look at her, she was looking at the sky with tremendous interest.

"I'm not going to fall over," said Mau.

"I know," said Frieren.

The hand stayed exactly where it was.

Rimuru rode on his right shoulder, in slime form, as light as he could make himself, which was very light.

"Ciel's idea," said Rimuru.

*It was not my idea*, said Ciel.

"It was a little your idea."

*I said that if he fell, it would be better if someone were already on him.*

"That's the idea," said Rimuru. "That's literally the idea."

*I would like the record to show that I said it as a hypothetical.*

Mau laughed, carefully. It only pulled a little. That was new. He did not say anything about it, and Frieren's hand came an inch closer anyway.



Anko caught up with them at the edge of the yard and fell into step on Frieren's other side, notebook open.

"Briefing," she said.

"I'm on a walk."

"You're on a walk in public for the first time in four weeks," said Anko. "So you get a briefing." She turned a page. "Four weeks is a long time for people who can't see you. There are three stories going round the river towns. In one of them, the summon got out and you're locked in a cellar. In one of them, you've gone away to a city in the west. And in one of them, you're dead."

Mau considered this.

"Which one's winning?"

"Dead's doing very well," said Anko. "Dead's very popular. There's a version where Frieren buried you under the tree."

Frieren made a small, sharp sound and did not look away from the sky.

"So," said Anko, closing the notebook, "you're going to the washing court. You're going to wash something. You're going to be seen being boring. Maomao's orders, from the council. You remember."

"I remember."

"Good," said Anko. She squeezed his shoulder once, very lightly, on the side Rimuru was not on, and went on ahead.



The washing court was full, as it always was in the middle of the morning. Water ran along the stone channels Senku had built, and there were women and men at the long troughs and children underfoot and a great deal of noise.

The noise changed when he came in.

It did not stop. It changed. Heads turned. A woman at the nearest trough stopped wringing a sheet and simply held it, dripping. Somebody said something low to somebody else.

Mau walked to an empty place at the trough. The fort, who had been told by Umaru that an escort did not crowd, crowded only a little. Rem handed him a shirt out of her basket. It was one shirt, because Maomao had said one, and Rem had brought exactly one. He rolled up his sleeves and put it in the water and washed it.

It was not interesting. That was the point. It took a long time, because his hands were still slower than he liked. Nobody helped him, because he had asked them not to, and it was the most ordinary thing he had done in a month.

At the far end of the court was a stall. It belonged to a native merchant who used to sell at the edge of the plaza, before the plaza, and who had moved his stall here afterward without ever saying why. Mau finished the shirt, wrung it out, handed it to Rem, and walked down to the stall.

The merchant watched him come the whole way.

"Morning," said Mau. "Do you still have the dried pears?"

The merchant looked at him for a long moment.

"I heard you were dead," he said.

"Not today," said Mau.

The merchant looked at him for a moment longer. Then something in his shoulders let go, and he reached under the counter and put the dried pears on it.

"Two," said Mau. "One's for Ori."

He paid. The merchant took the coins, and then, after a hesitation, pushed a third pear across the counter.

"For the walk," he said.

A small boy had come up behind Mau and was staring at him with frank, enormous interest. He had been one of the children at the troughs. His mother was three troughs away and had not noticed yet.

"Are you the one with the monster in his shadow?" said the boy.

Behind Mau, the fort went very still.

"It's a summon," said Mau. "I'm still training it."

"Can I see it?"

"Not today."

The boy thought about that.

"Were you sick?"

"I was," said Mau. "I'm better."

"Okay," said the boy, and lost interest completely and went back to the trough. His mother looked up, saw who he had been talking to, and opened her mouth. Then she saw Mau giving Ori a dried pear, and Ori taking it with both hands, and she closed her mouth again and went back to her washing.

Anko, by the gate, wrote something down.



Seiko was in her chair in the shade at the edge of the plaza. She had been there every morning that month, Rem said, which was not something Seiko had ever done before.

She watched him come across the new stones. She watched him the whole way, with her hands folded on top of a closed paper fan, and did not say anything until he was standing in front of her.

"Seiko-san," said Mau.

Seiko unfolded the fan, reached up, and smacked him across the top of the head with it.

It did not hurt. It made a very satisfying noise.

"What was that for?"

"You complained about the food," said Seiko.

"I said I was *hungry*—"

"I heard what you said." She folded the fan and put her hands back on top of it. "I told the girl what you'd do, and you did it. Every time." She sniffed. "They always do."

Then she looked at him for a long moment, and her face did something it did not do very often.

"Good," she said quietly. "Go on. Go and be boring somewhere."



He spoke to a few people that morning about the night he did not remember. He was allowed to. Apart from Frieren, he was the only one who was. Every one of them answered in a single line and then talked about something else, and he understood, after the second time, that this was on purpose.

Stark was at the woodpile.

"Rimuru told me," said Mau. "Thank you."

Stark split a log. "You'd have done it," he said, and split another one. "Fern says you're coming to dinner tomorrow. Don't be late. She's making the thing with the onions."

Mikasa was at the route watch above the bridge road with Eren, and Kaneki was with them. Since the night of the bay, the three of them had been seen together a good deal, and nobody had remarked on it.

"Thank you," Mau said to Mikasa.

"You're welcome," said Mikasa, and went back to watching the road.

He looked at Eren.

"You got in line," said Mau.

Eren shrugged, with his arms folded on the railing and his eyes on the road. "Didn't do anything."

"You got in line," said Mau again. Then he looked at Kaneki. "Both of you."

Kaneki did not say anything. But he looked at Mau for a moment, the way Mau sometimes looked at the left side of his own face in a mirror, and nodded once.

"The roads are quiet this week," said Eren, to the road. "Too quiet. Mikasa thinks so too."

"Tell Anko," said Mau.

"I did," said Eren. "She wrote it down."

Momo found him in the plaza. Mau had not even got the second word of *thank you* out before she held up a hand.

"Don't," said Momo. "You owe me dishes. A week of dishes. Two weeks. I'll tell you when." She narrowed her eyes. "And you're eating lunch. Here. Where people can see you. Grandma's orders."

"Seiko said that?"

"Seiko said that," said Momo, "and then she said I'm not allowed to hit you because she already did."

Yuta was the last.

He was at the training yard behind the Muslin, alone, which was unusual. He was sitting on the low wall with his sword across his knees, not doing anything with it. When he saw Mau coming, with Frieren at his elbow and a slime on his shoulder and most of the fort trailing behind at a respectful distance, he did not get up.

Mau sat down on the wall next to him. It took him a moment. Yuta did not help, because Mau would have hated it, and they both knew it.

"Rimuru told me," said Mau.

"Don't," said Yuta.

"I'm going to anyway."

Yuta looked at the sword across his knees.

"Two days," said Mau. "Rimuru said two days."

"It was fine."

"Rimuru said you couldn't stand up at the end."

"Maki held me up," said Yuta. "It was fine."

"Thank you," said Mau.

Yuta did not say anything for a long time.

"Roof," he said at last. "Next week. When Maomao says you can do ladders." He paused. "You're bringing the snacks. All of them. For a month."

"Okay."

"And tell Rika—" Mau started.

"She knows," said Yuta. Something cold and enormous and fond moved, very briefly, in the air behind him, and then was gone. "She heard you."



By the time the second bell was close, he was tired in a way he had not been since the bay. It was a good tired. It was the kind you get from a long day, not from being broken.

He did not go straight home.

He went the long way round, through the garden at the side of the Muslin, to the ring of small stakes at its edge. The house was going to be built around that ring and never on it. In the middle of the ring was the shoot.

It was not a shoot anymore. It had been two leaves the day Frieren stood in a doorway and said she could wait a hundred years. Now it was a stem as long as his hand, with nine leaves, silver-green, a color nobody had a name for. Coco had counted them that morning and written the number on a slip of paper tied to the stake.

Mau sat down on the grass outside the ring, slowly. Frieren sat down beside him. Rimuru slid off his shoulder and onto the grass and lay there like a small blue puddle in the sun, and was asleep within a minute.

The fort did not follow them into the garden. Somebody, probably Umaru, had decided that.

Mau looked at the shoot for a long time.

"It grew," he said.

"Four weeks," said Frieren. "Things grow."

He held his hand up beside it, the way you measure a child against a doorframe.

"Ninety-nine years and some months to go," he said.

Frieren looked at the shoot, and then at him.

"Idiot," she said.

But she leaned her head on his shoulder, very carefully, on the side away from the dressing, and stayed there. The hand that had hovered an inch from his elbow all morning finally came the last inch and held on.

They sat there until the second bell, and then a little past it. Maomao, who had been watching from the kitchen window, decided she had not heard the bell.
