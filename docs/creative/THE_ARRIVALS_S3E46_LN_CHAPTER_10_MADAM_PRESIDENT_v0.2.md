# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 18
## Chapter 10 — Madam President

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 10  
**Continuity state:** Days 11-14 | Umaru declares a presidency; Agott runs as Opposition | Milim becomes Guardian of the Fort ('strongest isn't the same as in charge'); Rimuru honorary citizen | Anko audits; 12-11 among 24, Ryo spoils | sashes PRESIDENT (TEMPORARY) and OPPOSITION | Aira arrives late and becomes the Protector | the fort's constitution, Article 6

It was Agott who started it, which surprised nobody.

On the first morning in the new fort, while Umaru was handing out breakfast assignments from the doorway of the toll booth, Agott raised her hand. She did not wait to be called on.

"Who decided that?"

Umaru looked at her. "Decided what?"

"Breakfast assignments. Who's on the stage this week. The annex. The six cushions." Agott folded her arms. "Who decides things in here? It's not four of you anymore. You said so yourself. There are twenty of us."

There was a short, interested silence.

"The founders decide," said Umaru.

"Why?"

"Because we founded it."

"That's not a reason," said Agott. "That's a history."

Coco made a small, involuntary sound, the sound of a person watching two of her favorite people about to disagree about something important.

Umaru considered Agott for a long moment. Then she stood up, very straight, in her hamster hood, in the doorway of the toll booth, and made the decision that would occupy the entire fort for the next three days.

"Fine," said Umaru. "Then I'm President."

"*President?*"

"President of the Fort," said Umaru. "With a constitution, and everything."

"On whose authority?"

"The fort's."

"The fort is *us*," said Agott.

"Then the fort," said Umaru, with the expression of someone three moves ahead, "can vote."



It turned out that a presidency needed an election, and an election needed candidates, and candidates needed opponents. Umaru had not considered that last part. It had seemed obvious to her that she would be President, the way it had seemed obvious, at the start of the war, that she would get the room.

Agott declared her candidacy within the hour.

"For President?" said Umaru.

"For Opposition," said Agott. "Somebody has to stop you."

"Opposition isn't a job."

"In a real government," said Agott, "it's the most important one."

Mai, unexpectedly, agreed with her. So did Karane, who agreed with anyone who was against something. So did Nano, who said she supported a functioning opposition on principle and would be voting according to policy.

"What's your policy?" said Umaru.

"Not you," said Agott.

That turned out to be a surprisingly popular platform.



The campaign lasted two days.

Coco drew posters for Umaru in charcoal, as she had in the war. They showed a small noble figure in a hamster hood leading a great crowd of people into a fort with the sun rising behind it. Tetia drew posters for Agott. They showed Agott with a stern face and a great many rules, written very small. Agott took one look at them and said she had never been so accurately represented in her life.

Milim, hearing that there was an election, stood up so fast under the Milim Beam that the hamster flag swayed.

"I want to run!"

The fort went very quiet. Everyone did the same arithmetic at once. Milim was the Head of Fun. Milim was loved by every single citizen of the fort, including the ones who were afraid of her. Milim had once stopped a war by losing a duel and then winning four games in a row.

Umaru went pale under her hood.

It was Agott who saved her, which nobody ever let her forget.

"You can't," said Agott. "You already hold an office. You're the Head of Fun. That's a household office, sworn in at the council, in chalk. You can't hold two." She folded her arms. "That's the rule."

"Is it?" said Milim.

"It is now," said Agott.

Milim considered this, frowning enormously. The whole fort waited.

"Then I want a fort office too," she said at last. "A different one. A real one."

Shizuka was already writing. She held up the page. On it, in her neat hand, was a new line for the constitution that did not exist yet: *Office of the Guardian of the Fort.*

Milim read it. Her face did something wonderful.

"Guardian," she said.

"You guard the fort," said Umaru, recovering. "You stand under the Beam. You say who gets in. You're the strongest person who's ever been inside it, and everybody knows it, and that's why you guard it and don't run it. Strongest isn't the same as in charge." She paused. "That's the whole point of the fort."

Milim looked at her for a long moment. Then, very seriously, she nodded.

"Strongest isn't the same as in charge," she repeated. "I know that one. I've known it for a really long time." She stood up straight under the Beam. "Guardian accepts."

She took it more seriously than anyone had ever taken anything. For the rest of the campaign she stood under the Milim Beam with her arms folded, or sat at the doorway with her chin on her fists, and decided who came in. Everyone came in. She let all of them in. But she made each one stop and say who they were first, even people she had known for two years, even Umaru, very solemnly. Anyone she liked, which was everyone, she patted on the head on the way past. It was not a rule. It became one anyway.

Ryo sold her vote to both sides.

She did it openly, for snacks, the same snack to each side, and saw nothing wrong with this. When Agott pointed out that she could not vote for both candidates, Ryo said she had not promised to vote *for* anyone, only to *consider* it, and the snacks were for the considering. Nijika put her face in her hands.

Kessoku played a campaign song for each side, so as not to be partisan. The song was the same song both times. Only the name in the chorus was different.

Hakari campaigned for Umaru with tremendous energy, and then for Agott with tremendous energy when Karane asked her to. She was eventually found to be campaigning for both. Like Ryo, she saw nothing wrong with this; unlike Ryo, she meant it both times.

Anko declared herself the election's independent auditor. Nobody had asked her. She took the job so seriously that by the second day she had a sealed ballot box, a list of eligible voters, and a procedure for counting. It was written on three pages and pinned to the RECORDS crate. Shizuka copied it out neatly. It became the first official document of the fort.

Senku, passing through the common room with a coil of rope, stopped in front of Umaru's posters.

He looked at them for a long time. Then he said, mildly, to nobody in particular:

"Don't make it a crown."

Umaru, who had been waiting two days for somebody to say exactly that, turned around.

"It's not a crown," she said. "It's a presidency."

"Same thing, with extra steps."

"No," said Umaru. "Presidents get voted out. Kings don't. That's the whole point." She pointed at Agott's posters, at all the small, stern rules. "She's not my enemy. She's the reason it isn't a crown."

Senku looked at her for a moment. Then he looked at Agott's posters. Then he looked back at Umaru.

"Ten billion percent approved," he said, and went on his way with his rope.

Agott, who had heard all of it from inside the fort, went red to the ears. For the rest of the afternoon she was noticeably less stern than her posters.



Mau, in bed in the room with the east window, followed the entire campaign through Rem.

She came in every few hours with tea and a report, standing at the foot of the bed with her hands folded, like a correspondent from a distant war.

"Agott is ahead in the Witch Hat precinct," she said on the first evening. "Umaru has the Kessoku stage, except Ryo. Ryo has been bought by both sides. Hakari has also been bought by both sides, for free."

"Who's winning?"

"It's too close to call," said Rem.

Frieren, in the reading chair, did not look up from her book.

"Nobody is winning," she said. "They're having the time of their lives."

On the second evening, Rem brought a ballot.

It was a small slip of paper, folded once. On the outside, in Anko's handwriting, it said ABSENTEE.

"Anko says you can't vote," said Rem. "You're not a citizen. But she says you may submit an opinion for the record. It will be read aloud. It will not be counted."

Mau looked at the slip for a while. Then he took the pencil from the bedside table, unfolded the paper, wrote something, folded it again, and gave it back.

Rem did not read it. She put it in her apron pocket and took it away.



The election was held on the third evening, by the light of Kusuri's jars, with Milim, Guardian of the Fort, standing under the Beam with her arms folded, and Rimuru, honorary citizen, sitting at the cold hearth as official observer, with Diablo standing behind him guarding democracy.

Twenty-four citizens voted. Fern voted, after asking Anko whether a dual citizen was permitted to, and being told that the procedure on page two did not prohibit it. Maki voted. Afterward she told Mai that she had voted for Agott, because somebody had to keep the President honest, and told Umaru that she had voted for her, and Mai believed neither. Anko collected the ballots in the sealed box, one at a time, and checked each name against her list. Nano counted them aloud, flat and precise, while Shizuka wrote down the tally.

It was Umaru, then Agott, then Umaru, then Umaru, then Agott, then Agott, all the way down. When there were two ballots left, it stood at eleven each.

The fort had gone completely silent.

Nano unfolded the twenty-third ballot.

"Umaru," she said.

Twelve to eleven.

Nano unfolded the twenty-fourth.

She looked at it for a long moment.

"Both names," she said. "With a drawing of a snack. Spoiled."

Every head in the fort turned toward Ryo.

Ryo, lying on the stage, opened one eye.

"I considered it," she said, and closed it again.

Twelve to eleven, with one spoiled ballot. Anko checked the tally twice, then a third time, then sealed it, and declared the result. In accordance with the procedure on page two, she announced:

"Umaru Doma is President of the Fort."

The fort erupted.

Milim, under the Beam, cheered so loudly that a slat came loose. Afterward she told everyone who would listen that she had voted for the one who bites her lip before the sneaky thing, and would not say which one that was, though everybody knew. Hakari cheered for both candidates. Kessoku played the campaign song, with Umaru's name in it. Coco hugged Umaru so hard her hood came off. And Agott, who had lost by one vote and a drawing of a snack, stood up, crossed the fort, and held out her hand.

Umaru looked at it.

Then she shook it.

"Leader of the Opposition," said Umaru.

"Madam President," said Agott.

Wakana, who had been watching from the long table, went to his workshop. He came back ten minutes later with two sashes made from the last of the refuge-curtain offcuts. Coco lettered them on the spot.

The first said: PRESIDENT (TEMPORARY).

The second said: OPPOSITION.

Agott put hers on immediately. She wore it for a week.



"There's one more thing," said Anko. "The record."

She took the absentee slip out of the RECORDS crate and unfolded it.

"From the Idiot Who Accepted, in bed," she read. "For the record, not counted." She paused, and her mouth twitched. "Quote: *Whoever wins, no capes.* End quote."

The fort laughed.

Then Anko looked at the slip again, and her face changed.

"There's more," she said. "Under it." She read it out more quietly. "*P.S. Thank you for the window.*"

Nobody said anything.

Ori, who was sitting by the window in the south wall, with her chin on her knees, looked down at the floor. Her ears had gone pink. The pencil with which she had cut the window's outline was still behind her ear.



The constitution was written that night, by Shizuka, at Umaru's dictation and Agott's amendment. It had six articles. It was pinned to the inside of the toll booth, where everyone would see it on the way in.

*ARTICLE 1. Anyone who comes in may stay.*

*ARTICLE 2. The toll is snacks. Except for Rem, and for whoever comes next.*

*ARTICLE 3. No capes.* (Amendment, by the Opposition: *This includes the President.*)

*ARTICLE 4. The window in the south wall stays open.*

*ARTICLE 5. The President can be voted out at every full moon, by anyone, for any reason.* (Amendment, by the President: *Except being too good at games.*) (Amendment struck, by the Opposition.)

*ARTICLE 6. Nobody in the fort is ever alone.*

Aira arrived at the very end of the evening, after the constitution was already pinned up, out of breath. She had been at the bridge helping a family get a stuck cart over the last plank, and had missed the entire election.

She stopped in the doorway in front of the Guardian, who made her say who she was.

"Aira," said Aira. "The one who saves everyone."

"Which one?" said Milim.

"There's only one."

Milim considered this carefully, and let her in, and patted her on the head. Aira permitted it with enormous dignity.

She looked around the fort. At the Milim Beam. At the stage, and the annex with its curtain, and the President and the Opposition in their sashes.

"You have a Guardian," she said. "Who decides who comes in."

"Yes," said Umaru.

"Then who protects everyone once they're *inside*?"

Nobody had an answer.

"Me," said Aira. "Obviously. I was chosen to save everyone. Everyone includes a fort."

By presidential decree, her first, Umaru created the Office of the Protector on the spot. Agott objected, as was her duty, and was overruled, and asked that her objection be recorded. Anko recorded it. Shizuka added a line to the constitution, very small, under Article 6: *The Protector protects everyone inside. (Office: Aira. She insisted.)*

Aira sat down in the exact middle of the fort, beside the Guardian, with her chin up. For the rest of the night, every time anyone so much as stubbed a toe, she was there first.

Rem brought Mau a copy the next morning. Shizuka had made it in her neat hand, specially, because he had asked.

He read it twice. When he got to Article 6, he stopped and read it a third time.

He did not say anything.

Frieren leaned over from the chair and read it over his shoulder, and did not say anything either. She put her hand on his arm instead, very lightly, and left it there. Down the corridor, faintly through two walls, the President of the Fort was losing a card game to the Leader of the Opposition, and enjoying it more than she would admit.
