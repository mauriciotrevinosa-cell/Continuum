On the fourteenth morning, Maomao added a name to the door.

The list had been pinned there since the evening Yuta carried Mau back from the bay. Four names in Maomao's small, decided hand, the only people allowed into the room with the east window until she said otherwise:

MAOMAO
RIMURU
FRIEREN
REM

Nobody had argued with it. Nobody argued with Maomao about a patient.

But every morning for two weeks, somebody had stood outside that door.

Mau had not known. He had been asleep most of the first days, and after that the door had been shut, and the somebody had been very quiet. Frieren had known. She had heard the footsteps every morning, a little after the first bell, coming down the family corridor and stopping outside, and standing there. Not knocking. Just standing, for about as long as it takes to listen to someone breathe through a door. Then they went away again.

Rem had known, because twice she had opened the door with the tea tray and found him there, and he had stepped back, and said *sorry*, and gone.

Maomao had known, because on the third morning he had asked her.

*Can I go in?*

*No.*

*I won't do anything.*

*You'll do something,* Maomao had said, looking at his grey face and his hands, which still shook a little when he forgot to hold them still. *You'll put your hands on him the moment he winces, and you'll pour, and you have nothing left to pour. You're running on Rika's reserve and spite. I'm not having you fall over in there. Go and sleep.*

He had gone and slept. He had come back the next morning. And the next.



On the fourteenth morning, Maomao came out of the room with her kit, found him standing in the corridor as usual, and looked at him for a long time.

His hands were steady. His face had color in it again. Maki had been feeding him, by the look of it, aggressively.

Maomao sighed, took a pencil out of her sleeve, and turned to the list on the door.

Under REM, she wrote:

YUTA

"Conditions," she said, without turning around.

"Anything."

"None of what you were doing." She pointed the pencil at his hands. "Not one drop. You don't touch him with it. You don't *think* about touching him with it. If he winces, you call me. If he bleeds, you call me. If he so much as sneezes in a way you don't like, you call me." She turned around. "I've had one of you on my table. I'm not having two at once. I don't need two patients at the same time. Understood?"

"Understood."

"Good." She looked at him a moment longer, and something in her face eased very slightly. "He's been asking where you were."

Yuta did not say anything.

Maomao stepped aside.



Mau was sitting up against three pillows when the door opened. He had a book open on his knees that Frieren had left him and that he had not been reading. He looked up, expecting Rem with the tea.

It was Yuta.

They looked at each other.

"Hey," said Yuta.

"Hey."

Yuta came in. He did it carefully, the way you come into a room where something fragile is sleeping, even though Mau was awake. He looked at the bed. At the dressing across the middle of Mau, under his shirt. At the window with the stone on its sill. Then he pulled the reading chair a little closer to the bed, sat down in it, and put his hands flat on his own knees, where he could see them.

"Maomao's rules," he said. "No hands."

"I heard," said Mau. "Through the door. *I don't need two patients at the same time.*"

Yuta almost smiled.

"She's right," he said.



For a while they did not say anything. It was not uncomfortable. It was the silence of two people who had sat on a roof together once a week for months, and had got good at not talking.

"Where were you?" said Mau finally. "I asked. Every day. Rimuru said you were sleeping."

"I was," said Yuta. "For three days. Maki says I didn't move." He looked at his hands. "And then I was outside the door."

Mau looked at him.

"Every morning," said Yuta. "Maomao wouldn't let me in. I'd have done something stupid. I'd have tried to fix it." He paused. "So I stood outside and listened to you breathe. It was enough. Mostly."

Mau did not say anything for a moment.

"Frieren said someone came every morning," he said. "She didn't say who."

"I asked her not to." Yuta shrugged a little. "You'd have worried."

"I'd have—" Mau stopped. *Worried* was exactly what he would have done. He would have lain in bed with a hole closing under a dressing, and worried about Yuta standing in the corridor. "Okay. Yes. I would have."

"See."



"Can I tell you something?" said Yuta.

"Okay."

Yuta looked out of the east window.

"When you were in the bay," he said. "At the end of the second day. I was so tired I couldn't see properly anymore. Rika was holding me up from the inside and Maki was holding me up from the outside, and I was pouring and pouring into you, and I thought, *this is it, there's nothing left, it isn't going to be enough*." He was quiet for a moment. "And then something pushed back."

Mau said nothing.

"From your side," said Yuta. "Inside you. Something took what I was giving it and *did something with it*. Like a second pair of hands. It was the strangest thing I've ever felt." He turned and looked at Mau. "And I'd felt it once before."

"After Sukuna," said Mau slowly. "Rimuru said."

"After Sukuna. The first time. You don't remember it. You were gone." Yuta's voice went very quiet. "I held you for three days that time. Under Rimuru's coat, and then in the old inn, and the first two days nothing happened. Nothing. I poured, and it went into you like water into sand. And then on the third night, something pushed back. Just like this time. And you lived."

He looked at his own hands on his knees.

"It's in you," he said. "Whatever it is. It learned the shape of what I do. That first time. It doesn't know how to do it alone. But when someone else is doing it, it remembers, and it helps." He paused. "That's why you're alive. Twice. Not me. Not only me. *It*. Your body. It pushed back."

Mau looked at him for a long time.

"Thank you," he said. "For—"

"Not yet," said Yuta.

"What?"

"Don't thank me lying down." Yuta leaned back in the chair. "When you can walk to the training yard, come and find me, and thank me there. Sitting up. Like a person. Then I'll let you." He paused. "And you can bring snacks."

Mau laughed. It pulled, under the dressing. He stopped.

Yuta's eyes went to the dressing at once.



"Left side," said Yuta quietly. "Under the ribs. And your back, low down. On the right."

Mau went still.

"How—"

"I rebuilt it," said Yuta. "Twice. I know where everything goes. It's like a map, after. I can feel where it isn't sitting right." He did not move his hands from his knees. "It's been hurting since you woke up. Hasn't it. Both places. And you haven't told Maomao."

"It's fine."

"It's not fine. It's the left side and the low right, and every time you laugh you stop halfway."

Mau looked at the ceiling.

"Don't tell her," he said.

Yuta looked at him for a long moment.

"I'm not allowed to fix it," he said. "Maomao's rule. Not one drop." He stood up. "So I'm going to tell her."

"Yuta—"

"Roof rule," said Yuta. "My rule. If one of us starts carrying something he shouldn't, the others say so." He went to the door. "You're carrying it. So I'm saying so."

He opened the door, put his head out into the corridor, and said, at a perfectly ordinary volume:

"Maomao. Left side, under the ribs. And the low right. Since he woke up. He didn't tell you."

There was a short, terrible silence from the direction of the medical room.

Then footsteps. Fast ones.

Yuta came back in, sat down in the reading chair again, put his hands flat on his knees, and looked at Mau with an expression of perfect innocence.

"Traitor," said Mau.

"Brother," said Yuta. "Same thing, sometimes."

Maomao arrived in the doorway with her kit and a face like a winter storm. For the next half hour she was very thorough and very unpleasant, and changed two things she had been doing and added a third. When she left, the left side under the ribs hurt a little less than it had in two weeks.

Yuta stayed all afternoon. He did not use his hands once. He read the book Frieren had left on the bed, aloud, badly, doing all the voices. Mau laughed four times and stopped halfway each time, and Yuta did not say anything about it, because Maomao already knew.

When Frieren came back in the evening and found the two of them there, Yuta asleep in the reading chair with the book open on his chest, and Mau awake watching him, she stood in the doorway for a moment.

"He's on the list now," said Mau quietly. "Fifth name."

"I know," said Frieren. "I asked Maomao to put him on it."

Mau looked at her.

"Three days ago," said Frieren. "She said not until his hands stopped shaking." She came in and put a blanket over Yuta, very carefully, without waking him. "They've stopped."
