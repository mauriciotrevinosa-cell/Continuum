# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 18
## Chapter 14 — Fifteen Minutes

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 14  
**Continuity state:** Day 30 | Mau sends Fern and Frieren out for the day | Mau and Stark make hamburg steak at the old inn, Momo supervises the pudding: 'Sorry for scaring you' | Fern and Stark become official | next morning: 'it was the chocolate'

Mau found Fern that evening in the common room, mending a sleeve.

"About tomorrow," he said.

Fern did not look up. "The thing with the onions."

"About the thing with the onions." He sat down across from her, slowly, the way he sat down now. "Don't make it."

Fern looked up.

"Take her out instead," said Mau. "Frieren. All day. Anywhere. Just the two of you." He glanced toward the corridor, where Frieren had gone to fetch a book and would be back in about a minute. "She's been holding her breath for a month. She walks next to me like I'm going to fall over. She doesn't sleep until I do. She needs to breathe. And I think she needs to breathe with you."

Fern was quiet for a long moment.

"And you?"

"I'll be fine. Maomao's slip says *slowly*. I'll be very slow." He paused. "And I'll make dinner."

"You," said Fern.

"Me. With help."

Fern looked at him for a long time, the way she looked at seasoning she did not trust. Then her face did something it hardly ever did in front of anyone. It came apart into a smile, a real one, bright and young and completely unguarded, for perhaps a second and a half.

Then it went away again. She pointed the needle at him.

"If you tell anyone I smiled," said Fern, "I'll kill you."

"I didn't see anything."

"Good," said Fern, and went back to the sleeve. Her ears had gone pink.



They left in the morning, after breakfast.

Frieren did not want to go. She said so three times: once at breakfast, once at the door, and once at the edge of the yard, looking back over her shoulder at the Muslin as if it might fall down without her. Fern simply took her by the wrist, the way she used to take her by the wrist when Frieren lay in bed until noon in a hundred different inns, and walked.

By the time they reached the river, Frieren had stopped looking back.

They did nothing all day. That was the plan, and it was Fern's, and it was a good one. They walked along the river path past the stepping stones. They sat on the bank with their feet in the water, which Fern said was childish and then did first. They bought skewers from a stall at the bridge and ate them on the spot, and Frieren bought Fern a second one without being asked, because she knew. At midday Frieren lay down in the grass with her hands behind her head, and fell asleep in the sun for an hour. Fern sat beside her and did not wake her, the way Frieren had once sat beside a small girl who could not stop crying and had simply stayed.

When Frieren woke up, she lay there for a while looking at the sky.

"I've been changing," she said.

Fern did not say anything. She had learned a long time ago that when Frieren started a sentence like that, you let her finish it, even if it took a year.

"I didn't notice at first," said Frieren. "Then I did. I'm different with him. In the room, with the door shut. I'm—" She made a vague, frustrated gesture at the sky. "I'm silly. I make him say things twice. I lie on him like a cat. I kick my feet." She paused. "I think I like it. I think this might be what I'm like. I just didn't know, before."

"I know," said Fern.

Frieren turned her head.

"I've noticed," said Fern. She was looking at the river, not at Frieren, and her voice was very level, the way it went when something mattered too much to let it shake. "For a long time. You laugh now. Out loud. You let people see you not knowing things." She stopped. "I couldn't be happier, Frieren. I mean that. I couldn't."

Frieren looked at her for a long moment.

Then she sat up and brushed the grass off her sleeves, and her expression changed completely.

"So," she said. "When are you going to make it formal with Stark?"

Fern went rigid.

"No."

"No, you won't, or no, you don't want to talk about it?"

"*No*."

"Your first date with him," said Frieren, "was because I demanded it."

"That's not—" Fern began, and stopped, because it was true, and they both knew it was true, and Frieren was looking at her with the infinitely patient face of a person who had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

"I see you with him," said Frieren, more quietly. "When he's being stupid and you're telling him where the table goes. You look the way I've been acting, these past months." She paused. "I'm telling you because I know now. It's something valuable. It's one of the most valuable things there is. You shouldn't leave it lying around in the hallway because you're too proud to pick it up."

Fern looked at the river for a very long time.

"Fine," she said at last. Her ears were pink again. "Fine. I'll ask him. To make it— to make it formal." She turned and glared at Frieren. "But you're helping. You and Mau. Both of you."

Frieren's face changed again.

Fern had known Frieren for most of her life, and she knew this expression very well. She had seen it before dungeons, and before mimics, and before every terrible idea Frieren had ever had and called a plan. It was the face of someone who had just seen how all the pieces could fit together, and who intended to make Mau one of the pieces without telling him.

"Of course," said Frieren. "We'll help."



Meanwhile, at the old inn, dinner was going badly.

Mau had chosen the old inn on purpose. The Muslin's kitchen was too busy to keep a secret in, and Rem had said yes before he had finished asking. It was also where the household had first eaten together, a long time ago, before there was a house or a settlement or anything to protect. It seemed like the right kitchen.

He had chosen hamburg steak on purpose too. He had asked Stark how to make it properly, because the one time the house had tried, back in the first weeks after Stark arrived, it had come out *approximately*. Stark had eaten it anyway, with his eyes watering, and had said that was not why.

"In my village," Stark had said, when Mau asked, "you make it for someone who worked hard. Who did their best." He had looked at Mau for a moment. "You're making it for her."

"Yes."

Stark had not said anything for a while. Then he had rolled up his sleeves.

That had been four hours ago.

Since then there had been three attempts. The first had fallen apart in the pan. The second had held together, burned on one side and raw on the other. The third had been very nearly right, until Mau, sitting on the stool Maomao insisted on, reached for the salt at the same moment Stark reached for the pepper, and they had both decided to keep going rather than discuss it.

Momo stood in the doorway with her arms folded.

"Let me help," she said, for the ninth time.

"No," said Mau.

"No," said Stark.

"You don't even know what you're doing."

"We have to do it ourselves," said Mau.

"It doesn't count otherwise," said Stark.

Momo looked at the two of them, both covered in flour and both very serious, and threw her hands up.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm doing the dessert supervision. You're not allowed to touch the dessert without me. That's my price."

The dessert was a pudding. Momo said it was the only dessert in the world you could not ruin, and then Mau nearly ruined it twice. It sat cooling now on the windowsill, a little lopsided. A folded note was tucked under the edge of the plate. The note had taken Mau longer than the pudding. In the end, it said only:

*Sorry for scaring you.*

He had been allowed to write it. He was the only one besides Frieren who was.

Stark had also, somewhere around the second attempt, quietly started making a second hamburg steak. He had not said who it was for. Mau had not asked.



Frieren and Fern came back an hour before sunset. They were early.

Fern walked straight into the old inn's kitchen before anyone could stop her, and stopped.

Stark was at the stove. He had flour in his hair and on his face, and his sleeves were rolled to the elbow. He was holding a pan over the flame with the absolute concentration of a man defusing something, while a hamburg steak hissed in it. It was the fourth attempt, and it was going well. He looked up, saw Fern in the doorway, and went white with horror.

Fern went white too, for a completely different reason.

"I'm not done!" said Stark. "I'm not— it's not— come back in fifteen minutes!"

"Fifteen minutes," said Mau, from the stool, very seriously.

Fern backed out of the kitchen without a word.

Frieren was waiting in the corridor. She had seen the whole thing over Fern's shoulder. She had the plan face again, worse than before.

"Fifteen minutes," said Frieren. "Come with me."



Frieren took Fern by the wrist and ran.

She did not run often. It was not dignified. But she ran now, out of the old inn and across the plaza toward the Muslin, towing Fern behind her. Halfway across the plaza she grabbed Marin as well, who had been sitting outside the atelier sketching and who came along at once without asking a single question. Momo saw them go past the inn window, untied her apron, and followed, because whatever was happening was obviously gossip and she was not going to miss it.

They reached the room with the east window with eleven minutes left.

"Something cute," said Frieren to Marin. "For Fern. Now."

Marin's whole face lit up like a festival.

"Oh, I have *exactly* the thing," she said. "Fern. Fern, sit down. Fern, trust me."

Fern sat down. She did not trust anyone, but she trusted Marin about clothes, which was not the same thing.

What Marin had was a dress she had been finishing for weeks without knowing who it was for. It was soft and pale, with small buttons and a collar that Marin described as *kawaii but in a serious way*. It fit Fern as if Marin had measured her in her sleep. Marin did her hair in under three minutes, talking the entire time. When Fern finally looked in the mirror, she did not say anything at all, which Marin correctly took as the highest possible praise.

Then Marin turned to Frieren.

"And you?"

Frieren was already changing.

She took off the red Ferrari shirt she had been wearing all day. It was Mau's shirt, the one she had stolen in the first weeks at the old inn and never really given back. She folded it and went to the closet, and took down the hoodie, the black-and-orange one, and a pair of soft shorts, and put them on. She pulled the sleeves down over her hands.

Marin stared.

"That's it?" said Marin. "Nothing fancy?"

Frieren turned around.

Her chin was up. Her face was perfectly composed. Her ears were doing something that suggested she was not composed at all.

"When I'm like this," said Frieren, in the proudest voice anyone in that room had ever heard her use, "he tells me I'm the most beautiful."

Marin made a sound like a kettle.

Fern looked at Frieren's face and knew at once what she was seeing. It was the face of someone who had just found a door marked EGO, opened it, and walked straight through. Fern had never seen it on Frieren before, not once in her whole life, and she found that she did not mind it in the least.

Frieren's heart, which she could feel, did something small and foolish in her chest.

From the far end of the corridor, from the medical room, which was nowhere near the room with the east window but which apparently had excellent acoustics, came Maomao's voice.

"*That's going to affect his heart rhythm!*"

Everyone froze.

There was a pause.

"*I think it's fine this time*," called Maomao. "*Under medical observation. Of course. So I'll require a plate. Please.*"

Momo laughed so hard she had to sit down on the bed.



They were back in fourteen minutes.

The old inn's dining room had been set: one table by the window, with four places and a candle, which was Rem's doing. The plates were already out. The hamburg steaks were, everyone agreed afterward, far better than anyone had any right to expect. Round, brown, holding together, glazed with something Stark would not name. On Frieren's plate sat the lopsided pudding, with its note.

The failed attempts had been arranged on two great platters on the long table in the middle of the room. There were a great many failed attempts. At some point in the last hour, word had got out, and the household had come. Not all of it, and not all at once, but enough to fill the old inn's long tables the way they used to be filled, long ago, in the first weeks. They were eating the failed attempts with every sign of enjoyment, and Senku was explaining to Suika exactly why attempt number two had burned.

Frieren sat down and read the note.

She did not say anything. She folded it very small and put it in the pocket of the hoodie, and kept her hand over it.

Then Stark came out of the kitchen.

He had washed his face and changed his shirt and done something hopeless to his hair. He was carrying the last plate, his own, the one he had made second and never explained, and he came around the corner and saw Fern.

He stopped dead.

He went red. Not a little. All of him, from the collar up, like a pan left too long on the fire.

Fern did not say anything either. She had meant to. She had rehearsed it all the way across the plaza. Now she sat at the table in Marin's dress with her hands in her lap and could not remember a single word.

Under the table, Frieren nudged her foot.

Fern did not move.

Frieren nudged it again, harder.

And Fern stood up. She crossed the space between them in four steps, took Stark's face in both hands, and kissed him, right there in the middle of the old inn, in front of the long tables and the failed hamburg steaks and Senku and Suika and everybody.

Then she let go. She stood back with her chin up and her face burning and said, very clearly:

"I love you. Romantically."

Stark turned the color of a tomato.

He opened his mouth. Nothing came out. He looked at the plate in his hands as if it might help, and it did not.

Mau got up from his chair. He did it slowly, the way he did everything now, and walked over, and leaned in close to Stark's ear.

"This is the part," whispered Mau, "where you tell her you love her too. Or that's what Frieren told me."

Across the table, Frieren gave him a look that could have frozen the river.

Stark swallowed.

"I love you too," he said to Fern. His voice cracked in the middle and he did not care. "Romantically."

Nobody in the old inn said anything.

Not one person. Not Momo, not Marin, not Senku, not Suika, not the whole long table of people with their mouths full of failed hamburg steak. They had all heard. They all very carefully went on eating, and looked at their plates, and let it be what it was.

Stark sat down. Fern sat down beside him, instead of across. Mau went back to his chair beside Frieren, who kicked him under the table, then left her foot resting against his.

They ate.



What happened for the rest of the evening, nobody in the household ever described in front of either of them. But everyone saw it.

Fern and Frieren behaved like girls.

Not behind a door. Not in a room with the curtains shut, the way Frieren had for months. Out loud, in the old inn's dining room, at a table by the window, with the people they loved. Fern leaned against Stark's shoulder and stole food off his plate and laughed at something he said that was not funny. Frieren sat sideways in her chair with her feet in Mau's lap and made him say things twice and corrected him. At one point they caught each other's eye across the table and both giggled, like children who have got away with something.

They did not notice the room. That was the thing. Neither of them noticed, the whole evening, that everyone was there.



They noticed in the morning.

It happened at breakfast, in the Muslin, at about the same moment for both of them, and it was terrible to watch. Frieren put down her spoon. Across the table, Fern put down hers. They looked at each other, and then, slowly, around the room, at every single person who had been at the old inn the night before.

Every single person very carefully went on eating.

Fern stood up.

"If anyone," she said, in a voice of perfect calm, "mentions anything about yesterday evening. Anything at all. To anyone. Ever."

"I will destroy the house," said Frieren, also standing. "All of it. The new one too, when it's built."

There was a long silence.

"It was the chocolate," said Fern.

Every head turned.

"Maomao's chocolate," said Frieren, without blinking. "She gave us some. It lowers inhibitions. She told us it was for our health."

Every head turned toward Maomao.

Maomao was sitting at the end of the table with a cup of tea halfway to her mouth. She opened her mouth to say something. Then she looked at the two of them, Frieren and Fern, side by side, chins up and faces scarlet. And she understood. She closed her mouth again and swallowed whatever she had been about to say, along with the tea.

"Maomao," said Seiko, from her armchair, in a voice of deep disappointment. "Again?"

"Again," said Anko, shaking her head. "Every week it's something with that girl."

"I didn't—" Maomao began.

"Every week," said Seiko.

"You can't keep drugging the family," said Anko. "We've talked about this."

"We have *not*—"

"We have talked about this," said Seiko firmly.

Somebody laughed. It might have been Momo. Then somebody else did, and then the whole table did, all at once. Even Fern, still standing, still scarlet, put her hand over her mouth, and her shoulders started to shake. Frieren sat back down and hid her face in Mau's shoulder. Stark was laughing so hard he had to hold on to the table.

Maomao did not laugh.

She sipped her tea and looked down the length of the table at Frieren and Fern. Her look said, as clearly as any words: *You owe me. Both of you. And I will collect.* Frieren in particular was going to pay. Frieren had once quietly raised one of Maomao's doses behind her back, and Maomao had never confronted her about it, which was not the same as not knowing.

And behind her eyes, very calmly, Maomao thought:

*Next time, the chocolate will also have an aphrodisiac.*
