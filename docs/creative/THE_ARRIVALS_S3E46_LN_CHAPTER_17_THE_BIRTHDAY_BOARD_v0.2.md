## Chapter 17 — The Birthday Board

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 17  
**Continuity state:** Some days after the second drill, before the road reports stop | Fern: 'Eleven days' | Coco's Birthday Board | remembered birthdays, each dated: Milim's Dragon Knuckles, Umaru's forty-one bags (after the Legendary Snack), Coco, Fern, Ori's found day, Bocchi | Frieren's name not on the board yet

It was Fern who noticed, which surprised nobody.

She was at the long table with the ledger, on a grey morning a few days after the second drill, doing the household's month ahead the way she did it every month. She stopped with her pen in the air and looked at a date for a long time.

"Eleven days," she said.

Mau, across the table with a bowl of porridge, looked up. "Eleven days until what?"

"Frieren's birthday."

He put the spoon down.

"Frieren has a birthday?"

Fern looked at him over the ledger with an expression that suggested this was the stupidest thing anyone had said to her in some time, and she had recently been kidnapped by a fort.

"Everyone has a birthday," she said.

"I don't. Not a real one. We picked one."

"Frieren has a real one," said Fern. "She told me what it was once, when I was very small and asked. She's forgotten it since. She's had about a thousand of them. They stopped meaning anything to her somewhere around the third century." She dipped her pen. "I didn't forget."

Mau looked at her for a long time.

"Eleven days," he said.

"Eleven days."

"Does she know you know?"

"She knows I know," said Fern. "She's never once let me do anything about it. Every year she goes somewhere on that day, or reads all day with the door shut, so there's nothing to celebrate." She looked down at the ledger. "Every year, for as long as I've known her, I've written it down anyway."



That was how the Birthday Board started.

Coco made it. She made it the same afternoon, because that was what Coco did when somebody said a thing out loud that should have been written down: she found a board. It was an old plank from Wakana's offcut pile, sanded smooth, and she pinned it to the wall of the common room beside the board with the rings and the rules. Across the top, in charcoal, she wrote:

BIRTHDAYS

Under it she drew twelve columns, one for each month of the calendar Rimuru had built from local timekeeping in the first weeks at the old inn. Then she went around the whole house with a stub of charcoal, asking.

It turned out that the house had never once asked.

It had done Mau's, because Mau had not had one and they had given him one: a day picked out of the calendar because people wanted him to have it. It had done Mau's again, late, the spring the memory crisis swallowed the real day, with one song and forty people. Everyone else's birthdays had come and gone in two years of arrivals and wars and moves and storms, and mostly nobody had known. Sometimes somebody had. Then it had been something small, in a corner, between the people who knew.

Coco wanted every name on the board.

It took her a week.



Some were easy.

Momo said hers at once, and then her grandmother's, and then said Seiko would kill her for telling. Seiko, consulted, said that her birthday was none of anyone's business and that if Coco wrote it on a board she would haunt the board. Coco wrote SEIKO in the column Momo had named, and next to it, very small, *(do not celebrate)*. Seiko saw it and did not take it down.

Bocchi wrote her own, in tiny letters at the very bottom of the column, where nobody would see it. Kita moved it up to the middle. Bocchi moved it back down. Kita moved it up again. In the end Nijika nailed it in place.

Yuta and Maki wrote theirs side by side, and then Maki drew a line between them. When Coco asked what it meant, Maki said it meant *we've been through this before, don't make it a whole thing*, and Yuta said nothing and looked pleased.

Some were not easy.

Rimuru thought about it for a long time. Then he wrote the day he woke up in a cave as a slime with nothing but a voice in his head and a very large dragon. "That's my birthday now," he said. "The other one belongs to someone else. Someone who died." Nobody asked him what he meant. Some of them knew.

"That's in three days," said Coco, looking at the calendar.

Rimuru blinked. "Is it?" He had genuinely not checked in two years.

At the long table, Umaru, who had been listening, went very quiet. Then she went very busy.

Diablo wrote the day Rimuru-sama named him, and looked at it on the board for a very long time with an expression of private rapture.

Milim said she didn't remember hers. She'd had too many. "Pick a good one," she told Coco, and Coco, after serious thought, picked the day Milim had lost the war for the end bay four games to three and gotten the room anyway. Milim said that was the best birthday she'd ever had, and it wasn't even her birthday yet.

Turbo Granny claimed every day of the year. Coco wrote TURBO GRANNY across the top of all twelve columns, very small. Turbo Granny was satisfied.

Ori did not have one. She did not know what day she had been born, or whether she had been, exactly, in the way other people were. She stood in front of the board for a long time with the charcoal in her hand. Then she wrote ORI in the column for the month Mau and Frieren had found her, on the day they had found her.

"That one," she said. "That's when I started."

Nobody said anything. Frieren, passing through the common room with a book, stopped and looked at the board, at that one name in that one column, and then kept walking. She held the book very tightly.

And Frieren's name was not on the board.

Fern did not put it there. She stood in front of the board on the last evening of the week with the charcoal, with Mau beside her, and did not write it.

"Not yet," said Fern. "If I write it, she'll see it, and go somewhere that day."

"So we don't write it."

"We don't write it." Fern put down the charcoal. "We do it anyway."



Once the board was up, the house began remembering.

It turned out that the birthdays had not all gone by unmarked. Some of them had been marked in corners, by the few who knew. Once the board was there, the stories came out, one at a time, over dinners, on the step, on the roof. Coco wrote each one on a slip of paper and pinned it under the right name. Under some of them she wrote, in brackets, when it had happened, so that nobody would get confused about time. She hated it when people got confused about time.

*MILIM. (This was in the weeks after the Move, while the Muslin was still being argued about.)*

Rimuru had remembered that Milim had a pair of gloves in their old world. The Dragon Knuckles. He had given them to her a long time ago, the way you give something to a friend who hits things. She had not brought them with her. Nobody brought anything with them.

So Rimuru had made her new ones. With everyone.

Wakana cut and stitched the leather, which took him four nights and two attempts. Senku, who had refused to call it anything but "a hand-protection engineering problem", worked out the padding and reinforced the knuckles with something he would not name. Maki fitted them, because Maki knew exactly how a fist should sit inside a glove that was going to hit something very hard. Mai polished them. Kusuri dyed the leather a deep red that did not fade. Coco drew a small dragon on the back of each cuff, in charcoal, which Wakana then stitched over in thread so it would last. Rimuru put them in a box Bocchi had found, and Umaru wrapped the box in a toll-booth paper bag, which was the only wrapping available.

Milim opened it at breakfast.

She did not say anything for a long time. Then she put them on, one at a time, very slowly. Then she cried. She cried so hard and so happily that she could not eat her breakfast, so Momo ate it for her, very tenderly. For a week afterward she would not hit anything at all, in case it scuffed them. On the eighth day she went out into the yard early in the morning, alone, and punched the sky once, straight up, as hard as she could. A cloud over the settlement broke in half and drifted apart in two pieces. Senku said that was not possible. Milim said it was a test.

*UMARU. (This was at the end of summer, a few weeks after the Legendary Snack.)*

Since the night they came back from the market town with a sack of potatoes and a small battered grimoire, the house had been able to make chips. Real ones. It took four people to do it. Momo fried. Seiko supervised, which meant telling Momo she was doing it wrong. Senku measured the oil, because there was a correct temperature and he would not let anyone guess it. And Frieren held her hand over every tray as it came out of the oil and said three words very quietly, because without the spell they were only very good sad potatoes.

It was an event. It happened perhaps once a fortnight, when all four of them could be got into the kitchen at the same time.

On Umaru's birthday it happened forty-one times.

They started before dawn and did not stop until the middle of the morning. Momo fried until her arms ached. Seiko supervised until she had no complaints left, which nobody had known was possible. Senku measured the oil forty-one times and wrote down every temperature. Frieren stood by the trays with her hand out and cast the crisping spell forty-one times in a row. Senku told her it was cheating forty-one times in a row, and she said *it's a spell* forty-one times, and neither of them got tired of it. The fort, which at that time was four people and a monument, folded paper bags out of old ledger pages under strict secrecy. Coco counted.

When Umaru opened the door of her room, she found it blocked. Wall to wall, floor to ceiling, there were paper bags of chips. Forty-one of them.

Umaru stood in the doorway and looked at them.

Then she went back into her room, shut the door, opened it again, and looked at them again, as if they might have been a dream. Then she picked up one bag and held it to her chest with both arms, and it crackled, and she cried quietly into the paper. It was the other kind of crying, the kind from the night in the kitchen when the first one cracked.

She ate one bag that day. She gave the other forty away over the next two weeks, one at a time, to everyone who had made them, and to Himmel, who had walked two hours to a market town for fried dirt and never complained once. She kept the last bag on the shelf above her bed for a month, unopened, so she could look at it.

*COCO. (This was in the storm, the second day.)*

Qifrey had given her a pen. That was all. A plain pen, a good one, with a nib he had shaped himself. But he had given it to her in front of Agott and Tetia and Richeh, and said, *for the circles you will draw when you are a witch.* Not *if*. *When.* Coco had not let go of it for the rest of the storm.

*FERN. (This was at the end of summer, the week before the sea.)*

In another world, a long time ago, Stark had forgotten Fern's birthday, and it had been a whole thing. In this one he did not forget. He had been planning it for two months. He made her a bracelet out of a thin silver wire he had begged from Senku, twisted into a pattern he had practiced fifty times on string. He gave it to her at breakfast, in front of everyone, red to the ears.

Fern looked at it for a long time.

"You remembered," she said.

"I wrote it down," said Stark. "In four places."

She put it on. She had not taken it off since. Everyone in the house had seen it, and nobody had ever mentioned it, and she had never once explained it.

*ORI. (This was at the end of summer, the day after the sea.)*

The day she had picked. Mau and Frieren had taken her down to the river at dawn, the three of them, without telling anyone. They sat on the bank and did not do anything at all until the sun was up. Then Mau gave her a stone, a small smooth one from the tidepools, grey with a white band through it, that he had saved from the trip.

She kept it on her windowsill for months.

Later, during the bay, she sent it back to him, in the fort's first basket, without a note, because it was the most important thing she owned and he was the most important person who needed it.

Coco did not write that last part on the slip. She did not know it. Only three people did.

*BOCCHI. (Undated. Bocchi asked for it to be undated.)*

The house had learned, over two years, how to celebrate Bocchi's birthday. You did not sing. You did not gather. You did not say *happy birthday* in a loud voice in a room with more than three people in it. What you did was this: you left a small thing outside her door, quietly, in the morning, without a note, so she could find it alone. Then, in the evening, if she played, you listened, and did not clap until she had stopped and was looking at the floor.

By the evening of her last one there had been nineteen small things outside her door. Kessoku had played on the stage corner of the old fort. She had played too, for an hour, and nobody had clapped until she stopped. And then the whole house had clapped at once, and she had put her face in her guitar.



Under every name, the slips filled up.

Under one name, there was nothing yet, because the name was not on the board.

Mau stood in front of the board on the evening of the seventh day after Fern had said *eleven*, and looked at the empty place in the column where it would have gone.

Four days.

He did not know what to give someone who had been alive for a thousand years and had stopped counting her birthdays somewhere around the third century. Someone who collected useless spells the way other people collected stones, and had more grimoires than she could carry, and still wanted more. Someone who had never once let anyone do anything about the day.

He stood there for a long time.

"You'll think of something," said Fern, behind him.

"I haven't."

"You will," said Fern. "You've got four days." She paused. "And the witches come tomorrow. Every second week. They've been coming since the second week after the containment."

He looked at her.

"They collect things too," said Fern, very casually, and went back to her ledger.
