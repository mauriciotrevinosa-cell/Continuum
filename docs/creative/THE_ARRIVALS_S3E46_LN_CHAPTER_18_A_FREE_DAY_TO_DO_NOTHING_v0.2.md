# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 18
## Chapter 18 — A Free Day to Do Nothing

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 18  
**Continuity state:** Same week as 'The Birthday Board' | Rimuru's birthday: Umaru's universal card with the Ministry's seal | he is bad at doing nothing | movies: Mau, Frieren, Yuta, Maki, Milim, Ori | the house complains; Marin decrees a movie day | Ciel: 'You have never once rested correctly'

On the morning of Rimuru's birthday, there was a card on the board.

It was pinned dead center, over the four rings, where nobody could miss it. It was a small card of stiff paper cut very neatly, the same kind the fort used for its passes, with Umaru's round handwriting on it:

*ONE FREE DAY TO DO NOTHING.*
*For: Rimuru.*
*Valid: any one day he chooses.*
*This card is UNIVERSAL. Everyone must obey it.*

Under that, in red ink, was a seal. It had been drawn by hand with tremendous care, and on close inspection it was a circle containing a cushion, a teacup and a sleeping cat. Under the seal:

*APPROVED BY THE MINISTRY OF NOT WORKING.*
*— Marin*

Rimuru stood in front of the board in his human shape with a bowl of porridge in his hand and read it three times.

"Universal," he said.

"Universal," said Umaru, from the fort's doorway, in her PRESIDENT (TEMPORARY) sash, very seriously. "It's a presidential decree with ministerial approval. It overrides everything. Fern checked."

Rimuru looked at Fern.

"It overrides the ledger," said Fern, without looking up. "For one day. I checked."

Rimuru looked at the card again.

He had been a lot of things, in two worlds. A slime in a cave. A name-giver to goblins. A lord, a Demon Lord, a founder of a city, the keeper of a dragon in his stomach. Here he was one of two people who answered the letters, the one Ciel called Master, and the honorary citizen of a fort with a bowl by the hearth. He had been given titles that took a breath in the middle to say, and swords, and treaties, and a sash that said ALSO AN IDIOT WHO ACCEPTED.

Nobody, in either world, had ever given him a day to do nothing.

"Happy birthday," said Umaru, and went back into the fort before he could say anything.



He used it the next day.

He went up the ladder into the loft in the room with the east window right after breakfast, let his human shape go, settled into his small pillow as a slime, and did nothing.

The card went with him. He pinned it to the beam above the loft, where anyone climbing the ladder would see it first.

Diablo stationed himself outside the door of the room with the east window with an expression of total devotion, and turned away, with great courtesy, every single person who came near it all morning. Anko, with a question about the evidence room: turned away. Senku, with a valve: turned away. Jinshi, with a letter from the second community: turned away so politely that Jinshi did not realize until he was halfway down the corridor that he had been refused.

"Rimuru-sama," Diablo said to each of them, with a small bow, "is doing nothing. By universal decree. Please return tomorrow."

Rimuru, in the loft, could hear all of it. He lay on his pillow and listened to the house go on without him, and did not have to do anything about any of it. It was the strangest feeling.

He did nothing until about the middle of the morning.

Then he found out something about himself that he had not known in two lifetimes. He was very, very bad at doing nothing.

He did not want to work. He did not want to answer letters, or argue about valves, or read Anko's evidence. He was not bored, exactly. He was something worse. He was a person who had been running for two lifetimes and had been told to stop, and now did not know what to do with his hands. He did not have hands at the moment. That did not help.

He lay on the pillow and looked at the beam above him, and the card on it, and felt slightly ridiculous.



A little after noon, the door opened.

Diablo did not stop it, because it was Mau and Frieren, and it was their room.

They had been down at the river. Maomao allowed Mau as far as the stepping stones now, with someone beside him, and he went most days when the weather held, because it was the farthest he was allowed and he liked to stand at the very edge of it. Frieren had her book under her arm. Mau had river mud on his boots, and Frieren's hand in his. They came in talking about nothing in low voices, and stopped, and looked up at the loft.

A small blue shape was looking down at them over the edge of the boards.

"You're doing nothing," said Mau.

"I'm doing nothing," said Rimuru.

"How's it going?"

Rimuru thought about it.

"Badly," he admitted.

Frieren made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

Mau looked up at him for a moment. Then he looked at Frieren. Then, very slowly, as if a terrible and wonderful idea had just occurred to him, he said:

"Doing nothing doesn't mean you can't watch something."

Rimuru looked at him.

"Movies," said Mau.



It was not meant to be a gathering.

That was what Rimuru said afterward, to everyone, many times. It was meant to be the three of them, in the room with the east window, watching the old movie machine on the wall opposite the bed. Mau knew how to make it work in the same way he knew a great many things from before, without knowing how he knew. Three people. A quiet afternoon. Nothing.

Then Rimuru thought of Yuta.

Yuta would have been doing work, because Yuta was always doing work. And Yuta was supposed to be on the roof once a week with them, and they had missed it for so long, and he would like this. So Rimuru sent a message through the speaking tube in the corridor, and Yuta came, and Maki came with him, because Maki went where Yuta went.

Then he thought of Milim.

Milim would never forgive him if she found out he had watched movies on his birthday without her. Milim had been trying to give him things all day, and Diablo had been turning her away from the door with increasing difficulty. So he sent a message to Milim, who arrived approximately one second later through the door. Diablo was still in the middle of bowing to her.

Then, on the way back from the corridor, he passed Ori's door. It was open. Ori was inside, reading, alone, with her knees up. He stopped.

"Movies," he said. "In there. Doing nothing. Want to?"

Ori looked up at him. Then she put down her book.

That was seven people in a room built for two and a slime. Mau and Frieren were on the bed. Ori was between them, because that was where Ori went now. Yuta and Maki were on the floor with their backs against the bed. Milim was in the reading chair, which she had picked up and turned to face the wall and then sat in sideways, with her legs over the arm. Rimuru was in his loft, in his little black agent coat, which he had put on for the occasion, because a birthday was an occasion.

They watched three movies.

Nobody did anything. Nobody answered a letter or fixed a valve. Milim fell asleep halfway through the second one and snored. Ori laughed at something in the third that nobody else thought was funny, and then everyone thought it was funny because she had laughed. Yuta and Maki held hands on the floor the whole time and thought nobody had noticed. Frieren read her book during the boring parts and looked up for the good parts, and somehow always knew which was which.

It was, Rimuru thought, lying in his loft in his coat with the light from the movie machine flickering on the beam above him, the best day of nothing he had ever had. Possibly the only one.



By evening, the house had found out.

It was Momo who found out first. She came down the family corridor with a question about dinner, and heard the movie through the door, and saw Diablo outside it with an expression of guilt so profound that he could not meet her eyes.

"Is that," said Momo slowly, "a *movie*?"

Diablo said nothing.

"Is the whole of the second ring watching a *movie* in there? On the free day? Without *us*?"

Diablo bowed very low and said nothing at all.

Momo went back down the corridor and told the kitchen. The kitchen told the fort. The fort told everyone. Within a quarter of an hour, half the house was standing in the family corridor outside the room with the east window, complaining.

"A free day to do *nothing*," said Agott, as the Opposition. "Nothing. Not a private screening for a select few."

"I like movies," said Hakari, wounded.

"I'm *in* movies," said Marin, which nobody understood.

"I was told," said Senku, "that he was doing nothing. I was turned away. With my valve."

Rimuru put his head out of the door, in slime form, in his agent coat.

The corridor looked at him.

"It was supposed to be three people," said Rimuru.

"It's seven," said Agott.

"It got away from me," said Rimuru.



The matter went, as all matters of not working did, to the Ministry.

Marin heard the complaint that evening, in the common room, sitting very upright at the long table with her seal and a fresh sheet of paper, while half the house stood in front of her with grievances. She listened to all of them. Then she looked at Rimuru, who was sitting in his bowl by the cold hearth in his coat, trying to look like a slime who had not done anything wrong.

"The Ministry finds," said Marin, "that the free day was used correctly. Nothing was done. Nobody worked. The decree was obeyed."

The corridor deflated.

"*However*," said Marin, "the Ministry also finds that movies were watched, and that a movie is a Ministry matter, and that this house has not had a movie day for everyone since the old inn." She drew her seal at the bottom of the sheet with great care: the circle, the cushion, the teacup, the cat. "Therefore the Ministry decrees: tomorrow is not for work either. Tomorrow is for movies. All of them. Everyone."

The common room erupted.

Marin pinned the decree to the board beside Umaru's card, and sat back down, glowing.

Rimuru, in his bowl, looked at the two pieces of paper side by side on the board. ONE FREE DAY TO DO NOTHING. TOMORROW IS FOR MOVIES.

*Master*, said Ciel, privately.

*Don't.*

*I would only like to note,* said Ciel, *that you were given one day to do nothing, and you have turned it into two days, a movie festival and a ministerial decree. In two lifetimes I have never once seen you rest correctly.*

*It was supposed to be three people.*

*Yes,* said Ciel. *It always is, with you.* A pause. *Happy birthday, Master.*

Rimuru did not answer. He settled lower in his bowl, in his small black coat, by the cold hearth, and closed his eyes. Around him, the house argued happily about which movies to watch first, and nobody asked him anything at all.
