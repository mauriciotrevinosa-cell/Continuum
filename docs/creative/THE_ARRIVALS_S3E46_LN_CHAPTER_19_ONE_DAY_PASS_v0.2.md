# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 18
## Chapter 19 — One Day Pass

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 19  
**Continuity state:** Three days before Frieren's birthday | the witches on the bay night (two days in the yard) and under the window during recovery | Diablo carries the invitation | 'No questions today' | Umaru's ONE DAY PASS (Ring Three) | the one who keeps books, the morning spell: 'Come yourself'

The witches had not missed a day.

Not one. Not since the second week after the containment, when Mau had asked them himself, in the old inn, for an hour every second week. They were to come at the agreed bell and ask him their quiet, precise questions about his seams, and whether he was real, and what he remembered of a cabin that had never existed. They had come every time. Through the Move, and the memory crisis, and the storm, and the council. They came at the bell and sat in the room with the door that stuck and the wobbly chair Umaru had broken, and asked, and went back to their forest.

On the night of the bay, they had come without being asked.

Nobody in the house had sent for them. Nobody had thought to. But some time after midnight, while Yuta held the edges and Maomao worked inside them and the fort held its ground, two figures had appeared at the far edge of the Muslin's yard, in the dark, under the agreement. They did not come any closer. They did not ask anything. They stood there.

They had felt it, the first witch told Rem afterward. His seams. They always felt them, faintly, at a distance, the way you feel a lamp in another room. That night they had flared, all at once, brighter than they had ever been. Then they had begun to go dark.

The last time that had happened, it had been the containment, and they had come then too.

They stood at the edge of the yard for two days. Rem took them tea. They drank it. When Rimuru came out of the bay at the end of the second day and said *it stopped*, they turned and went back to their forest without a word, as if they had only been waiting for that.



Then he was in bed for two weeks, and could not walk to the old inn. It was hours by road. He could barely walk to the end of the corridor.

So the witches came to him.

Maomao would not let them into the room. Ring Three, the list on the door, five names and no more. But she let them stand outside, in the garden, under the east window. And at the agreed bell, every second week, Frieren opened the window. The two of them stood below it in the cold, and asked their questions up through it, and he answered from the bed. Their hour, kept, through a window.

*How are your seams.*

*Quiet. Ciel says quiet.*

*Do you remember the cabin.*

*No. Not since the containment. It's gone.*

*Are you real.*

*I think so. The leaves land on me now.*

*Good*, the first witch had said, the second time. Then, after a pause, as if it were an afterthought: *We felt you go dark. We would rather you did not do that again.* And they had turned and gone.



On the morning of the third day before Frieren's birthday, the witches' day came round again.

Mau was well enough now to walk to the old inn. Maomao had said so, with conditions. He had been planning to go. He had been planning, in fact, to ask the first witch something that had nothing to do with seams, and he had been working out all week how to ask it without Frieren hearing.

But Rimuru had other ideas.

"They came for two days," said Rimuru, at breakfast. "On the night of the bay. Nobody asked them. And then every second week under the window, in the cold." He put down his spoon. "They've been coming to *you* for a year. To the inn, to the window. Every time." He looked at Mau. "Maybe today, they come here. Not for questions."

Mau looked at him.

"Diablo," said Rimuru.

Diablo, who had been standing behind his chair, bowed.

"Go to the old inn," said Rimuru. "Wait for them at the bell. Tell them Rimuru asked you to come. Tell them Mau would like to speak with them. Here. At the Muslin." He paused. "And tell them they're invited in."



They came at midmorning.

Diablo brought them. He walked them across the plaza and the yard to the front door of the Muslin with tremendous ceremony, one step behind and to the side, the way he walked behind Rimuru. The two witches came through the door into the common room and stopped.

The whole house was there. It always was, by midmorning, but today it was there on purpose. The long table had been cleared. Momo had made lunch, a great deal of lunch. The fort had its curtain tied back.

The first witch looked around the room, slowly, taking in all of it. Then she looked at Mau, standing at the end of the long table.

"You asked to speak with us," she said.

"Yes."

"Then ask," said the first witch. She folded her hands, ready, the way she folded them at the start of every session. "Your seams first. Then the cabin. Then—"

"No questions today," said Mau.

The first witch stopped.

Something went across her face. It was very small, and it went very fast, and if Mau had not been watching her every second week for a year he would not have seen it. Then it was gone, and she nodded once, politely, and turned to the other witch.

"Then we will go," she said.

The other witch was already turning toward the door. They did it neatly, without complaint, the way people do who have been dismissed before and know how to leave a room.

"No," said Mau. "No. That's not— wait."

They stopped.

"No questions," said Mau, "because we're doing something else." He took a breath. "You came on the night of the bay. Nobody asked you. You stood in the yard for two days. Then you came under my window every second week, in the cold, for six weeks. You kept your hour at the inn even when I wasn't there." He looked at the first witch. "You've been coming to me for a year. Every time. Today, you don't have to ask me anything. Today you're guests. Just guests. In the house." He gestured at the long table and the lunch and the room full of people. "That's all. You stay. You eat. Nobody asks anybody anything."

The first witch looked at him for a long time.

"Guests," she said.

"Guests."

The other witch, the one who never spoke, was looking past him at the north end of the common room. At the fort.

"What," she said, very quietly, the first word Mau had ever heard her say, "is *that*?"



Umaru came out of the fort before anyone could explain.

She came in her hood and her sash, with the Guardian a step behind her, and stopped in front of the two witches. She looked up at them, the way she looked at new players before a game, sizing them up. Then she reached into the pocket of her hood and took out two small cards.

They were the same stiff paper as all the fort's cards. Coco's lettering:

*ONE DAY PASS.*
*THE FORT.*
*Valid: today.*
*(Ring Three)*

"Ring Three can't live in the fort," said Umaru. "That's the rule. The rings are the rings. But anybody can visit for a day, with a pass, if the President says so." She held them out. "The President says so."

The first witch looked at the card in Umaru's hand for a long moment, the way she might have looked at an unfamiliar spell.

Then she took it.

The other witch took hers more quickly, as if she were afraid it might be taken back.

"You have to tell the Guardian who you are," said Umaru. "Before you go in."

Milim looked down at the two witches, very solemn. "Who are you?"

The first witch considered the question with apparent seriousness.

"Guests," she said.

Milim thought about it. Then she patted each of them on the head, firmly, once, and stood aside.

"Pass," said the Guardian of the Fort.



They stayed until dusk.

They ate Momo's lunch at the long table, carefully, the first witch with exquisite manners and the other with something that was almost hunger. They sat in the fort. Seiko looked down at them from her high seat with the expression of one old thing recognizing another, and said nothing, and offered the first witch a pickle from her jar. The first witch took it.

Kessoku played on the stage corner. Bocchi played to the floor, the way she always did, and did not look at the strangers once. The other witch sat closer to the stage than anyone, with her hands in her lap, and listened to every note as if she were memorizing it.

Hakari tried to teach them the string game. The first witch learned it in one try and was very good at it and did not enjoy it at all, exactly like Nano, and Nano and the first witch looked at each other with a sort of grim professional respect. The other witch could not get it, and kept trying, and Hakari kept showing her, very patiently. When at last she got it, Hakari cheered, and the other witch looked down at the string between her fingers with an expression that only Hakari saw. Hakari, who was not good at naming things, would only ever describe it afterward as *like someone looking in through a window*.



Mau found his moment in the middle of the afternoon.

Frieren had gone to the kitchen to help Momo, which meant she was in the kitchen watching Momo work and occasionally holding a spoon. The first witch had stepped out into the garden for air, alone, and was standing by the ring of stakes, looking at the ink tree with its ten silver-green leaves.

He went out and stood beside her.

"I wanted to ask you something," he said. "Not a question. Not a session one. A different kind."

The first witch looked at him sideways.

"Frieren's birthday is in three days," said Mau. "She's had about a thousand of them. She stopped counting. She never lets anyone do anything about it." He looked at the tree. "She collects spells. Useless ones. Beautiful, stupid, useless spells. A spell to make a field of flowers. A spell to find a lost button. She has more grimoires than she can carry, and she still wants more." He swallowed. "I don't know what to give her. I thought, maybe, you collect things too. Maybe you'd know of a spell she doesn't have."

The first witch was quiet for a long time.

"There is one of us," she said at last, "who keeps books."

He waited.

"She keeps all the books. Everything she has ever read, she has kept. She has a great many." The first witch looked at the ink tree. "In one of them there is a spell for the morning. It is very old and very small and of no use at all. When you wake, it dresses you in the thing you most love to wear." She paused. "Your Frieren would like it, I think."

Mau stared at her.

"Can I have it? Trade for it? Anything—"

"The book is at our house," said the first witch. "In the forest. The one who keeps books does not lend them, and does not carry them, and does not send them by anyone." She turned and looked at him directly, which she almost never did. "So come yourself."

He opened his mouth.

"We have come to you," said the first witch, "every second week, for a year. To the inn. Through the window. To your yard on the night your seams went dark." She inclined her head very slightly. "You can walk to us once."

Mau looked at her for a long moment.

"Tomorrow," he said.

"Tomorrow," agreed the first witch. And then, after a pause, with the smallest change in her voice: "Bring someone. It is a long walk for a man who could not reach the end of his own corridor a month ago."



At dusk, Diablo walked them back across the plaza toward the road.

At the edge of the yard the other witch stopped, and turned, and looked back at the Muslin for a long time. At the lit windows. At the fort's flag on its broom handle, just visible through the common-room glass. At the long table, where people were still sitting, arguing about nothing.

She still had her card in her hand. ONE DAY PASS. Valid: today.

She did not give it back. Nobody asked her to.

She folded it once, very carefully, and put it somewhere inside her robe. Then she turned and followed the first witch into the dark.
