## Chapter 20 — The Grimoire

The cover story was Fern's.

"Inventory," she said, at breakfast, to the whole table and to Frieren in particular. "At the old inn. It's my day. I need two people to carry."

Fern did the old inn's inventory once a week, and had since the Move. It was a long walk, hours by road, and she always went at dawn and came back in the dark. Nobody had ever questioned it. Nobody ever volunteered for it either.

"I'll carry," said Stark, who had been told about this the night before and was a very bad liar, and so was looking at his porridge.

"I'll carry," said Mau.

Frieren looked up from her book.

"You," she said.

"Maomao said I could go as far as the old inn now. With conditions."

"You've never once helped with the inventory."

"I'm turning over a new leaf," said Mau.

Frieren looked at him for a long time. Her eyes went from him to Fern, to Stark, who was concentrating on his porridge as if it contained instructions, and back to Mau.

"Liar," she said.

But she went back to her book. Her ears had gone very slightly pink.



Maomao's conditions came on a slip of paper, handed to him at the door.

TWO DAYS. SLEEP AT THE OLD INN. STARK CARRIES EVERYTHING. NOTHING HEAVIER THAN A BOOK. IF YOU COME BACK WITH A HOLE IN YOU I WILL PUT YOU BACK IN BED FOR A YEAR. — M.

"A year," said Mau.

"A year," said Maomao, and shut the door.



The old inn was hours by road. Beyond it, the forest.

They reached the inn a little after noon. Rem, who had been told, gave them bread and soup and did not ask a single question. Then they went on, past the inn and the yard and the stable, into the trees.

Mau had been in that forest once before, though he did not remember it. The witches had told him, in the second session, a long time ago. In the first disappearance, during the memory crisis, they had found him collapsed by a stream somewhere in these woods. He had remembered a cabin afterward that had not been there. A door, a woman saying *good morning*. None of it real.

He thought about that as they walked. He wondered whether the cabin he had remembered had been a false memory of a real place. He wondered if he was about to find out.

The forest was very old and very quiet. The trees were huge, and grew far apart, and the ground between them was soft with a hundred years of needles. There was no path. But every so often, at the height of a hand, there was a small mark cut into the bark of a tree. A mark that was not a glyph, and not a letter, and not anything Fern knew. They followed it, tree to tree, deeper in.

"Do you know where we're going?" said Stark, after an hour.

"No," said Mau. "But they do."

"How do you know?"

"They said *come yourself*," said Mau. "They wouldn't say that if they didn't mean to be found."



The cabin was smaller than he had expected.

It stood in a clearing among the old trees: one storey, dark timber, a stone chimney with a thread of smoke going up from it, a door with a wreath of something dried and grey hanging on it. A garden at the side, very neat, with rows of things Mau did not recognize. A bench by the door, with a cup on it.

It was not the cabin he had remembered. He knew that at once. The one in his false memory had been bigger and older and had stood on its own in a meadow. This one was small and real, and somebody had left a cup on the bench.

The door opened before they reached it.

"Good morning," said the first witch, although it was well into the afternoon.



Inside, the cabin was much bigger than it was.

Fern noticed first, because Fern noticed the proportions of rooms. The front room was ordinary: a table, a stove, a shelf of jars. But the door at the back of it, which should have opened onto the back wall of a small timber cabin, opened instead onto a corridor. The corridor went on further than the cabin could possibly have gone, lined on both sides with shelves, and the shelves were full of books.

Not a shelf of books. Not a room of books. A corridor of them, receding into a soft warm light, floor to ceiling. Stark had to sit down on the bench by the door and put his head between his knees for a moment.

"She keeps books," said the first witch mildly, as if that explained it.

The one who kept books came down the corridor to meet them.

She was a woman in black, with hair the color of milk and a face that seemed very young until you looked at her eyes. She looked at Mau the way you look at a book you have heard about for a long time and have finally been handed. She did not smile, exactly. Her mouth did something that was the beginning of a smile, held, as if she were saving the rest for later.

"The boy whose seams shine," she said. "You came yourself."

"You said to."

"Others have been told to," said the woman in black. "Very few come." She looked at Fern, and at Stark on the bench, with polite and total interest, as if they were also books. "You want the spell for the morning."

"For Frieren," said Mau. "For her birthday. It's in two days."

"I know whom it is for." She turned and went back down the corridor of books, and they followed her. It went on for a long time. Mau did not look too closely at the titles. Some of them were in no language he had ever seen, and some of them seemed to be looking back.

She stopped at a shelf, about halfway along, and took down a very small, very old book, bound in something pale.

"It is not a powerful spell," she said, turning the pages. "It is not a useful one. In my world, a woman who could not decide what to wear in the mornings wrote it for herself, and used it every day of her life, and was laughed at for it. When she died, nobody else wanted it. I kept it." She found the page. "I keep everything."

She did not give him the book. She took a blank one from a different shelf, small and thread-bound, and a pen. In a fine, precise hand she copied the spell onto its first page while they watched, line by line, with every mark exact. When she was finished, she blew on the ink, closed the little book, and held it out.

Mau reached for it.

She did not let go.

"There is a price," said the woman in black.



Mau had expected that. He had expected it all the way through the forest. The witches had once asked him for two hours, in the old inn, for a leak he had called for them on purpose. *You owe us two.* Prices were part of the agreement.

"What is it?" he said.

The woman in black looked at him with her saved half-smile.

"Tell me something," she said, "that I do not know."

Mau looked at her. At the corridor of books going on behind her into the soft light, further than a cabin could go. A woman who had kept everything she had ever read.

"I don't think there's anything I know that you don't," he said honestly.

"There is one thing," said the woman in black. "There always is." She tilted her head. "We stood in your yard for two days, on the night your seams went dark. We watched your house wait for you. A woman with white hair sat on a corridor floor outside a door and did not move." She paused. "I have read every account of waiting that has ever been written down in my world. I have never once been the one waited for." The half-smile did not change, but something behind it did. "Tell me what it is like."

Nobody said anything.

Fern, beside Mau, had gone very still.

Mau thought about it for a long time. He thought about a woman reading by a tree all day with a book she could not possibly have been reading. About a fort that sent notes. About a corridor floor, and a ribbon on his ankle, and a word sent home each evening from a rock. About walking down a river path at dusk and seeing the whole house come out into the garden.

"It hurts," he said at last. "That's the first thing. It hurts, to know someone's waiting. Because you know what it's costing them, and you can't stop it, and you can't hurry. You can only come back as fast as you can." He paused. "And it's the easiest thing in the world. Coming back. When you know someone's there. You don't even have to think about which way to walk."

The woman in black said nothing.

"Frieren said that," said Mau. "Not to me. To the tree, I think. While I was gone. She said she'd always thought waiting would be the hard part, and it was the easiest thing she'd ever done. It was only that it hurt." He looked at the little book in the woman's hand. "I think it's the same from both ends."

The woman in black was quiet for a long time.

Then, very slowly, she let go of the book.

"That," she said, "I did not know."

She turned and went back up the corridor of books without another word, and did not come back. A little later, from somewhere deep among the shelves, came the faint scratch of a pen. She was writing it down.



They did not leave at once. The first witch would not hear of it. It was a long walk, and the light was going, and in the forest at night the marks on the trees were hard to see. So they sat in the front room of the cabin, at the plain table by the stove, and the first witch gave them tea.

Stark held his cup in both hands and did not drink any, in case it was anything. The first witch watched him with mild amusement and did not tell him it was only tea.

The other witch came in from the garden. She was the one who never spoke, the one who had taken a ONE DAY PASS and not given it back. She sat down at the far end of the table from Fern and looked at her, sideways, for a long time.

Then she looked at the ledger.

Fern had brought it, of course. Fern brought the ledger everywhere. It was on the table beside her cup.

"Is that," said the other witch, very quietly, "your house?"

Fern looked at her for a moment.

Then she turned the ledger around and pushed it across the table.

The other witch did not touch it at first. Then, very carefully, she opened it to a page in the middle, and read.

It was nothing. It was a page of a household ledger. *Day thirty: hamburg steak, four attempts, three failed; Momo's good pan survived. Day thirty-one: drill; Mau at the inner junction, Maomao's note on his card. Day thirty-two: Hakari's group out of string; Kessoku, new song, no title yet. Umaru beat Milim at cards, again.* Fern's neat hand, line after line, every ordinary thing that had happened in the house for a month.

The other witch read every line on the page. Then she turned to the next page and read every line on that one.

Fern did not take the ledger back. She drank her tea and let the witch read, all the way to the end of the month, until the light was gone from the windows and the first witch lit a lamp. When the other witch finally closed it and pushed it back across the table, her face had the look Hakari would have called *like someone looking in through a window*.

"Thank you," she said.

It was the second thing Mau had ever heard her say.



They slept at the old inn, as Maomao had ordered, and walked home the next day.

Mau carried the little thread-bound book inside his coat the whole way, against his chest, and did not take it out once. Every so often he put his hand flat over it, to check. It was nothing heavier than a book. Stark carried everything else.

They came in through the side door at dusk, past the ink tree.

Frieren was in the common room, in the reading chair by the cold hearth, with a book. She looked up as they came in. She looked at Fern, and Stark, and Mau with his hand flat on the front of his coat, and at the three of them with forest needles in their hair and mud on their boots that had certainly not come from the road to the old inn.

"How was the inventory?" said Frieren.

"Thorough," said Fern.

"Very thorough," said Stark.

"We counted everything," said Mau.

Frieren looked at him for a long, long time.

"Liar," she said.

Her ears were pink all the way to the tips.
