## Chapter 21 — Frieren's Birthday

Frieren tried to leave before dawn.

She did it every year. She had done it for as long as Fern had known her, and for a great many centuries before that. On the day, she got up early, very quietly, took a book, and went somewhere. A ruin, a hill, a long road, a room with the door shut. It did not matter where. She went somewhere where there was nothing to celebrate, because nobody would be there to celebrate it, and she read until the day was over, and it was over.

She got up very quietly. She did not wake Mau. She took her book and the hoodie and went down the family corridor in the grey dark, past Ori's door and the narrow room at the turn, and across the common room toward the front door.

The fort was in front of it.

Not in the north end of the common room, where the fort lived. In front of the front door. Or rather the fort's citizens were. Every one of them, in a solid line across the doorway, in the grey before dawn, in their nightclothes and their blankets and their winter coats. The President in her hood and sash in the middle. The Opposition beside her. Milim at the front, under no Beam at all, with her arms folded.

"Who are you?" said the Guardian of the Fort.

Frieren looked at them for a long time.

"I'm going for a walk," she said.

"Not today," said Umaru.

"How," said Frieren, very quietly, "do you know?"

Fern stepped out of the line.

"You told me," said Fern. "When I was six. You'd forgotten I asked. I hadn't." She folded her hands. "You said elves stop counting after the first few hundred. I said I'd count for you. Every year I've written it down. Every year you've gone somewhere so I couldn't do anything about it." She took a breath. "This year you can't go anywhere. The Guardian won't let you."

"The Guardian won't let you," agreed Milim.

Frieren looked at Fern for a very long time.

Then she put her book down on the long table, carefully, and did not pick it up again all day.



They did not do it loudly. That was Fern's rule. Frieren did not like loud. So they did it the way the house had learned to do Bocchi's: small things, one at a time, without anyone gathering or singing.

Fern went to the Birthday Board, picked up the charcoal, and in the column it belonged in, on the line it belonged on, she wrote:

FRIEREN

Under it, over the course of the day, the slips filled up.

The fort gave her a cushion. It was the seventh slip from the annex, FRIEREN (SISTER-IN-LAW), pinned to a cushion of her own. They put it in the main room of the fort, between Ori's and the window in the south wall, where she could see down the corridor to the room with the east window if she leaned. Frieren sat on it for exactly one minute, very correctly, with her hands in her lap, while the whole fort watched her do it in total silence. Then she stood up and said "It's a good cushion," and the fort cheered as if she had won a war.

Umaru gave her a card. ONE FREE DAY TO DO NOTHING. With the Ministry's seal.

"I do nothing every day," said Frieren.

"Now it's official," said Umaru.

Milim gave her a box. A small wooden box with a hinged lid, which Milim had made herself, badly, with a great deal of help and several injured thumbs that were not hers. Frieren opened it. It was empty.

"It's a mimic," said Milim, with tremendous pride. "For practice. Because you're AGENT MIMIC. So you can practice not putting your head in."

Frieren looked at the box. Then, slowly and deliberately, in front of everyone, she put her head in it. It was much too small. She got about as far as her nose.

Milim laughed so hard she had to lie down on the floor.

Stark gave her a pair of warm socks, which he said were from both of them, him and Fern. Fern said they were from Stark, and that the socks were the important part, and Stark went red.

Seiko gave her nothing. She looked down from her high seat in the fort and said, "Happy birthday. You're too old for presents," and then, after a pause, "So am I. Nobody listens." Then she threw down a pickle wrapped in a cloth, which Frieren caught.

Himmel came in the middle of the afternoon, alone. He did not say anything. He held out a single flower, a small blue one, the kind that grew along the river path. Frieren looked at it for a long moment and took it. Himmel nodded once, with a face entirely at peace, and went out again. Nobody asked her about it. She put the flower in a cup of water on the windowsill of the room with the east window, beside the stone, and did not explain that either.

And Mau did not give her anything all day.

She noticed. He saw her notice. Every time a small thing arrived and Mau was there and did not have one, her eyes went to him, just for a second, and then away. She did not say anything. She was being Frieren about it. But her ears did something every time, a small drooping, quickly corrected.



He gave it to her at night.

In the room with the east window, with the lamp low and the door shut and Rimuru up in the loft, very quietly pretending to be asleep. Frieren was sitting on the edge of the bed in the hoodie, taking off the socks Stark had given her, when Mau took the little thread-bound book out of his coat, where it had been all day, against his chest.

He held it out.

Frieren looked at it.

"What's that?"

"Open it."

She took it. She opened it to the first page and read, by the low light of the lamp, the spell copied out there in a fine, precise hand that was not his.

Mau watched her read it.

She read it once, quickly, the way she read anything new, to see what it was. Then she stopped. Then she read it again, slowly, all the way through, her lips moving very slightly the way they did when she was following the shape of a spell in her head. Then she read it a third time.

"It's useless," she said.

Her voice did something strange in the middle of the word.

"It dresses you in what you most love to wear," said Mau. "In the mornings. When you wake up. That's all it does."

"It's completely useless," said Frieren. She did not take her eyes off the page. "Nobody would ever need this. There's no reason in any world for this spell to exist." Her thumb moved very slowly along the edge of the page. "It's the most useless spell I've ever seen."

"I know," said Mau. "I thought you'd like it."

Frieren looked up.

Her eyes were shining. Not wet. Shining, the way they shone in the flower field, or by the sea at night, or anywhere there was something new and beautiful and completely pointless that nobody else had ever wanted.

"Where did you get this?"

Mau looked at the floor.

"Inventory," he said.

Frieren stared at him.

"There was no inventory," she said slowly. "There was mud on your boots that wasn't road mud. There were needles in Fern's hair. Stark couldn't look at his porridge." She looked down at the book again, at the fine precise hand. "This isn't your writing. This isn't anyone's writing in this house."

"No."

"Mau."

He told her. Not all of it. The witches under the window, and the one who kept books, and the forest with marks on the trees, and the cabin that was bigger inside than out. A corridor of books, and a price, and a question nobody in that cabin had ever been able to answer. He did not tell her what his answer had been. That part had been for the woman in black, and for the tree.

Frieren listened to all of it with the book held against her chest.

When he finished, she did not say anything for a long time.

"Two days," she said at last. "You walked two days. A month after you couldn't reach the end of the corridor. For a spell that doesn't do anything."

"It does something," said Mau. "It dresses you."

Frieren made a sound. It might have been a laugh.



They went to bed.

Frieren lay down first, on her back, on her side of the bed, the way she slept now. Carefully. Since the night of the bay she had not slept on top of him. Not once. Not even a little. She slept beside him, close, with a hand on his arm, and the rest of her very carefully not on him. He had noticed. He had never said anything. He had not known what to say.

Tonight he knew.

He did not lie down beside her. He moved over, slowly, and lay down on top of her instead. Carefully, the way she used to lie on him, his weight on his elbows, his face above hers in the lamplight.

She went rigid.

Her whole body went still underneath him, the way it had gone still at the edge of the bed once, a long time ago, with her hand on a sheet that was wet. Her eyes went wide. Her breath stopped.

"Frieren," said Mau. "Hey. Look at me."

She looked at him.

"I hope you know," said Mau quietly, "that it wasn't your fault."

She did not move.

"You were lying on me," said Mau. "That night. I know you were. Nobody told me, but I know. I know because you haven't done it since, not once, not even a little. You sleep next to me like I'm made of glass." He held her eyes. "It wasn't you. It was never you. It was the thing in me that didn't understand the walls, and it's understood now. Ciel checks every day. I'm fine." He breathed out. "I'm fine. Look. I'm fine."

Frieren's eyes were very wet now.

He held her gaze for a moment longer. Then he moved off her, slowly, and lay down on his back on his own side of the bed, and held out his arm.

"You can come back," he said. "If you want. You can lie on me. Just carefully. The left side under the ribs is still a bit sore." He paused. "Yuta told Maomao."

Frieren looked at him for a long time.

Then, very slowly, telegraphing every inch of it, the way Maki had once taught her without either of them knowing, she moved over, and lay down on top of him. Carefully. With her weight on her own arms, and then, when nothing happened, a little less on her arms, and then, when still nothing happened, not on her arms at all. Her chin on his chest. Her hair everywhere.

The way it had been.

She lay there and breathed. He lay underneath her and breathed. Nothing happened. Nothing at all.

After a long while, she lifted her head.

"Say it again," said Frieren.



He almost laughed. He did not. She was looking at him with an expression of enormous expectation, her ears up, waiting.

"Say what?"

"All of it," said Frieren. "Everything. From the beginning."

"Which beginning?"

"Today's." She put her chin back down on his chest and looked up at him. "Why you went. What you said to the woman with the books. Why you walked two days. All of it."

So he said it.

He said that she was the most important person in his life. He said it plainly, the way she said true things, because she had taught him how. He said that he loved her. He said he had heard about the spell from the first witch in the garden, by the ink tree, and had known at once that it was the most useless spell in the world and that she would love it. He said he had walked through a forest with marks on the trees for a whole day. He had sat in a cabin bigger inside than out, and been asked a question by a woman who kept every book she had ever read, and answered it, for her. He said that he had wanted, more than he had wanted anything since he came back, to give her one thing that was entirely, completely useless, so that she would have something new to collect, on a day she had never once let anyone touch.

Frieren listened to all of it with her ears up and her eyes on his face, absolutely still, the way she listened to a spell she had never heard before.

When he stopped, she waited.

He knew what she was waiting for. Her ears told him.

He went red. He felt it go up his neck and into his face and all the way to his own ears.

"Frieren," said Mau. "I love you. You're the most beautiful person I know."

Frieren looked at him.

"*Idiot*," she said.

But her face said something else entirely. Her face said, as clearly as any face had ever said anything: *yes. You're right. I know.*



From the loft, very small, came a sound.

It was a snort. Muffled, desperate, and completely unmistakable. Somebody up there had been holding it in for a very long time, and could not hold it in for one second more.

Frieren did not look up.

She lifted one hand off Mau's chest, without moving anything else, and pointed one finger, without looking, at the loft.

A line of light went past the edge of the loft boards a hand's width from the small blue shape lying there. It was very thin, very bright and very fast. It went on through the gap under the roof and out into the night, and was gone. Zoltraak. Frieren could put one through a demon at a hundred paces.

She had missed. On purpose. By exactly a hand.

The loft went completely silent.

In two heads in that room, and only two, someone began to laugh.

It was Ciel. Rimuru had never once heard her laugh in his life, in either world. She laughed, helplessly and for a long time, in a voice he had not known she had.

*Master,* said Ciel at last, when she could. *That is what you get. For being nosy.*

Rimuru did not answer. He lay very flat on his small pillow, a hand's width from a neat, smoking hole in the boards under the roof, and did not move again all night.

Wakana fixed the hole the next morning without asking anyone how it had got there.



They fell asleep like that. Frieren on top of him, her hair across both of them, the little thread-bound book on the nightstand, his arm across her back. The lamp went out on its own sometime after midnight.

Some time before dawn, Frieren woke.

She woke all at once, the way you wake from a fall in a dream, with her heart going too fast. Her hand was already moving, on its own, down onto the bed beside him, onto the sheet, feeling for something.

It was dry.

She lay very still with her hand flat on the dry sheet and her heart slowing, and listened. He was breathing, slow and even, underneath her. Warm. There was no wet. There was nothing. Only the dark, and the east window going grey, and the faint smell of the river on his skin.

She had only remembered. That was all. Her body had remembered, the way his did with rain.

She put her hand back on his chest, over his heart, and felt it go. Then she put her head back down and closed her eyes.



In the morning, Frieren woke in the hoodie.

She had not been wearing it when she went to sleep. She had taken it off in the night, when it got too warm with him under her, and dropped it on the floor beside the bed. She remembered doing it.

She was wearing it now. The black-and-orange one, too big in the shoulders, the sleeves past her fingers, exactly the way she liked it.

She lay on top of him in the grey morning light and looked at the sleeves over her hands for a long time.

She had cast it once, in the dark, after he fell asleep, from the little book, under her breath. To see. And it had seen what she loved to wear, more than any robe or cloak or anything she had ever owned in a thousand years, and it had put her in it.

Mau woke up and found her looking at him.

"Happy birthday," he said, sleepily, a day late.

Frieren looked down at him from inside the hoodie.

"Useless," she said.

And she did not take it off all day.
