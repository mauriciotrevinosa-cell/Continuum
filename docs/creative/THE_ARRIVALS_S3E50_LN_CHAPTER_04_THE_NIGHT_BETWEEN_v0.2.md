# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 18
## Chapter 16 — The Night Between

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 16  
**Continuity state:** Night after Last Defense | Household sleeps in the common room | One Searcher still unresolved

Nobody decided that the household would sleep in the common room.

Forty-three beds were unusable. Everyone knew it before Fern counted. The refuge had the bunks that still stood, the injured had medical, the guests had the old inn and the second community's offer.

That left the floor.

So the household came to the common room the way water comes downhill, one by one, still in what they had fought in, and lay down wherever there was space, and did not discuss it.



Okarun did not lie down so much as stop being upright.

He had run all day.

North line to inner junction. Inner junction to medical. Medical to the north line again, with Turbo Granny's speed in his legs and Turbo Granny's voice in his ear, carrying messages when the relay went dark and carrying people when there were no more messages to carry.

He had stopped counting at nineteen trips.

His legs had stopped working somewhere around sunset.

Momo found him face down beside the fireplace and sat down beside him without a word and pulled his feet into her lap.

"Ow," said Okarun into the floor.

"I know."

"Ow."

"I know." She started working her thumbs into his calves, hard, the way Seiko had taught her when she was little and had run too far. "Shut up and let me."

Okarun shut up and let her.

From somewhere above them, small and cross and impossible to locate, Turbo Granny said:

"Idiot ran too much."

"You made me run," said Okarun.

"I lent you legs. I didn't tell you to use them nineteen times."

Okarun turned his head so his cheek was on the floor.

"Twenty," he said. "I lost count."

There was a long silence.

Then, so quietly that Momo nearly missed it:

"You did okay, idiot."

Okarun went very still.

"What?"

"Nothing. Go to sleep. Your face is ugly when you're awake."

Momo bent her head over Okarun's legs so that he would not see her smile.

He saw it anyway.



Marin went around with a basin.

She had a cloth and a basin of warm water and a look on her face that dared anyone to refuse her, and she went from person to person and washed the soot off their faces.

"Beauty isn't optional tonight," she said, to Maki, who had tried to wave her off.

Maki looked at her.

Then sat still and let her.

Marin did Kita, who was half asleep and leaned into it like a cat. She did Umaru, who had been running supply boards for twelve hours and had ink on her nose. She did Qifrey, who looked so surprised that he forgot to object. She did Coco, who was asleep and did not wake.

Then she came to Mikasa.

Mikasa was sitting against the wall by the door with her scarf around her neck and her eyes on the window, the way she had been sitting since sunset, the way she would sit all night if nobody stopped her.

Marin knelt down in front of her with the basin.

Mikasa looked at the cloth.

"I'm fine."

"You're filthy," said Marin cheerfully. "Hold still."

Mikasa did not hold still. She tilted her head away.

Marin waited.

She did not push.

She just knelt there with the warm cloth and the basin and a patient, ridiculous, completely immovable smile.

After a while, Mikasa closed her eyes.

Marin washed her face very gently. Forehead. Cheeks. The cut along her jaw, carefully, around the edges. The smoke-dark lines beside her eyes.

When she was done, Mikasa opened her eyes again.

She did not say thank you.

She reached up and touched her own clean cheek, once, as if checking it was still hers.

Wakana, across the room, watched Marin carry the basin away to the next person and did not say anything at all, but he looked at her the way people look at the thing they would rebuild first if everything else fell down.



Nazuna woke up at sunset.

She had slept through the entire Last Defense.

Everyone had agreed in advance that she would. There was no use for a vampire at noon, and every use for one at midnight.

She came down the stairs into a room full of sleeping, broken, soot-faced people, and stopped.

She looked at them for a long time.

Then she went to the door, where Mikasa was still sitting.

"Go to sleep," said Nazuna.

Mikasa did not move.

"Someone has to watch the north road."

"Yeah," said Nazuna. "Me."

Mikasa looked at her.

"It's night," said Nazuna. "Night's mine. I can see a rabbit move at a thousand meters in the dark. You can't see your own feet." She crouched down to Mikasa's level. "I'll wake you if anything moves. I promise. I'll wake you before I wake anyone else."

Mikasa looked at the window.

At the dark.

At Nazuna, who was so seldom serious that it was startling when she was.

"Anko's coming with me," Nazuna added. "So I don't get bored and do something stupid."

From the far side of the room, Anko raised one hand without opening her eyes.

"Correct."

Mikasa looked at the door for one more moment.

Then she lay down.

Just like that, against the wall, with her scarf pulled up over her chin.

She was asleep almost at once.

Nazuna stood up and looked down at her, and something in her face was soft in a way she would have denied under torture.

"Come on," she said to Anko. "Roof."

Anko got up, grumbling, and followed her out.

Later, much later, if anyone had looked up, they would have seen two shapes on the roof against the dark sky: one sitting very still, watching the north, and one lying on her back with her hands behind her head, talking quietly about nothing.

Neither of them saw anything move all night.

Nazuna counted that as a good shift.



Yuta could not lift his arms.

He had been healing since midmorning.

Not dramatic healing. Not miracles. A cut here, a crushed hand there, a break that needed setting while Maomao held it straight, a burn, another, a child who had fallen during evacuation, a militia fighter who had cursed at him the whole time and then cried. Hour after hour. Body after body.

By nightfall his hands were shaking worse than Mau's.

Maki put a bowl of soup in front of him.

He looked at it.

"I can't—"

"I know you can't," said Maki. "That's why I'm holding the spoon."

Yuta looked at her.

"Maki—"

"Open."

He opened.

Across the room, Mau watched them for a while.

Then he got up, slowly, because standing had become work, and filled a cup with tea, and carried it over in both hands to stop it spilling, and set it down beside Yuta.

Yuta looked up at him.

For a moment neither of them said anything.

It had always been the other way around. Since the first week. Yuta bringing tea to Mau. Yuta noticing. Yuta the older brother.

"Your hands are shaking," said Yuta.

"So are yours."

"Mine are from healing."

"Mine are from—" Mau stopped. "I don't know what mine are from."

Yuta held out his own shaking hand.

After a moment, Mau put his shaking hand in it.

They sat like that while Maki fed Yuta soup with the other hand, and none of the three of them made it into anything, and all of them knew exactly what it was.



Mai sat apart.

She always did, at first.

She had stood at the east door of the refuge all day, with her revolver, and the one bullet she had made that morning.

She made one every morning. It was what she could afford. Her body paid for it the way other bodies paid for running.

She had not fired it.

Nothing had come through the east door.

She sat on the window seat with the bullet in her hand and turned it over and over, a small, heavy, useless thing, and did not look at anybody.

Maki saw.

Maki got up, leaving Yuta in Mau's keeping, and went over, and sat down beside her sister, and did not ask.

After a while Mai said:

"I didn't need it."

"Good."

"I made it, and I stood there all day, and I didn't need it."

"That's good, Mai."

Mai turned the bullet over.

"I know," she said. "It just feels like I didn't do anything."

Mau, across the room, was looking at his own hand.

It was still shaking.

Mai looked at it too.

She looked at it for longer than she meant to.

Then she got up, crossed the room, and sat down on the floor in front of him.

"Construction," she said.

Mau looked at her.

"That's what they call yours," said Mai. "Construction." She held up the bullet. "That's what mine's called too."

Mau looked at the bullet.

"I didn't know that."

"Most people don't." Mai turned it in her fingers. "Mine costs me. Every time. One of these a day and I feel it for hours. Bigger than this and I'd be in medical." She looked at his hand. "I thought yours didn't cost you anything."

"It costs."

"Yes," said Mai. "I can see that now."

She was quiet.

Then she put the bullet on the floor between them.

"Mine shakes too," she said. "After. Every time."

She held out her hand, palm down, so he could see.

It did.

Very faintly.

Mau looked at her hand, and at his own, and at the small unfired thing on the floor between them that she had made out of her own body that morning in case someone needed protecting.

"You did do something," he said.

Mai looked at him sharply.

"You stood at the door," said Mau. "All day. So nobody behind it had to wonder who was standing there."

Mai did not say anything for a long time.

She did not say thank you.

She did not say anything sentimental at all.

But she did not go back to the window seat.

She stayed on the floor, next to Maki, a little closer to everyone than she had been, and fell asleep there sometime after midnight with her shaking hand curled around the bullet she had not needed.



It rained, a little after midnight.

Rem did not go to the narrow room at the end of the corridor.

She had given it to a mother and two small children from the refuge before anyone had to ask, and folded her blanket over the children, and come back down to the common room, and was sitting against the wall with Ori asleep on her shoulder.

Frieren saw.

She did not say anything.

She reached over and pulled a corner of her own blanket across Rem's knees, and went back to watching Mau not sleep.



Fern did not sleep.

She stood at the long table with the search list.

One name was not crossed out.

A Searcher. Missing after the battle. Not dead. Not found.

She stood there with the list and the lamp and did not sit down, because sitting down would mean she had stopped, and she had not stopped.

Stark did not try to make her sit.

He had learned.

He sat on the floor instead, with his back against the leg of the long table, close enough that she could have touched his head without moving her feet, and stayed awake as long as he could.

He fell asleep around three, sitting up, as if he might be needed.

Fern looked down at him.

She did not sit.

But she moved her lamp so the light would not be in his eyes.



Near the end of the night, when the rain had stopped and the room had gone heavy with sleep, somebody started to cry.

One of the refuge children, who had come in with Bocchi earlier and been too frightened to leave her side.

Bocchi woke at once.

She looked at the child.

She looked at the guitar against the wall.

She did not reach for it.

Kita, beside her, did not wake.

But Kita's hand found Bocchi's in the dark without looking, and held on.

Bocchi breathed in.

Then, without the guitar, she hummed it.

Three notes.

Soft.

Again.

The child's crying slowed.

Three notes.

Again.

Across the room, Coco murmured in her sleep and turned over. Umaru sighed. Okarun, face down beside the fire, his feet still in Momo's lap, stopped twitching.

Mau, who had not slept at all, closed his eyes.

Three notes.

Again.

By the time the window turned grey, almost everyone was asleep.

Frieren was not.

She was sitting up against the wall with Mau's head finally on her shoulder, watching the light change, listening to forty people breathe.

She counted them.

Not because she was afraid.

Because she wanted to.

All of them.

Then, a little after dawn, she let herself close her eyes too.
