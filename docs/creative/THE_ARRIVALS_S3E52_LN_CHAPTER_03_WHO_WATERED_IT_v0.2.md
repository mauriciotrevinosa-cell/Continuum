# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 18
## Chapter 24 — Who Watered It

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 18  
**Reading order:** 24  
**Continuity state:** Rebuild weeks | Memorial for the three Last Defense dead | Household attends as part of everyone

The memorial was not the household's to plan.

Fern made sure of that before anyone else had to.

Three of the settlement's people had died in the Last Defense. A volunteer from the east village who had gone back into the north lane for a family that was not his. Two residents of the settlement: a woman named Dessa who worked the washing court and had come through Arrival House in the first wave of refuge, and an old man everyone had called Pim, who had arrived with nothing and spent his mornings mending nets nobody had asked him to mend.

A fourth had died too, from the other side: a militia fighter from the hostile column who had died pulling civilians out of an overturned wagon. His captain had asked for his body, and Fern had made sure he got it before anyone else had to decide how to feel about it.

None of the three had lived in the family wing.

None of them had been at the long table.

Fern went to the people who had known them, one by one, and asked what they wanted, and wrote it down, and then did exactly that and nothing else.

The household came as part of everyone.

That was the point.



The east village came on foot, early, carrying bread.

Marin had made paper flowers.

She had been up half the night making them, the way she made things when she did not know what else to do with her hands, and she was halfway across the commons with an armful of them when she saw the villagers' bread, and stopped.

She stood there for a moment.

Then she turned around and walked back to the volunteer's mother, a small woman with a face like a closed door, and said, carefully:

"I made these. I don't know if they're right. Where I'm from, we bring flowers. I don't know what you do."

The woman looked at the paper flowers for a long time.

"Flowers are for weddings," she said. "For the dead, we bring bread. So they don't go hungry on the road."

Marin nodded.

She did not look embarrassed.

She looked like someone who had learned something.

"Then I'll put them away."

The woman looked at the flowers again.

"No," she said. "He liked weddings. He liked dancing. Put one on the bread."

Marin put one on the bread.

The woman watched her do it, and some small part of the closed door opened, just slightly.

Momo and Seiko had been baking since before dawn.

Nobody had told them about the bread.

They had simply decided, in the way that grandmothers and granddaughters decide things between them without discussion, that a day like this needed food. When they heard what the village did, Seiko looked at the trays and said, "Hm. Good," and sent Momo to give half of it to the volunteer's mother, for the road.



Each of the three had people who spoke.

The volunteer's brother spoke, briefly, in his own language, and then in the trade language for those who did not know it. He said his brother had been bad at farming, good at fixing roofs, and impossible to stop once he had decided someone needed help.

Several people in the household looked, very briefly, at Mau.

Mau looked at the ground.

Dessa's friend from the washing court spoke. She was a large, red-handed woman who had arrived through Arrival House the same week Dessa had. She did not cry. She said Dessa had sung terribly every morning, and that the washing court would be too quiet now, and that she would sing badly herself starting tomorrow so somebody did.

Nobody spoke for Pim.

There was an awkward space where somebody should have.

Then Okarun stood up.

He looked very surprised to be standing.

"He fixed my shoe," Okarun said. "Last month. I tore the sole running a route, and I was sitting on the wall trying to tie it together, and he came over and took it out of my hands and sewed it with net string. He didn't say anything. He just gave it back." Okarun looked down at his own foot. "It's still holding."

He sat down again quickly, red to the ears.

Momo took his hand.

After that, other people stood up.

Someone Pim had given a fishhook to. Someone whose child he had taught to tie a knot. Kita, who had once sat beside him on the wall at the old inn for an hour without either of them speaking, and had liked it. Six people. Then eight.

Nobody had known him well.

Everybody had known him a little.

It added up to more than anyone expected.



Afterward, the gathering broke apart into the kind of standing-around that follows such things. Bread passed hand to hand. People stayed longer than they needed to.

Frieren did not stand around.

She went from person to person.

She went to Dessa's friend from the washing court and asked what songs Dessa had sung badly. She went to the volunteer's brother and asked what roofs he had fixed. She went to the people who had spoken for Pim, and to the people who had not, and asked each of them what they knew about an old man she had nodded to perhaps three times in passing.

Some of them were puzzled.

The elf who had stood on the east line throwing light at flying monsters, asking them about a net-mender's mornings.

But they told her.

Mau watched her do it from the edge of the commons.

He understood, slowly, what she was doing.

So did Himmel.

Himmel had been standing near the bread table, and when Frieren finally came back toward them with her hands empty and her face very still, he was the one who spoke first.

"You're asking about them," he said.

Frieren looked at him.

"I didn't ask about you," she said. "Then."

Himmel was quiet.

"At your funeral," Frieren said, and stopped, and started again. "At your funeral I realized I didn't know anything about you. Ten years. And I didn't know what you liked to eat or why you did half the things you did. I'd been standing next to you and I hadn't looked." She looked back at the commons. "I spent a long time after that going around asking people what you'd been like."

Himmel did not say anything for a while.

Mau did not either.

This was not his.

He stayed anyway, because Frieren had not asked him to go.

"Fern told me," Himmel said at last, gently. "After I came here. She said you cried."

Frieren's ears went faintly pink.

"Fern talks too much."

"Fern," said Himmel, "talks exactly the right amount." He smiled, a little crookedly. "I'm glad you asked about them. I'm glad you didn't wait until it was too late to know them at all."

"It was already too late," said Frieren.

"Not for knowing them," said Himmel. "Only for telling them."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment.

Then at Mau.

Then she reached over without looking and took hold of Mau's sleeve, the way she did when she wanted to be sure where he was, and held on.



Turbo Granny had been sitting on the edge of the bread table the entire time, insulting the bread.

When the gathering began to thin, Mau found himself beside her.

He did not know why he stayed.

"You're thinking stupid things," said Turbo Granny, without looking at him.

"I'm not thinking anything."

"You're thinking they died because of you." She broke off a piece of the bread she had been insulting and ate it. "Because the papers those idiots carry say there's a man in charge here, and the papers have your name in them."

Mau said nothing.

That was answer enough.

"I've been dead a long time," said Turbo Granny. "You want to know what's bad about it?"

Mau looked at her.

"Not the dying. Dying is quick. Dying's nothing." She chewed. "Being forgotten. That's the bad part. That's the part that lasts."

She jerked her chin at the commons. At Frieren, still talking quietly with someone from the washing court. At Okarun, still holding Momo's hand. At the volunteer's mother, with Marin's paper flower on her bread.

"Nobody's forgetting those three," said Turbo Granny. "So shut up and eat something, idiot. You look like a corpse, and I know what those look like."

She shoved the rest of the bread at him.

Mau ate it.

It was good bread.



Dessa's friend found him later.

The large red-handed woman from the washing court, who had promised to sing badly.

She stood in front of him with her arms folded.

"You're Mau."

"Yes."

"Somebody told me you've been walking around since the battle looking like it's your fault."

Mau opened his mouth.

She did not let him use it.

"Dessa didn't die for you," the woman said. "She didn't even like you much. She thought you worked too hard and made everyone else feel lazy." Her voice did not shake at all. "She died because the refuge door jammed and there were kids behind it and she was the closest one with a crowbar. She died for those kids. That's hers." She jabbed a thick finger at his chest. "Don't you dare take it off her and put it on yourself."

Mau stood very still.

Then he nodded.

"I won't," he said.

The woman looked at him for a long moment.

"Good," she said, and went back to the bread.



The board still hung on Wakana's stake by the garden circle.

WHO WATERS IT

Everyone.

The sapling behind it had grown past Coco's knee now, thin and stubborn, with leaves that turned toward the morning light in a way nobody could quite explain.

Coco stood in front of it with a stick of charcoal.

She had been standing there for some time.

Frieren found her.

Then Mau, because he had followed Frieren.

Then Qifrey, because he had followed Coco.

"Dessa watered it," Coco said, without turning around. "Every morning. On her way to the washing court. She had a blue bucket. She'd stop and pour a little bit and then keep going. She never said anything. I don't even know if she knew what it was." Coco's voice wobbled. "She just saw the board."

She lifted the charcoal.

Underneath Everyone, small, careful, in her best hand, she wrote:

Dessa.

Then, after a moment:

Pim.

She stopped.

She did not know the volunteer's name.

Frieren did.

She had asked.

She said it quietly.

Coco wrote it.

Then she stepped back, and looked at the board, and her face crumpled, and she turned around and walked straight into Frieren, and Frieren caught her.

Not stiffly.

Not like someone who did not know what to do.

Like someone who had watched Mau do it for long enough to know exactly how.

Mau did not move.

Neither did Qifrey.

They stood a little way back, the two of them, and let Frieren hold her.

After a while Qifrey said, very quietly, so only Mau could hear:

"She's learned."

"Yes," said Mau.

"From you."

Mau watched Frieren's hand move on Coco's back, slow and steady, the way his own did.

"From everyone," he said. "I think she's been learning from everyone."

The sapling moved a little in the wind.

The board said:

WHO WATERS IT

Everyone.

And under that, three names, in charcoal, that the rain would eventually wear away, and that Coco would rewrite every time it did, for as long as she lived there, without ever once being asked to.
