# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 19
## Chapter 16 — Hundreds

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 19  
**Reading order:** 16  
**Continuity state:** Late Trial | Exact loop count intentionally unresolved | Scale confirmed as hundreds

Mau stopped counting somewhere after sixty.

He thought.

That was the problem.

Maybe fifty-eight.

Maybe seventy-one.

Several loops lasted seconds.

Others lasted days.

A handful lasted years subjectively.

Counting deaths without counting lived time became meaningless.

Ciel's number still came at every anchor, one word through the wall. To her, every loop was an instant. A loop that lasted him three years arrived in the thread exactly like one that lasted three seconds: one jolt, one more. She could not know the difference. She counted anyway.

He stopped hearing the numbers somewhere in the hundreds. He knew they were in the hundreds because once, without meaning to, he caught the first word of one.

After that he made himself stop listening.

She never stopped saying them.

So Mau changed the metric.

He counted people.

Frieren.

Rimuru.

Bocchi.

Ori.

Yuta.

Maki.

Fern.

Himmel.

Strangers.

Enemies.

Children.

Adults.

People who hated him.

People who loved him.

People who chose him.

People who begged him not to choose for them.

That count failed too.

The Trial repeated faces under different conditions.

Same person.

Different choice.

Different regret.

Different ending.

At some point Mau asked:

"Have I died a hundred times?"

The Goddess answered:

"Yes."

Nothing else.

Mau stood still.

The number should have meant something.

It did not.

Later:

"Two hundred?"

Silence.

Not confirmation.

Not denial.

Mau stopped asking.

Hundreds became the only useful scale.

Not an exact count.

A category his mind could not hold all at once.

He remembered selected deaths perfectly.

The first.

Frieren's bridge.

Bocchi behind smoke.

Ori's door.

A stranger who thanked him before Mau died.

A hostile fighter who cursed him for interfering.

Many others blurred.

That frightened him.

Not forgetting pain.

Forgetting whose pain.

He started making rules.

Never let the child choose under panic.

Never let Frieren sacrifice herself because of him.

Never let Bocchi decide while crying.

Never let someone leave because they think staying hurts Mau.

Never let someone stay because they think leaving hurts Mau.

Never let someone choose without understanding consequence.

Never let someone carry uncertainty alone.

The rules contradicted each other.

Mau wrote them mentally anyway.

The Trial kept disproving each one.

Someone made a fully informed choice and regretted it.

Someone chose under fear and later called it the best decision of their life.

Someone refused to decide and lost both options.

Someone asked Mau to choose for them.

He did.

They hated him later.

Another asked.

He refused.

They hated him later.

No general rule survived.

That should have freed him.

Instead it produced a darker conclusion:

If no rule guarantees freedom from regret, then maybe the only mercy is removing the burden itself.

The thought horrified him.

He rejected it.

Next loop.

It returned.

He rejected it again.

Next loop.

Again.

By the time the scenarios shifted toward others choosing Mau, the seed was already there.

Not:

I know better.

Not:

They need me to decide.

Something sadder.

I know what the moment of choosing can do to a person.

I have lived it hundreds of times.

If I can carry that moment instead, maybe they never have to.

Outside, nobody heard the thought.

They would only meet its consequences later.
