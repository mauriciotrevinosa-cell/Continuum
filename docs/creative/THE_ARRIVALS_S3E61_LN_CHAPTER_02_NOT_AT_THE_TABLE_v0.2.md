# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 19
## Chapter 29 — Not at the Table

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — REBUILD v0.2  
**Volume:** 19  
**Reading order:** 29  
**Continuity state:** Day 2 of ten-day window | Household keeps ordinary life without asking anyone's answer

On the second morning, Seiko made a rule.

She did not stand up to make it. She did not raise her voice. She sat at the head of the long table, where she had started sitting at some point during the year without anyone deciding she should, and put down her tea, and said:

"Nobody asks at this table."

The table went quiet.

Momo, halfway through passing bread, stopped.

"Grandma—"

"Not here." Seiko picked up her tea again. "You want to ask somebody something, you ask them in the hallway like an animal. Not where people eat."

Momo opened her mouth.

She closed it.

She passed the bread.

Down the table, people looked at each other. Then, one by one, they looked at their food.

Nobody asked.

It turned out to be the easiest breakfast anyone had eaten in two days.



Mau was at the far end, where Frieren had put him.

He had slept about an hour. Everyone knew. Nobody said so.

When Okarun dropped a spoon, Mau's whole body went rigid, and his eyes went to Frieren, and then to Ori, and then to Bocchi, counting, before they came back to the table.

Nobody said anything about that either.

Yuta slid a cup of tea across to him.

Mau looked at it.

His hand was shaking too much to lift it cleanly.

He lifted it anyway.

Yuta did not help him.

He just waited, the way you wait for someone to finish crossing a street, and when the cup was safely down again, he went back to his own breakfast.

"Yuta," Mau said, very low.

"Not at the table."

"I wasn't going to ask that."

Yuta looked at him.

"Okay," Mau admitted. "I was going to ask that."

"Not on the porch either," said Yuta. "Or the yard. Or the hallway, whatever Seiko says."

Mau almost laughed.

It came out as a breath instead.

It was still more than he had managed the day before.



Marin started measuring people at mid-morning.

She came out of the workroom with a tape and a notebook and a fierce, cheerful expression, and began, without explanation, to measure everyone she could catch.

Shoulders. Arms. Chest. Length from neck to wrist.

"What's it for?" asked Kita, holding her arms out obediently.

"Coats."

"Coats?"

"Winter coats." Marin wrote down a number. "It's going to get cold. Seiko said. She can feel it in her knees."

Seiko, passing, said, "Hm," which meant yes.

Karane, being measured next, opened her mouth.

Everyone near her could see the question forming. Why make winter coats now, with eight days—

She closed her mouth.

"Arms out," said Marin.

Karane put her arms out.

Hakari asked to be measured twice, to be sure.

Shizuka held up a page that said: SHE'S MEASURING. NOT ASKING.

Hakari looked at the page, and at Marin, and at Karane with her arms out, and her face did something soft, and she held out her own arms and did not say anything else.

Marin measured all of them. Every person who came into the common room that morning. She did not skip anyone. She did not ask anyone anything. She just wrote down numbers, one after another, in a notebook she had labeled on the cover in big, looping letters: WINTER.

Wakana sat at the workroom table behind her, cutting patterns from the measurements as fast as she could take them.

He did not ask either.

When Marin came to Mau, he stood still for her, and she measured his shoulders and frowned at the number.

"You're too thin," she said.

"Seiko says that."

"Seiko's right." Marin wrote it down. Then she looked up at him, and for a moment the cheerful expression slipped, and underneath it was something much quieter.

"It's going to get cold," she said again. "Everybody who's here should have a coat."

She did not say who would be here.

Mau did not ask.



Stark and Fern did the laundry.

It was their turn on the board. It had been their turn on that day of the week for months.

They hung the wet sheets in the yard without speaking. Then they took down the dry ones from the day before and began to fold them, the way two people fold a sheet: one at each end, pulling it taut between them, folding it in half, walking toward each other until their hands met in the middle, then stepping back and folding it again.

They did it eleven times.

They did not say a word.

On the twelfth, when their hands met in the middle, Stark did not let go right away.

Fern looked at him.

"Not at the table," she said.

"We're at the laundry line."

"Not at the laundry line either."

Stark nodded.

"Okay," he said.

He let go of the sheet.

They folded the rest.

When they were finished, Fern picked up the basket, and Stark took it out of her hands, the way he always did, and she let him, the way she always did, and they went in.

Himmel had been sitting on the porch the whole time, pretending to read.

He watched them go.

He did not say anything to anyone.

But he closed the book, after a while, and sat there for a long time looking at the empty laundry line.



Nazuna did not go to bed.

She should have. It was day. Day was for sleeping. Everyone knew that about Nazuna, the way they knew Umaru would be on the sofa and Milim would be loud.

But at noon, she was still awake, sitting on the common-room floor with her back against Anko's chair, yawning so hard her jaw cracked.

Anko looked down at her.

"Why are you awake?"

"Don't want to be asleep."

"You're a vampire. It's noon. You're going to fall over."

"Probably."

Anko waited.

Nazuna yawned again.

"Ten days," she said. "Well. Eight now. I slept through half of everything already this year. I slept through most of Mau's birthday. I slept through the whole Last Defense. I'm not sleeping through these too."

Anko looked at her for a long moment.

Then she reached down and, without comment, put her own jacket over Nazuna's shoulders.

Nazuna fell asleep against the chair leg about forty minutes later.

Anko did not wake her.

She did not move her chair either, for the entire afternoon, though she had three reports to write and the light was better by the window.



Okarun found Turbo Granny on the kitchen windowsill, glaring at the yard.

"Granny."

"What?"

"Are you—" He stopped. "Did you—"

"Ask me in the hallway, idiot."

"We're not at the table."

"I'm on a windowsill. Windowsill's part of the table."

"That's not—"

"I'm a ghost," said Turbo Granny. "You think a goddess gives ghosts personal terms?"

Okarun looked at her.

"Did you ask?"

There was a very long silence.

"Shut up," said Turbo Granny.

Okarun sat down on the floor under the windowsill.

After a while, Momo came and sat down beside him.

She did not ask him anything.

He did not ask her anything.

Seiko came past with a basket of onions, looked at the two of them sitting on the floor under a glaring ghost on a windowsill, and said, "Good. You're learning," and kept walking.

Momo leaned her head on Okarun's shoulder.

Turbo Granny looked down at them both.

"Disgusting," she said.

She did not leave the windowsill.



Mai made her bullet on the training ground, the way she did every morning.

She knelt. Closed her eyes. Paid for it.

When she stood up, her hand was shaking, and there was one small heavy thing in it that had not existed a moment before.

Maki was there, as she usually was.

Mai looked at the bullet for a while.

Then she held it out.

Maki looked at it.

"What's that for?"

"In case."

"In case what?"

Mai did not answer.

She just kept holding it out, until Maki took it.

Maki turned it over in her fingers.

"Mai."

"Not on the training ground," said Mai.

Maki looked at her sister for a long time.

Then she put the bullet in her pocket, and picked up her staff, and said, "Again?" and Mai picked up hers, and they trained until neither of them could lift their arms, without one word between them that was not about footwork.



Kessoku played in the evening.

Nobody arranged it.

Bocchi started tuning in the corner of the common room after dinner, and Nijika drifted over, and Ryo was already lying on the floor nearby, and Kita came and sat down so close to Bocchi that their knees touched.

They played quietly.

People stayed.

Mau sat against the far wall with Frieren on one side and Ori on the other.

He watched Bocchi play.

He watched her fingers on the strings, and the way she bent her head, and the way Kita's hand, without looking, found her knee between songs.

And then, without his permission, the other room arrived.

The Trial room.

The one where she had—

His hands went cold.

He did not move.

Across the room, Bocchi looked up.

She did not stop playing.

She just changed what she was playing.

Three notes.

Soft.

Again.

Mau breathed in.

Three notes.

Again.

The room went back to being the common room.

Bocchi alive in the corner. Kita's hand on her knee. Frieren's shoulder against his. Ori's head tipping slowly sideways onto his arm as she fell asleep.

Bocchi went back to the song she had been playing.

She did not look at him again.

She did not need to.



Coco found him on the way to bed.

She had a bucket.

"It's your turn," she said.

Mau looked at the bucket.

"I don't have a turn."

"It says everyone," said Coco. "You're everyone."

She pushed the bucket into his hands.

They went out together in the dark to the garden circle, where the sapling had grown past her knee, and the board hung on Wakana's stake.

WHO WATERS IT

Everyone.

Dessa.

Pim.

And the third name, in Coco's careful hand.

Mau poured the water slowly.

He watched it sink into the dark earth around the thin stubborn trunk.

His hands were still shaking.

The water went where it needed to anyway.

"Coco," he said.

"Not at the garden," said Coco.

Mau looked at her.

She was not smiling.

She was not crying either.

She was just standing there, in the dark, beside a tree that would take a very long time to grow, holding the empty bucket.

"Okay," said Mau.

They stood there a while longer.

Then they went in.

It was day two.

It counted.
