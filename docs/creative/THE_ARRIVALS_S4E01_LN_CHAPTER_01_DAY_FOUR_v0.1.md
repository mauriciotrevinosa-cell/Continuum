On the morning of the fourth day, Mau woke with a ribbon around his ankle.

It was Frieren's. Blue, a little frayed at one end, the one she sometimes wore in her hair and more often lost in the bedclothes. One end was tied in a neat, practical knot around his ankle and the other around the bedpost, and there was about an arm's length of slack between them. He looked at it for a while before he remembered why.

He had floated in his sleep. Not far. A finger's width, then a hand's, the first night after the road. He did not remember it. Frieren had told him afterward, in two sentences, the way she told him facts she did not want to discuss: *You were drifting. I tied you down.* Like a boat at a mooring. Like the old inn, a long time ago, when it had been a joke.

It was not a joke now. Nobody had untied it.

Frieren was not in the bed. She was in her reading chair, which had been pulled across to the bedside again. Mau tried not to think about the last time that chair had been there. She had a book open on her knees and was not reading it. She was looking at the window.

"Morning," said Mau.

Frieren turned a page she had not read.

"Morning," she said.

That was all.



It had been like that since the road.

Not silence, exactly. Silence would have been easier to understand. People spoke to him. They told him when breakfast was. They told him which rules were on the board. They told him that Maomao wanted to check his pulse, and that Yuta would walk him to the washroom, and that the ribbon could come off when Frieren said so. They told him things. They did not talk to him.

He understood why. He had gone out of this house before dawn with three drafts destroyed and a fourth left folded on the table for them to find, and walked south toward a clearing to ask a Goddess for something that would have changed every one of their lives. He had not asked any of them. They had caught him on the road. He had told them some of it there, shouting and crying in the half-dark, and not the whole of anything. Then he had slept for most of a day with a ribbon on his ankle, and woken, and signed a page in Fern's ledger. It said he would not go to the Goddess until he had told them what happened, and they had talked about it.

*Not tonight*, he had said.

That had been the night of the third day.

Now it was the fourth.



Breakfast was in the common room, as it always was. It was not as it always was.

The board had his rules on it, in four different hands, under the rings and the fort's proclamation and Marin's old decree from the Ministry of Not Working:

NO RESIDUAL TRIAL / GODDESS CONTACT SITE ALONE.
NO CONTRACT AFFECTING ABSENT PEOPLE.
NO SECRET COLLECTIVE TERMS.

Under them, in Yuta's hand, larger than the rest:

SLEEP.

Mau sat where he was put. Momo put a bowl in front of him harder than she needed to, so that some of the soup jumped the rim. She did not apologize. She did not look at him. She went back to the kitchen, and he heard a pot go down on the stove like a sentence ending.

He ate.

Around him, the house talked. About the water court, and the second community's road report, and whether the first snow would come before the end of the month. About anything. They talked to each other across him and over him, warmly, the way people talk at a table, and every word of it went around him the way a stream goes around a stone.

Milim was at the far end of the long table with her back to him. She had been sitting like that since the road. When someone asked her to pass the bread, she passed it. When Mau asked, she passed it too. But she passed it without turning around, reaching back over her own shoulder, and set it down a foot from his hand.

Ori was not at the table at all.

He looked for her. He did not find her. Fern, who saw him looking, said without lifting her eyes from the ledger:

"She ate early."

It was the first thing anyone had said to him that was not a fact about a rule. It was also a fact about a rule. Ori had made it. She did not eat where he could see her.



After breakfast, Yuta walked him to the washroom.

Yuta did not say anything on the way there. He did not say anything while Mau washed his face. On the way back, at the turn of the family corridor, by the narrow room with REM on the door in pencil and *(SISTER)* under it, Yuta stopped.

Mau stopped too.

"I'm not going to yell at you," said Yuta. "Maki already did. Frieren already did." He looked at the side of Mau's face, at the place where the slap had landed on the road, which had not left a mark, though Mau could still feel it if he thought about it. "Everyone's already done everything."

"I know."

"You don't," said Yuta. Very quietly. "That's the problem. You went alone. Do you understand what that looks like from here? From me?"

Mau understood. He had understood on the road, the moment he saw Yuta's face. In Yuta's world there had been a man who stood in front of everyone, alone, because he was the strongest. Everyone had let him. Yuta had let him. That was what Yuta's face had said on the road, and what it was saying now in the corridor, without any of the words.

"I'm sorry," said Mau.

"I know you are." Yuta started walking again. Over his shoulder: "It doesn't help yet. It will. Not yet."



The ribbon came off in the afternoon. Frieren untied it without a word, coiled it, and put it in her pocket, not in her hair.

Mau was allowed outside, with an escort. That was a rule too, one of Maomao's, carried over from the council: he had to be seen. A man in his shadow with a summon he was still training. Ordinary. Washing shirts. Eating in the plaza. Not a monster behind a door.

He went to the washing court with Mikasa. Mikasa did not speak at all, which from Mikasa was not anger, only Mikasa. But she walked half a step closer to him than she used to. He noticed she kept her body between him and the road south.

The plaza was full.

It was full the way it had been full every day since the Goddess opened her hand over the settlement and said *Return*, and *Stay*, and *ten days*. Arrival House had a line outside it that went around the corner and down past the new stones. People were waiting to go in and ask for their terms: privately, with a witness, with the doors shut, the way Jinshi and Fern had set it up. People were waiting to come out. People who had come out were standing in small groups along the edge of the plaza, talking very low. When a door opened, they all looked.

Mau knew some of the faces. Not most. The settlement was bigger than the house; it always had been. Refuge families. Second-wave travelers. People who had come over the bridge in the weeks after Last Defense because they had heard there was a place that would take anyone. People who had heard, since the plaza, that the same place had a monster in it.

The Goddess had offered every one of them a way home.

He felt the plaza notice him.

It was not like the washing court on his first day out, weeks ago, when the noise had changed and then, slowly, gone back. This time it changed and stayed changed. Heads turned and did not turn back. A woman in the Arrival House line took her child's hand. A man by the water point said something to the man beside him, and the second man laughed, not kindly.

Mikasa moved half a step closer.

Mau washed his shirt.

It was the only thing he could think of to do.



That evening, Rimuru found him on the step outside the front door.

It was cold. The first real cold of the year, the kind that gets into the boards. Somewhere behind them Marin was hanging winter coats by the door one at a time, unclaimed, each with a name pinned to it in Wakana's hand. There was one for everybody, she said, whether they liked it or not.

Rimuru sat down beside him. He did not say anything for a long time.

"Three days," said Rimuru finally.

"I know."

"I'm not asking. I'm counting." Rimuru looked out at the dark plaza, at the lamps along the bridge and the shape of Arrival House with one window still lit. "Ciel won't tell me."

Mau said nothing.

"She says you made her promise. She says she promised. She says, and I'm quoting, *not forever*." Rimuru's mouth twisted. "I've never had her keep something from me before. Not once. Not in two worlds."

"I'm sorry."

"Everyone keeps telling me you're sorry," said Rimuru. "I believe it. I don't need it." He breathed out, and the breath went white. "I need you to tell us. Not tonight. You said not tonight, and you meant it, and that's fine. But you signed it. And the clock on that page isn't the Goddess's clock, it's ours, and ours runs out too."

Mau looked at his hands.

"Seven days," he said.

"Six," said Rimuru. "Ciel's counting too."

He stood up. He put his hand on Mau's head on the way past, very briefly, the way he had a long time ago, when Mau had been someone who did not know his own name.

Then he went inside, and the door closed, and Mau sat on the step in the cold. Somewhere behind the wall, very deep down, something counted with him.
