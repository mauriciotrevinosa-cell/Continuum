# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 2 — Not Your Choice

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 2  
**Continuity state:** Day 5, morning | Ring Three asks for him at the old inn | the cooper: 'I'd go back. Maybe I will. Either way, it's mine.' | the teacher's bucket | the witches: 'For some of us, Return is a grave' | Rem: Lise

Ring Three asked for him on the morning of the fifth day.

They did it properly, which was somehow worse. A message came to the house through Rem, from the old inn, in a careful hand Mau did not recognize. It said that some of the people of the Third Ring would like to speak with him, at the old inn, at midmorning, if he would come. It said that he could bring whoever he liked. It said *please*.

"You don't have to go," said Fern, reading it over his shoulder.

"I know."

"That isn't the same as *I'll go*."

"I'll go," said Mau.

Fern looked at him for a long moment. Then she wrote it in the ledger, with the time, and the place, and under it, *Escort: Rimuru, Anko*, and showed him, so that he would know it was written.



The old inn had not changed. That was the first thing that hurt.

The same door that stuck. The same creak two steps inside. The long room where the household had first eaten together, a lifetime ago, before there was a house or a settlement or anything to protect. Rem had pushed the tables back against the walls and set chairs in a loose half-circle in the middle, the way she set them for travelers who needed to talk and did not know yet how.

There were eleven people in the chairs.

Mau knew all of them, a little. That was what Ring Three meant. Not family, not close, but known. Each of them had been vouched for by someone in the house and approved by the first two rings together, at the council, in chalk.

A cooper from the river towns, broad and quiet, whom Wakana had vouched for because he had shown her a better way to steam a plank. A woman who ran the second-community bridge crossing and whom Mikasa trusted with routes. Two of the refuge's cooks, who had fed half the settlement during Last Defense with Momo, and whom Momo had vouched for with a single word: *family*. A young man from the Searchers' second team. A girl of perhaps sixteen who helped Rem with the travelers' rooms and had come over the bridge alone in the summer. An old man who taught the refuge children their letters and whom Qifrey had vouched for. Two more Mau knew only by face.

And at the end of the half-circle, in the doorway, not quite in the room, the witches.

Not all of them. Two. The first witch, the one who spoke for the others, and one other. They stood under the agreement, as they always did, and watched. They did not sit.

Rimuru took the chair at Mau's left. Anko took the one at his right and opened her notebook and did not write anything in it.

Nobody spoke for a while.



"We heard," said the cooper finally.

His voice was very even. He was the kind of man whose voice was always even, the kind who had built barrels for forty years and knew that most things were held together by pressure applied slowly.

"We heard what you tried to do. On the road. Before dawn." He looked at his hands. "Everybody's heard. The whole settlement. Some of it right. Most of it wrong. So we wanted to hear it from you."

Mau looked at the eleven faces.

"I was going to ask the Goddess," he said, "whether there was a way for everyone to Return. All together. So nobody would have to choose alone."

The silence that followed was different from the silence before.

"Everyone," said the woman from the bridge crossing.

"Everyone who wanted—" Mau began, and stopped, because it was not true. That was not what the fourth draft had said. The fourth draft had said *collective*. It had said *remove the burden*. It had not said *who wanted*.

He did not finish the sentence.

The woman from the bridge crossing nodded slowly, as if he had.



"I'd go back," said the cooper.

He said it to the floor, very quietly.

"I want you to know that. I have a daughter. She was nine when I came through. She'll be — I don't know what she'll be." He looked up. "The first day the Goddess said *Return*, I went into Arrival House and asked for my terms. I've read them six times. I haven't decided. Maybe I'll go. Maybe I won't. I haven't told the house any of this. I'm telling you."

Mau did not move.

"So you'd think I'd thank you," said the cooper. "For trying to make it easy. For trying to get all of us home together, so I wouldn't have to stand at that door alone." His voice did not change at all. "But it wasn't your choice to make. You hear me, son? I'd go back. Maybe I will. Either way, it's mine. It was never yours."

"I know," said Mau.

"No," said the cooper. "You don't. Not yet."



They went around the half-circle after that, not in order, the way people talk when something has been held too long.

The refuge cook had buried her husband in her own world, before she came through. She asked Mau, without anger, whether he had thought about where Return would put her. Next to a grave? In a house that had been sold? In a year that had kept going without her? She had read her terms too. She was staying.

"I'm staying because I chose to," she said. "You'd have taken that from me. You'd have taken the choosing."

The young man from the Searchers' second team was angry, openly and badly, the way young men are. He said that three of their people had died at Last Defense, three, and one of them had gone back into the north lane for a family that was not even his, and their names were written in charcoal on the board by the ink tree, where Coco rewrote them every time the rain wore them away. He said that Mau, of all people, the person the whole outside world called the leader of this place, had decided to fix it by sending everyone away. As if the problem was that people were *here*.

That one went in deep. Mau did not say anything. He thought about the three names in charcoal. He thought about the night after Last Defense, and the thing that had turned in him, quietly, without his permission, and kept turning: *why weren't you in front?* That turning had carried him through the Trial, and out of the Trial, and down the road south in the dark.

He had never told anyone that.

He did not tell them now.

The girl who helped Rem at the inn did not say anything at all. She sat in her chair with her hands knotted in her lap and looked at him, and when it was her turn she shook her head, once, and looked at the floor. Rem, standing by the kitchen door, moved closer to her and stayed there.

The old teacher said the thing Mau would remember longest.

"I've taught children for fifty years," he said. "In two worlds now. And every year, without fail, there is one child who decides that the way to keep the others safe is to take every hard thing onto himself. To do the whole lesson alone. To carry the bucket for everybody." He looked at Mau over his spectacles, kindly. "It's never the weakest child. It's always the strongest. And it is always, always, the one who needs the most looking after. Because nobody thinks to look after the one carrying the bucket."

Rimuru, at Mau's left, went very still.



The witches spoke last.

The first witch had not moved from the doorway. She spoke in the same quiet, precise voice she used at their sessions every second week, when she asked about his seams.

"You would have sent us back too," she said.

Mau looked at her.

"I didn't—" He stopped. "I didn't think about you."

"No," said the first witch. "You didn't." She tilted her head very slightly. "Do you know where *back* is, for some of us?"

He did not.

"For some of us," said the first witch, "Return is a grave. Not a home, not a year that kept going. A grave. Ours." She said it without any emphasis at all, the way she might have told him the weather. "We came here from the end of something. We found a forest, and a cabin, and a boy whose seams shone, and an hour every second week in a room with a wobbly chair. And you would have asked a Goddess to send us all home. Together. For our own good."

The witch beside her said nothing. But she was looking at the long room of the old inn, at the tables pushed back against the walls and the warm stove and Rem's cups on their shelf, with an expression Mau had never seen on her before. He could not name it. Later he would think it might have been envy.

"We are not angry," said the first witch. "We are not anything so simple." She inclined her head to him, very slightly, exactly as she did at the end of every session. "But it was not your choice."

Then the two of them turned and went out into the morning, and the door that stuck did not stick for them at all.



Afterward, when the eleven had gone, Mau sat in the empty half-circle of chairs for a long time.

Rimuru did not say anything. Anko closed her notebook. She had not written a single word.

Rem came out of the kitchen with three cups of tea on a tray and put them down on the nearest table, and then, instead of going back, sat down in one of the empty chairs across from him.

"The girl who didn't speak," said Rem quietly. "Her name is Lise. She came over the bridge alone in the summer. She asked for her terms on the first day, too." Rem paused. "She doesn't want to go back. She wanted someone to *ask* her whether she wanted to go back. Nobody ever had, in her whole life. And then she heard that you were going to ask the Goddess to decide it for everyone. So that nobody would have to be asked."

Mau closed his eyes.

"She isn't angry," said Rem. "She's frightened. She thought, for one morning, that she was going to be sent somewhere again without anybody asking."

She picked up her own cup and held it in both hands.

"You can be sorry," said Rem. "You should be. But you should be sorry to *her*. Not to the house. The house will forgive you. That isn't the hard part." She looked at him over the rim of the cup. "The hard part is that there are people out there who are not your family, and who you would have decided for anyway. And you didn't know their names."

Mau did not say anything for a long time.

"Lise," he said finally.

"Lise," said Rem.

He drank the tea. It had gone cold.
