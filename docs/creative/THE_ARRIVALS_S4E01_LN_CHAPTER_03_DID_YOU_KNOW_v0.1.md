# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 3 — Did You Know

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 3  
**Continuity state:** Day 5, afternoon and evening | Ori's stone is gone from the windowsill | Bocchi: 'You're my brother... do you know the difference?' | the President's question at Ori's window | Frieren: 'You'll have to walk there yourself'

When Mau came back from the old inn, the stone was gone from his windowsill.

It had been there for weeks. A small smooth stone from the tidepools, grey with a white band through it, that Ori had sent in the fort's first basket when he was in bed after the bay. No note, only the stone. He had kept it on the windowsill where he could see it from the bed, and he had not moved it once. Not when he got up. Not when he was allowed outside. Not on the night he went out of this room before dawn with a note in his pocket.

Now there was a small clean square in the dust where it had been.

He stood looking at the square for a long time.

He did not go and find Ori. He knew where she was. She was in the fort, by the window in the south wall that she had cut herself, where you could see down the corridor to this door. He knew she was there because he could feel her not looking at him through two walls.

He did not go. He did not know what he would say. *Give it back* was not a thing he had the right to say. *I'm sorry* was not a thing she wanted.

He sat down on the bed and looked at the square instead.



In the afternoon, Bocchi stopped playing.

Mau was crossing the end of the common room, with Mikasa a step behind, toward the washing court and the rule that said he had to be seen. He passed the music room door. It was half open, as it always was, because Bocchi liked to be heard without being seen, and the sound of her guitar came out of it into the corridor like water from a spring. It had been playing all day. It had been playing all week, something slow and circling that did not seem to have an end.

It stopped as he passed.

He stopped too.

The door opened a little further, and Bocchi stood in it with her guitar still on its strap, her hair over her face, not looking at him. Her hands were shaking. Mikasa, very quietly, took two steps back down the corridor and turned to look at something on the wall.

"M-Mau," said Bocchi.

"Hey."

Bocchi did not say anything for so long that he thought she was not going to. Her fingers were knotted in the guitar strap. He could see her trying, the way he had seen her try a hundred times, at the edge of a stage or a crowded table, to make a sentence come out of her that wanted very badly to stay in.

"D-did you," said Bocchi. "Did you think. We wouldn't. Miss you."

Mau did not understand.

"If we all went home," said Bocchi, to the floor. "All of us. Together. Like you wanted. And everything here was a dream we half-remembered." She swallowed. "Did you think we wouldn't *miss* you? Or did you think it didn't matter, if we did?"

He opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

"You're my brother," said Bocchi.

She had never said it before. Not out loud, not to his face. Everyone in the house knew it, the way they knew the weather. She had never said it.

"Not because I need you," said Bocchi. Her voice cracked and kept going anyway. "I mean. I do. I do need you. But that's not why. That was never why." Her hands twisted the strap so hard her knuckles went white. "Do you know that? That we— that we *love* you? Not just need you? Do you know the difference?"

Mau looked at her.

He wanted to say *yes*. He wanted to say it the way he said everything else that they needed to hear, quickly and kindly, so that the moment would pass and they would be all right. It was right there, in his mouth.

It would not come out.

Bocchi looked up, for the first time, through her hair. She saw his face. Whatever she saw there made her own face crumple, all at once, like a child's.

"You don't," she said. "You don't know."

She went back into the music room and shut the door, the whole way, which she never did.

A moment later the guitar started again, the same slow circling thing, not quite steady.

Mikasa came back down the corridor and stood beside him. She did not say anything. She put her hand on the back of his neck, briefly, the way she would have steadied someone on a wall, and then took it away.

They went to the washing court.



In the evening, the President of the Fort summoned him.

The summons came through Suika, who delivered it in person, at the door of his room, in her most official voice: the President requested the presence of the Idiot Who Accepted at the south window of the fort, at once, and he was not to come in.

So he went to the south window.

It was the small square window Ori had cut, at the height of someone sitting on a cushion. He had to crouch to look through it. Umaru was on the other side, sitting very straight on her cushion in her hamster hood and her PRESIDENT (TEMPORARY) sash, with her hands folded on her knees. Beside her, Agott, in OPPOSITION. Behind them, in the soft green light of Kusuri's jars, the fort was full and very quiet.

Ori was not at the window. Mau could see the shape of her farther back, sitting against the north wall with her knees up.

"The President has a question," said Umaru.

"Okay."

"It's not a rule," said Umaru. "It's a question. You have to actually answer it." She looked at him through the square of the window. "Did you think we'd be happier without you choosing for us? Or did you think we'd be happier without *you*?"

The fort was completely silent.

Mau crouched at the window and looked at Umaru, and did not answer for a long time.

"I don't know," he said.

Umaru's face did not change.

"That's the wrong answer," she said.

"I know."

"You promised," said Umaru. Her voice stayed perfectly steady, presidential. Only her hands moved, tightening on her knees. "At the ceremony. In front of everybody. Seiko read it, and then I read the last one myself. *If it gets too heavy, you'll put it down, and let us carry it for a while.* And you said *I'll try*, and I said that's not one of the answers, and you said *yes*. You said *I promise*."

"I remember."

"Then *why*," said Umaru, and her voice broke for the first time, "didn't you put it down?"

Agott, beside her, without looking, put a hand on her shoulder.

Mau did not have an answer for that either.

He crouched there at the small square window for a long time. Nobody in the fort said anything. Then Umaru, with great dignity, wiped her face with the end of her sash, and said:

"The President has no further questions."

And Mau stood up and went back down the corridor to the room with the east window. He knew that every person in the fort was watching him go through Ori's window, the whole way.



Frieren was in the reading chair when he came in. She had a book open and was not reading it.

He sat down on the end of the bed. He looked at the empty square on the windowsill.

"Bocchi asked me if I knew they loved me," he said. "Not needed. Loved."

Frieren turned a page.

"And Umaru asked whether I thought they'd be happier without me."

"What did you say?"

"I said I didn't know."

Frieren closed the book.

She looked at him for a long time, across the room, in the lamplight. She did not come closer. She had not come closer, not really, since the road. Not since her hand had gone toward his and stopped, and he had seen it stop.

"Tonight," said Frieren, "they're going to ask the rest."

"I know."

"Rimuru's going to ask about the number."

He looked up.

"I don't know what it is," said Frieren. "Ciel won't tell him. You won't tell anyone. But I know there's a number, because I know your face when you think about it. You think about it all the time." She paused. "You signed Fern's page. You said *not tonight*. That was two nights ago."

"I know."

"So tonight," said Frieren, "you're going to have to choose. Whether to keep it, or to tell us."

She stood up, put the book down on the chair, and went to the door.

"They're waiting," she said. "In the common room. All of them. I'm going now. You can come, or not." At the door she stopped, with her hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "If you don't come, I won't come back in here to get you. That's the only thing I'm going to decide for you. That you'll have to walk there yourself."

She went out.

Mau sat on the end of the bed and looked at the square in the dust where a stone had been.

Then he got up and walked there himself.
