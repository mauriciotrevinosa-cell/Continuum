# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 5 — Rule Five

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 5  
**Continuity state:** Night of Day 5 | 'Mau is told everything' only goes one way | Ciel: under five hundred; 'press' | Senku's minimum: 112 | '437' / Ciel: 'Correct.' | 'Four hundred and thirty-seven times, I died.'

Mau did not answer the question.

Not yet. He sat on the couch beside Frieren, with every face in the common room turned toward him for the first time all evening, and looked down the long table at Fern.

"Fern," he said. "Rule five. Can you read it?"

Fern looked at him for a moment. Then she turned back through the ledger, a long way, past the rings and the council and the storm and the move, to a page near the front. It had been copied out on the night after the containment, when the house had sat in this room and written the guardrails by lamplight while he was still somewhere inside Rimuru, held.

She found it. She read it aloud in her clear, even voice.

"*Five. Mau is told everything.*" She paused. "*Frieren's rule.*"

"Mau is told everything," said Mau.

He looked at Frieren. Frieren did not look away.

"That was the rule," said Mau. "You wrote it so that nobody would ever keep anything about me from me again. Not Ciel, not Rimuru, not anyone. Because they had. Because in the containment, things happened to me and I didn't know about them, and you said never again." He swallowed. "And it worked. It's why Ciel told me the number. The night after the Trial. She said it the way she says everything I'm owed. *Rule five.*"

Nobody said anything.

"It only goes one way," said Mau. "Doesn't it."

Frieren's face did not change. But her hand, on her own knee, closed slowly.



He turned to Rimuru.

"What did Ciel tell you," said Mau. "About the number. What did she say it was?"

Rimuru was still standing by the cold hearth. He looked at Mau for a long moment, as if deciding whether this was a way of not answering.

"She said it was the number of attempts," said Rimuru. "That's all. That you made a number of attempts in the Trial, and she counted them, and you asked her not to tell me how many. She told me that much because I asked, and she said rule eight was on my side." His jaw tightened. "She hasn't told me the number. She told me what it counts."

"Attempts," said Mau.

"Attempts."

Mau nodded slowly.

"Yes," he said. "I tried. Many times." He looked at his hands. "Thousands of ways. Thousands of shapes. Thousands of days."

Rimuru went very still.

His eyes went somewhere else for a moment, the way they did when Ciel was speaking to him, and the whole room watched his face change.

"Ciel says," said Rimuru slowly, "that the number she recorded doesn't pass five hundred."

Mau said nothing.

"Not thousands," said Rimuru. His voice was rising. "Under five hundred. She counted every one. She never missed one. She says she never missed one." He took a step forward. "So which is it? Thousands, or under five hundred? Are you lying to me now? Is that what this is? You made her keep a secret from me for days, and now that I ask you to your face, you give me a number she says is wrong?"

"Rimuru," said Yuta quietly.

"No." Rimuru did not look away from Mau. "No. I've waited. She's waited. I want to know what he's hiding, and I want to know why he made the one person in two worlds who has never kept anything from me keep *this*."

Mau looked up at him.

"Ciel doesn't know," he said. "That's all. She doesn't know that the number she has is something else."

Rimuru stopped.

His eyes went somewhere else again. This time it took longer.

"She says," said Rimuru, and his voice had changed, "*press*."

The room was completely silent.

"She says press," said Rimuru. "She says she has been wondering for days what she counted. She says she counts things, it is what she is, and she has never in her existence counted something without knowing what it was." He breathed in. "So I'm pressing. If it isn't attempts, then what is it? Why did you make Ciel hide it?" His voice dropped, very low. "Because it looks like you don't want anyone to know what that number is, Mau. Not me. Not anybody. Not even her."



At the north end of the room, beside the curtained door of the fort, Senku stirred.

"I counted," he said.

Everyone turned.

Senku was sitting with his back against the fort's frame and his notebook on his knees, as he always was. He was not looking at the notebook.

"During the Trial," said Senku. "Outside. The domain was showing us what was happening in there. Not all of it. Fragments. I counted the ones I could see." He turned a page, and then did not read it. "After a while it stopped showing us everything, so I couldn't count properly anymore. I kept a minimum. And I stopped saying it out loud, because Rimuru asked me what it was, and I told him, and I watched his face, and it wasn't helping anyone."

"What was the minimum?" said Fern.

Senku did not look up.

"A hundred and twelve," he said. "That I saw. That I could count. Before it stopped showing us." He closed the notebook. "I don't know what I was counting either. Not exactly. I only knew that each time the picture changed, something had ended."

Nobody spoke.

Mau looked at Senku for a long moment. Senku, who measured everything. Who had counted every one he could see, alone, outside, while everyone else watched, and then stopped saying the number because of what it did to people's faces.

Then Mau looked back down the table at the ledger, still open in Fern's hands to the page near the front, at the line in Frieren's rule.

*Mau is told everything.*

"Four hundred and thirty-seven," said Mau.



The words went into the room and stayed there.

In two heads, and only two, very quietly, a voice said:

*Correct.*

Rimuru let out a breath. Something in his shoulders came down. It was not relief, exactly. It was the end of not knowing, which is not the same thing, and sometimes feels like it for a moment.

"Four hundred and thirty-seven," he said. "That's the number she has. That's what she counted." He rubbed his face. "Okay. Okay. Four hundred and thirty-seven attempts."

"She thought they were attempts," said Mau.

He was looking at his hands again.

"The number's wrong," he said. Then he stopped. "No. I think it's right. I think the number's right. I lost count after two hundred. I stopped being able to count anything, after a while." He was quiet. "She's right about the number. She's wrong about what it counts."

"Then what does it count?" said Rimuru.

Mau did not answer.

He looked up at Rimuru instead, and when he spoke again his voice was very calm, the way a voice goes calm when there is nothing left underneath it to shake.

"Do you know what it feels like," said Mau, "to have every part of you cut? Destroyed. Vaporized. Or tortured, for days. If not for centuries." He paused. "I don't remember how long I was there. Not really. I know how long it was for you. You were standing in the plaza. You saw the sun move. But in there—" He stopped. "In there I don't know."

Nobody moved.

Four hundred and thirty-seven. They did not know yet what it meant. But they had all begun, in the same moment, to understand that it was something else. Something that was not an attempt. Something that was not a number Ciel could count without seeing.

Frieren, beside him, had gone so still that she did not seem to be breathing.

The silence went on for a long time. It was the dense kind, the kind that has weight, the kind that a room holds the way you hold your breath under water.

And then Mau said it.

"Four hundred and thirty-seven times," he said, "I died."
