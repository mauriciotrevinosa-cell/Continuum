# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 6 — Every Time

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 6  
**Continuity state:** Same night | 'You said sometimes.' / 'I lied. It was every time.' | in front, they lived; behind, he watched | 'The only way they live is if they go back' | he pulls away from Frieren's hand

Nobody said anything.

That was the thing he would remember afterward, out of all of it. Not one person in that room said anything at all. Not Milim, who always said something. Not Momo, who would have said something sharp, to make it smaller. Not Fern, not Yuta, not Rimuru, still standing by the cold hearth with his hand halfway to his face. The fort, clustered at the north end of the room in front of its curtain, did not make a sound.

The lamps burned. Somewhere in the kitchen a pot ticked as it cooled.

Frieren spoke first.

Her voice was very quiet and perfectly steady, and he knew from the steadiness how much it was costing her.

"On the road," she said, "you said *sometimes*."

Mau looked at the floor.

"You said, *sometimes I died and came back*." She did not raise her voice. "Sometimes. That was the word. I heard it. Everyone heard it. We stood on that road and heard you say *sometimes*, and we thought, *that's terrible*, and we thought, *sometimes*."

"I lied," said Mau.

Frieren's breath went out of her.

"It wasn't sometimes," said Mau. "It was every time." He made himself look up. "Ciel counted every time it started again. It only started again when I died. That was the rule in there. You could fail, and fail, and lose everyone, and it kept going. It only went back to the beginning when I was dead." He swallowed. "So she counted my deaths. She didn't know. She couldn't see. She felt it start again, four hundred and thirty-seven times, and she counted."

In two heads in that room, a voice said nothing at all. It was the loudest nothing Rimuru had ever heard from her.



"Some of them," said Mau, "were quick."

He did not know why he was telling them this part. Only that he had started, and that if he stopped now he would never start again, and that Fern's page said *until he tells us*. He had signed it.

"Some of them were very quick. Something came, and I didn't see it, and I was back at the start. I stopped being afraid of those. I stopped being afraid of the first second." He looked at the long table. "Some of them weren't quick."

Maki, on the bench under the window, closed her eyes.

"In some of them," said Mau, "they killed everyone. Whatever was in that one. Whatever the Trial built. It killed all of you, and then it killed me, and it started again." His voice did not change. "In some of them, I put myself in front. I got between you and whatever it was. I let it have me. And you lived."

He stopped.

"And in some of them," he said, "I let you stand in front of me."

Yuta's hand, on the bench beside Maki's, went very still.

"Because you wanted to," said Mau. "Because you asked. Because I'd learned, by then, that if I always stood in front, I was deciding for you, and you hated it, and I thought maybe that was the test. Maybe the test was to let you choose." He was quiet for a moment. "So I let you. And I watched you die. One at a time. One by one. Every one of you, at least once."

Bocchi made a small sound and put both hands over her mouth.

"Every time I was in front," said Mau, "you lived. Every time I let you be in front, I watched you die." He looked at his hands. "That was the rule. As far as I could ever find one. I tried to break it. I tried *everything*." His voice cracked for the first time. "Every single thing I could think of. Every way I could think of to have both. Both of us. All of us. I never found one. Not once, in four hundred and thirty-seven."

He did not look at Frieren when he said *both of us*. She heard it anyway. Nobody else in the room knew what those two words were, or where he had said them before: in a bed, in the morning, with a dressing across the middle of him. Only she did.

He had just said them again.



"I never knew why," said Mau.

He was talking faster now, the way a man walks faster downhill, because stopping is harder than going on.

"I never knew what it was for. Who it was for. The Goddess never said. There was no reason. There was just the white, and then a scenario, and then everyone I loved in it, and then something that wanted them dead, and me." He breathed. "And every time. *Every* time. Every day, in every one of them, somewhere, there was the same sentence. Not a voice. Not words, exactly. Just the shape of it, under everything. The way you know a thing in a dream without being told."

"What sentence?" said Anko quietly.

Mau looked at her.

"*The only way they live,*" he said, "*is if they go back.*"

The room went very cold.

"I ignored it," said Mau. "The whole time. Four hundred and thirty-seven times. I told myself it was false. Like the memories. Like everything in the memory crisis, all those things that felt real and weren't. I told myself, *that's the Trial talking. That's the thing it wants you to believe. Don't believe it.*" He shook his head slowly. "And I didn't. I didn't believe it. Not in there. Not once."

He stopped.

"And then I came out," he said, "and the Goddess opened her hand, and said *Return*. And it was *there*. Real. In the sky. The way home." He looked down at the floor. "And four hundred and thirty-seven times of not believing something is a very long time to hold on to a door, when someone finally opens it."



Frieren reached for his hand.

She did it the way she had done it a thousand times, without thinking, the way you reach for a railing on a stair in the dark: her hand moving across the space on the couch between them, palm up, to find his.

He pulled away.

It was not a choice. He saw her hand coming, a woman's hand, her hand, coming toward him across a small space, and his whole body moved before he did. His arm jerked back. His shoulder hit the arm of the couch. He was on his feet without having decided to stand.

Frieren's hand stayed where it was, open, in the empty air above the cushion.

Everyone saw.

"I'm sorry," said Mau. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I didn't—"

But his breath had already gone wrong.
