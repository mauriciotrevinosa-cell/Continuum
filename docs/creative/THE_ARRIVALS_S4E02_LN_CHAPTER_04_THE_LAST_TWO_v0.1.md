# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 8 — The Last Two

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 8  
**Continuity state:** Same night | the inn loop: 'I think I beat you'; Eren: 'I know that one' | the white room, ONE LEAVES | the wedding told, and the false Frieren's last words repeated | 'Then I heard a voice. Yours.' | Anko: like Sukuna; Yuta: a third way | 'I'm sorry'

He did not go back to the couch. He sat on the floor at the end of it with his back against the arm, where Maki had brought him back, with his knees up. Nobody asked him to move. Frieren did not move either. She sat on the couch above him with her open hand resting on her own chest and her eyes on the back of his head.

"There are two more," said Mau. "The last two. I should tell you those."

"You don't have to tonight," said Fern.

"I signed it."

"Not all of it in one night."

"If I stop," said Mau, "I won't start again."

Fern looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded and left the ledger open on the table in front of her.



"Near the end," said Mau, "I tried something different."

He was looking at the floor between his feet.

"I'd tried everything with you there. Everything I could think of. Standing in front. Not standing in front. Asking. Not asking. Running. Staying. And it always ended the same way. So I thought—" He stopped. "I thought maybe the problem was me. Maybe you died because I was there. Maybe the thing the Trial wanted was for me to go."

Nobody said anything.

"So I left," said Mau. "In that one. I went away by myself, to look for a way. Any way. A door, a rule, something I hadn't tried. I don't know how long I was gone. Long. It felt like years. I don't know." He swallowed. "And then I came back. To the inn. To see if it had worked. If you were alive because I was gone."

He was quiet for a moment.

"You weren't," he said. "Most of you. They were in the yard. In the common room. On the stairs." His voice did not change. "And somebody was screaming at me. I don't know who. I couldn't see. Just a voice, screaming, *where were you, where the hell were you*. Over and over. As if I'd been the only thing that could have stopped it, and I'd gone away."

Momo, at the end of the long table, put her face in her hands.

"I don't know what happened after that," said Mau. "Not really. I remember hearing it start. Construction. Starting to adapt. On its own. And the wheel. I remember the wheel." He looked up for the first time, past all of them, at nothing. "It turned and it didn't stop. Faster than I've ever felt it. Faster than the plaza. It turned and turned, and everything in me was changing, everything was being made, faster than I could feel what it was."

He looked at Rimuru by the cold hearth. At Milim, on the floor, who had turned around at last and was facing him, her face blank with shock. At Eren, standing by the window.

"I think I beat you," said Mau quietly. "In that one. The three of you. You came to stop me, and I think I won. I think I destroyed everything."

Milim made a sound like something breaking.

Then Mau looked at Frieren, on the couch above him.

He did not say anything. He looked at her for a long moment, and his face did something none of them had ever seen it do. Then he looked away, back at the floor.

"I don't remember what I did in that one," he said. "To anyone. I don't want to."

By the window, Eren had gone very still.

"I know that one," said Eren.

It was barely a whisper. Nobody but Mikasa heard it. She turned and looked at him, and he did not look back. He was looking at Mau, on the floor, with an expression that was not pity, or horror, or fear. It was something much closer to recognition.



"The last one," said Mau, "was a wedding."

The room changed.

Not all of it. Most of them had not seen. But some of them had stood in the plaza at the very end of the Trial, when the domain went thin. Through it, faint as breath on glass, they had seen another place: a clearing, frost, a ruin of cut flowers, a figure kneeling in the middle of it holding someone. They had seen that. They had not known what it was.

"Before that," said Mau, "there was a white room. Once. Two chairs. And a door with a sign on it. ONE LEAVES." He looked at the floor. "And you stood up, Frieren. You walked toward the door. And I stood in front of it."

Frieren's hand pressed harder against her chest.

"I didn't understand that one, at the time," said Mau. "I understood it later. At the wedding."

He told them about the wedding.

He did not know the word for most of it. He described it the way you describe a thing you have only seen once, from the inside. A clearing, the first one. Frost on the grass. Flowers everywhere, white and blue, and he had made them: he had made every single one, with his own mana, the way he had once failed to in the flower field and then, years later, had not. Everyone coming up through the trees in twos and threes and crowds. Fern complaining about a dress. Milim on Rimuru's shoulders. Bocchi with her guitar.

"I'd lived a long time in that one," said Mau. "I was on the elf route. It was open. We both had time." He paused. "Everyone came. Everyone. Because I asked."

Then something came out of the sky.

He told them about the seam in the sky, and the slashes, cutting the air and the trees and the flowers. He told them how he had moved to get in front of them, the way he always did, and how the seal had opened. Not broken: opened, one door, on its own. And Infinity had come out.

"I couldn't move toward you," he said. "Every step I took, the space stretched to fit it. I couldn't drop it. I tore at it, and it didn't notice." His voice had gone very flat. "So I watched. All of you. One at a time. From inside it. I could hear my own heart, it was so quiet."

"And Frieren," he said.

"Frieren wasn't fighting the sky. She was fighting *me*. Trying to get in. To get to me. Spell after spell into the space around me, and every one of them stopped a finger's width from my skin." He held up his hand, flat, in the air in front of his own chest. "Here. Her hand was here. She went down on one knee in the flowers and she kept her hand up. Here. A finger's width. And then it let go. All at once. And I caught her."

He lowered his hand.

He did not say anything else.



"What did she say," said Frieren.

Mau did not look up.

"The false one," said Frieren. Her voice was perfectly calm, and terrible. "At the end. She said something. I saw her mouth move, through the wall. I saw your face." She took her hand away from her chest. "You've never told anyone. You didn't tell us on the road. You haven't told anyone in this house." She leaned forward on the couch. "Tell me what that false version of me said to you."

"Frieren—"

"*Tell me*," said Frieren.

And something in Mau went.

It was not like the panic. It was the opposite of the panic. It was like a weight coming off a beam that had held it too long, so that the beam does not break but rises, a little, shuddering, into a shape it has forgotten it had. His shoulders came down. His face opened. His breath came out of him in a long broken rush.

He told her.

"She said, *you stood in front of the door*," said Mau. "She said, *I could have gone home. I could have gone back to my world. You stood in front of the door, and I let you.*" He was crying now, without seeming to notice. "She said, *I wanted this so much I was angry about it. And look.* And then she said, *if it weren't for you, if you'd let me go back, I would be happy. I would be bored, somewhere, and happy. I would still be alive.*"

He stopped.

"And then she closed her eyes," said Mau. "And she didn't open them again."

Frieren made a sound.

"No," she said. "No. No, no, no, no—"

"I know," said Mau. "I know. I know it wasn't you." He looked up at her at last, with his face wet. "But then I heard a voice. Yours. From outside. You said my name. Just my name. And I knew it was you, the real you, because it was the only thing in four hundred and thirty-seven that had ever come from *outside*."

Frieren's hands were over her mouth.

"And the Goddess asked," said Mau, "whether you'd choose me. And I said—" He stopped.

"You said *they shouldn't*," said Frieren through her hands. "I heard you. I heard you say it."

"Yes."

"I couldn't reach you." Her voice broke completely. "I heard you say it, and I couldn't get through the wall, and I couldn't— I couldn't *defend* you. From her. From that. From what she was doing to you."

"I know."



"And then it ended," said Mau. "And I was in the real world. Kneeling in the plaza. And the Goddess opened her hand and said *Return*, and *Stay*, and *ten days*. And the reward was a door. A way out of this world, for all of you. Or a way to bring the people you love here, to where I was." He looked down at his hands. "And all I could think was, I'm the problem. I'm the reason all of it happens. Everything goes to hell because I'm in it. Every single time." His voice dropped very low. "So I saw a chance. One I hadn't seen in four hundred and thirty-seven deaths. A way to give you a life where you didn't die."

"Without you in it," said Anko.

Mau did not answer.

Anko closed her notebook. She had written nothing in it all night.

"You're thinking the way you thought with Sukuna," she said, quite gently. "Again. You know that, don't you? You stepped into the line on that field because you thought it was you or her. You were ready to stay there, as long as she was alive. And now you've just done it again. Bigger. With all of us."

Mau stayed very still.

He had not seen it. He had been inside it so long, four hundred and thirty-seven times, and then three days of not sleeping, that he had not seen it at all. It was the same shape. The same field. The same line. He had promised her, afterward, in a room with the door shut, that he would never decide alone again. He had believed he hadn't. He had written *I don't think it is the same* on the fourth draft, at three in the morning, and meant it.

It was the same.

"And you forgot," said Yuta, from the bench, "that against Sukuna we found a third way."

Mau looked at him.

"You thought it was you or her," said Yuta. His hand was still locked around Maki's. "It wasn't. It turned out it wasn't. I held you together, and Rimuru held the line, and every single person in this room did something. And you both lived." He leaned forward. "There was a third way. There was always a third way. You just couldn't see it from where you were standing, because you were standing in front of everyone, alone, where nobody could reach you."

Mau's face crumpled.

"I'm sorry," he said.

He said it to the floor, and then to Yuta, and then to all of them, and then to Frieren, and then to nobody, over and over.

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

Nobody told him to stop. Nobody told him it was all right, because it was not all right yet, and he would have known they were lying. Nobody judged him either. Every person in that room understood something about him that night that they had not understood before. It was not an excuse. It was a reason, which is different, and harder, and has to be carried.

They let him say it until he was done.
