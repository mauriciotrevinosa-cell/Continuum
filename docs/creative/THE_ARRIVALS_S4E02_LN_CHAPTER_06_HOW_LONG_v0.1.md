# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 10 — How Long

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 10  
**Continuity state:** Same night | Ciel's three measures | short end: a hundred years older than Frieren | far end: 'a possibility' past all of Chloe's resets, which Rimuru keeps | aloud: 'oldest in the house'; Milim | Infinity leaks: Frieren's hands stop a finger's width away

It was Rimuru who asked. Not aloud. Inside, where only she could hear him.

*How long was he in there?*

The answer did not come at once. That was unusual. Ciel always answered at once. Even *I don't know* came at once, from her.

*I do not know exactly,* said Ciel at last. *I will not pretend I do. To me, every loop was an instant. A jolt in the thread, and a number. I could not feel time pass in there. Only the restarts.*

*But you looked.*

*I looked. And in what I saw, there are three things I can measure against each other, the way Senku measures a river by three stones.*

*Tell me.*

*The first is the seasons. Many of the scenarios had seasons in them. Winters. Harvests. He remembers some of them passing, one after another, in a single loop. Some loops were seconds. Some were years. I counted the winters I could find.*

*The second is his body. In some of the memories he is older. A scar on the back of his left hand that he does not have yet. Lines at his eyes. In one of them he walks slowly, and in another a child he does not know calls him grandfather.*

Rimuru did not move.

*The third,* said Ciel, *is the elf route. Some of the scenarios were not one life. In some he had taken the route. It was open in there. And on the elf route, a single loop could be a very long time.*

*How long?*

*Long.*

*Give me a number, Ciel. Rough. Just rough.*

There was a pause. In it, Rimuru had time to understand that he did not want the number.

*At the short end,* said Ciel, *he is now about a hundred years older than Frieren.*



Rimuru sat very still on the floor of the common room with his back against the long table.

Across the room, Frieren was on the couch with her hands in her lap. Frieren, who had been alive for more than a thousand years. Who had walked out of a hero's funeral and kept walking for decades. Who had told him once, half-joking, that she measured time the way other people measured weather.

A hundred years older than her.

*And the far end?* said Rimuru.

Ciel did not answer.

She did not answer for so long that Rimuru began to think she would not. When she did, her voice had changed. It had gone very quiet, the way she went quiet only about two things, and he was one of them.

*It is a possibility,* said Ciel, *that he is older than all of Chloe's resets combined.*

Rimuru stopped breathing.

Chloe. A girl in another world, in another life, who had gone back through time again and again and again, for him. Each time she had lived through two thousand years to reach him, and each time she had watched him die and gone back to try again. She had done it alone. She had done it for so long that nobody, not even Raphael, had ever been able to say how many times. He had found out at the very end. Afterward he had sworn, with everything he was, that he would never again let anyone carry something like that alone for him. Never again.

*A possibility,* he said.

*A possibility. The far end of three measurements that do not agree. I do not think it is likely. I think it is possible. I would like it noted that I said possible.*

He took it as that. As the far edge of a range, the way you say *as far as the horizon* without meaning that anyone will walk there. He took it, and folded it very small, and put it somewhere inside himself where he would not have to look at it tonight. He did not say it aloud. He was not sure he could have.



"What happened?" said Mau.

Rimuru looked up. Everyone was looking at him. He had not realized how long he had been sitting there with his face doing whatever it had been doing.

"Ciel finished the arithmetic," said Rimuru.

His voice came out much steadier than he felt. He made it come out that way. Someone in the room had to.

"How long you were in there," he said. "She looked at the seasons in your memories. At how old you are in some of them. At the elf route." He breathed. "She can't be exact. But she gave me a number. A conservative one."

Mau waited.

"Conservatively," said Rimuru, "you're about a hundred years older than Frieren."

Nobody said anything.

Nobody in that room had any idea what to say to that. Frieren's hands, in her lap, had gone completely still. Fern's pen had stopped halfway across the page. Milim, on the floor, had turned all the way around and was staring at Mau as if she had never seen him before. Perhaps, in a sense, she had not.

Mau looked at his own hands. They looked the same. They were the same hands. No scar on the back of the left one. Not yet.

"And the not-conservative one?" he said.

Rimuru had known he would ask. He had decided, in the moment between Ciel's voice and his own, what he would say.

"Officially," said Rimuru, and made his mouth do something that was almost a smile, "you'd be the oldest person in this house."

It was meant to make it smaller. It did not.

Milim made a sound.

It came out of her small and cracked, the way it had come out of her on the floor beside Frieren on the night of the bay. She was crying, enormously and silently, the way a child cries before the noise arrives.

"Are you sure?" said Milim. "Rimuru. Are you *sure*? Because I've— I've lived a long time. A really, really long time. Longer than anyone. You know how long."

Rimuru looked at her.

"Yes," he said gently. "I'm sure. Longer than you, Milim."

Milim stared at him.

Then she looked at Mau. At the boy her brother had come to love, sitting on the floor with his back against the arm of a couch, who had lived longer than she had. Who had done it in four hundred and thirty-seven pieces, every one of which had ended in his death. And who had come out the other side and walked down a road in the dark to try to save them all from himself.

Milim, who was older than kingdoms, and who had spent more years alone than anyone in the room could imagine, understood what that meant better than any of them.

She did not say anything else. She put her face in her knees and stayed like that.



Frieren moved.

She did it slowly, the way you approach something wounded. She got down off the couch onto the floor and came across the small space to where Mau was sitting, on her knees. She reached out with both hands, to hold his face, or his shoulders, or anything of him at all.

Her hands stopped.

They stopped a finger's width from his skin. Not because she stopped them. They simply stopped, the way water stops at glass.

She pushed.

Nothing. The air between her palms and his face did not get smaller. It did not get anything. She pushed harder, and the space was still a finger's width, perfectly, and her hands were trembling with the effort, and it did not notice her at all.

Mau looked at her hands.

A finger's width from his face.

His whole body went cold.

He had seen this. Kneeling in cut flowers, with frost on the grass, a woman's hand held up flat against nothing a finger's width from his chest, until the nothing let go and she fell into his arms and said *if it weren't for you*.

"No," said Mau. "No. Not that. Not *that*—"

He tried to drop it. He tore at it, inside, the way he had torn at it in the clearing. It did not notice him either.

"I can't," he said. His voice had gone high and strange. "I can't make it stop. Frieren, I can't—"

"I know," said Frieren. Her hands were still up, trembling, a finger's width from his face. "I know. It's all right. I'm here. I'm right here."

"You were right there *then*—"

"I'm not her."

"I *know* you're not her, I know, I *know*—"

Rimuru was on his feet. Yuta was on his feet. Maki had half-risen from the bench. Nobody could get any closer than Frieren had. Milim lifted her face from her knees and reached out one hand, very carefully, from where she sat. It stopped too, a finger's width short, in the air.

Infinity.

It had leaked. Not from the Trial this time. From him. From the inside, from the grief, from the place where the walls held everything else and could not hold this. It wrapped him whole and perfect and kept everything out. No slash. No spell. No falling tree.

No one.

He was perfectly safe.

And he could not bear it.
