# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 11 — Two Days of Punishment

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 11  
**Continuity state:** Same night | fifteen minutes; the S1 backpack | exit rights | the contract: upriver, 'somewhere I never died' | Frieren won't sign: 'You said both of us' (she speaks of that night) | 'Halfway... Bad joke.' | Coco: 'Were we with you?' | 'Here.' each evening | he goes north

Mau stood up.

He did it the way you stand up in a room where you are about to be sick: carefully, all at once, without looking at anyone. Frieren's hands were still up. She did not lower them. She knelt there on the floor with her palms open toward the space where he had been sitting, a finger's width from nothing.

"I need—" said Mau. He stopped. "I need a minute. I need space. I'm sorry. I need a minute."

He walked out of the common room.

Nobody stopped him. Nobody could have. Even if anyone had tried to take his arm, it would not have reached him.

They heard his footsteps go down the family corridor, past the fort's south window and the narrow room with REM and *(SISTER)* on the door. They heard the door of the room with the east window open and close.

Frieren got up off her knees.

"Frieren," said Rimuru.

She did not answer. She went to the mouth of the corridor and stood there, looking down it at the closed door at the end.

She did not go down it.

She wanted to. Everyone in the room could see how much she wanted to. Her whole body was leaning toward that door. But her hands were still trembling from pushing against something that did not notice her. She stood at the mouth of the corridor and did not go, because she understood that if she went and knocked and he opened it, her hands would stop a finger's width from him again. She did not think she could watch his face if that happened twice.

So she stood and waited.

Nobody else moved.



Fifteen minutes.

Afterward, more than one of them would think about that number. Fifteen minutes was what Stark had asked for, in Momo's kitchen, with flour on his face. Fifteen minutes, and then Fern had come back in a dress Marin had made, and kissed him in front of everyone. It had been a good number, once. It had been, for a little while, the funniest number in the house.

Fifteen minutes after he went down the corridor, the door at the end of it opened again.

Mau came back up the corridor and into the common room.

He was carrying a backpack.

Everyone who had been there in the beginning knew that backpack. It was the one he had woken up with, in a clearing, on the first morning of his life that he could remember. The one that had sat open beside the bed in the old inn for weeks, because packing and unpacking it every day had become pointless. The one he had carried through every move since, and never used.

It was packed.

The room went cold in a different way.

"The rules," said Mau. He stood just inside the common room, with the backpack on one shoulder. He did not come any closer to anyone. "The settlement's rules. They apply to everyone. Don't they." He looked at Fern. "Exit rights. Anyone can leave. Anyone can come back. Nobody has to ask. That's the rule. That's what we wrote, when Eren came."

"Yes," said Fern. Her voice was very careful. "That's the rule."

"So I can leave," said Mau. "Whenever I want."

Nobody answered.

Every person in the room was thinking the same thing. The road south. The first clearing. The fourth draft. A note left behind on a table. They were all thinking it, and they could all see each other thinking it.

Mau reached into the pocket of his coat and took out a sheet of paper. It was folded twice. He unfolded it and held it out toward Fern at arm's length. He did not walk over to her. Rimuru took it from him, carefully, and carried it the rest of the way.

Fern read it aloud.

"*I'm going away for two days,*" she read. "*Nobody may follow me, come with me, or come looking for me. I will be back on the night of the second day, or the morning of the third.*

"*I am not going far. I am not going to the first clearing, or the road south, or anywhere the Goddess has been, or anywhere the Trial ever was. I am going upriver, past the stepping stones, to the hills, where the river comes down. Somewhere I never died.*

"*I need to breathe. I need to understand that I came back here.*"

Fern looked up.

"*Signed, Mau,*" she read. "There's a line under it. For witnesses. It's empty."

"I don't expect anyone to sign it," said Mau. "It's my contract. I signed it. That's enough. It doesn't affect anyone who isn't here." He almost smiled. "That's one of your rules too."



He turned to Frieren.

She was still standing at the mouth of the corridor, where she had been waiting. He looked at her across the room, the whole width of it, and he did not come any closer.

"Will you sign it?" he said. "Not because you have to. I'd like you to have seen it. That's all."

Frieren looked at him.

"No," she said.

He nodded slowly. He had expected that.

"I can help you," said Frieren. "Whatever this is. I can help you with it. You don't have to go anywhere. You don't have to go *alone*." Her voice rose for the first time all night. "That's the whole point. That's the thing you just spent four hours telling us you got wrong. You went alone. You stood in front alone. And now—"

"I know," said Mau.

"You said *both of us*."

The room went very still.

None of them knew what those two words meant. Only she did. But every one of them who had written their name in Fern's ledger under a single line, the whole of Rings One and Two, understood at once what she was doing. She was speaking of that night. In front of everyone. The one person besides him who was allowed to.

"In that bed," said Frieren. "With a hole in you. You looked at me and said *both of us*. *That's when I'm alive. Not one. Both.* You *promised*."

"I know," said Mau. "I know. That's why I need to go."

"That makes no *sense*—"

"It's the only thing that does." He looked at her across the room, and his face was very calm, and very wet. "I need to understand where I went wrong. I need to understand how I could have said that to you, and meant it, and then walked out of our room at three in the morning anyway. It doesn't matter how long I was in there. A hundred years. More. It doesn't matter. I can't break my promises. Not again. And I can't keep them until I understand how I broke them." He swallowed. "I can't do that here. Not with everyone looking at me like this. Not when nobody can touch me."

Frieren did not say anything.



"But you kept it," said someone.

It was Umaru. She had come forward from the fort, in her hood and her PRESIDENT (TEMPORARY) sash, and was standing in the middle of the room with her hands in fists.

"You kept it," said Umaru. "Tonight. You put it down. You told us. That's the promise. That's what I asked for. You *did* it."

Mau looked at her.

"Halfway," he said gently. "I put it down halfway." He shifted the backpack on his shoulder. "So. Two days of punishment."

Umaru's mouth opened.

"Bad joke," said Mau, before she could say anything. "Sorry. Bad joke."



He was almost at the door when someone else spoke.

It was Coco. She had not moved from the north end of the room. She was sitting with the Witch Hat girls, small, with her hat in her lap and her face streaked. When she spoke, her voice was very thin.

"Mau," said Coco. "In there. Were we with you? The whole time? Were we there?"

He stopped with his hand on the door.

He did not turn around for a long moment. When he did, his face was the face from the end of the road, when they had caught him in the dark.

"No," he said. "When you died, it was always at the start. Before I could get to you. It never gave me time to reach you." He paused. "And when I could reach you, I was the one who died."

Nobody said anything after that. Nobody could.

"Ciel," said Mau, to the air. "Every evening. One word. To Rimuru. *Here.* So they know."

In two heads, a voice said:

*Yes.*

Mau opened the door. The cold came in, the first real cold of the year, smelling of frost and river and the end of autumn. He went out into it with the backpack on his shoulder and a lamp in his hand, and pulled the door shut behind him. It clicked.

They stood in the common room and listened to his footsteps cross the yard. Past the ink tree in its ring of stakes. Past the plaza. Not south. North, toward the river, toward the stepping stones and the dark shape of the hills.

Fern looked down at the contract in her hands. At the empty line for witnesses.

She did not sign it either. She copied it, word for word, into the ledger, under the date. Then, beneath it, in her neat hand, she wrote:

*Day five. He told us. He went to breathe. He said he would come back.*

She closed the ledger.

At the mouth of the corridor, Frieren stood looking at the door he had gone out of. Her hands were still trembling, very slightly, held a little way out from her body, a finger's width from nothing at all.
