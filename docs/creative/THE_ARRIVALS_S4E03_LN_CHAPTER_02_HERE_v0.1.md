## Chapter 13 — Here

The house woke on the sixth day and did not know what to do with itself.

It did the work, because the work was always there. Momo lit the stove. Senku went out to the water court and found that a valve had frozen in the night, and spent the morning swearing at it with great satisfaction. The washing court filled. Arrival House opened its doors at the first bell, and the line was already there, going around the corner. Fern opened the ledger.

But the house kept stopping.

People would be in the middle of something, carrying a basket or arguing about the valve, and they would stop and look up, toward the north end of the settlement, where the river path went. Then they would go back to what they were doing, a little slower. Then they would stop again.



Frieren had not slept.

She had lain down in the bed in the room with the east window, on his side, because the pillow there smelled like him. She had lain there all night with her eyes open, looking at the ceiling. In the morning she got up and sat in the reading chair and did not read.

She did not go to the common room for breakfast. Fern came at mid-morning with a bowl and set it down on the table by the bed and sat on the end of the bed without saying anything. After a while Frieren picked up the spoon and ate. It was the way she had eaten in the corridor outside the bay, a long time ago. Neither of them mentioned that.

"He'll come back," said Fern.

"I know."

"He said the night of the second day. Or the morning of the third."

"I know."

"He's never broken a promise he made out loud," said Fern. Then she stopped, because that was not true. They both knew it was not true. She did not take it back.

"He broke one on the road," said Frieren. "He broke one to Ori. He broke the one he made to me after Sukuna. He broke the one he made to Umaru at the ceremony." She turned the spoon over in her fingers. "He told us all of that last night. He sat on the floor and told us. And then he wrote a new one, and signed it, and asked me to sign it, and I said no."

Fern waited.

"I said no," said Frieren, "because if I signed it, I was agreeing that he should go alone." She put the spoon down. "And then I didn't follow him. Because that's the rule. Because I won't decide for him. Because I'm not going to be the one who stands in front of the door." She was quiet for a long moment. "I'm so angry I can't breathe, Fern."

"I know," said Fern.

"Not at him. Or not only at him." Frieren looked at her hands. "At whatever it was, in there, that taught him he had to. At four hundred and thirty-seven of whatever it was. At the false one, with my face, who said—" She stopped. She did not say it. "I couldn't reach him. Last night. I couldn't touch him. My hands stopped."

"I saw."

"It was the same," said Frieren. "As in there. He said it. A finger's width. That's what she did. The false one. With her hand up, until it let go." She pressed her open hand flat against her chest, the way she had in the common room. "I'll never be able to reach for him again without him seeing her."

Fern did not say *that's not true*. She did not say *it'll get better*. She had learned from Frieren, a long time ago, not to say things she did not know.

She moved up the bed and put her arms around her teacher from behind, and held on, the way she had held on when she was very small and Frieren had not known what to do with a crying child, and had simply stayed.

They stayed like that for a long time.



The fort convened in emergency session at noon.

All twenty citizens. The President in her sash, the Opposition in hers. The six NEXT cushions in the annex were empty behind their curtain. Ori sat by her window in the south wall, looking down the corridor at the closed door of the room with the east window. Everyone knew it had nobody behind it but Frieren.

"Article 6," said Umaru.

Nobody needed it read. It was pinned inside the toll booth. *Nobody in the fort is ever alone.*

"He's not in the fort," said Agott, not unkindly. "He was never a citizen."

"He's alone," said Umaru. "Right now. Up the river. By himself. On purpose." She looked around at all of them. "I know he's not a citizen. I know he went because he wanted to, and the rules say he can, and I'm not saying anybody should go after him. I'm not saying that." She swallowed. "I'm saying I don't know what to do with Article 6 when the person who's alone isn't one of us."

Nobody had an answer.

Then Ori spoke, from the window.

"He is one of us," she said.

Everyone turned.

Ori did not turn from the window. She was still looking down the corridor.

"He's not a citizen," she said. "But he's one of us. He was in the first basket. He wrote back to every single note. He sent Agott *thank you for the second side*." Her voice was very quiet. "He sent me a stone. From under the window. Because he couldn't go and get one himself."

She put her hand in her pocket. When she took it out, there was a small smooth stone in her palm, grey with a white band through it.

The fort looked at it. Nobody said anything about the empty square on the windowsill of the room with the east window. Everybody knew.

"I took it back," said Ori. "On the fourth day. Because I was angry. Because he left a note instead of telling me first, and I wanted him to see the empty place and know." Her fingers closed around the stone. "I'm still angry."

"Okay," said Umaru.

"I'm going to give it back," said Ori. "When he comes home. Not because I'm not angry. Because last night he told us. Everything. He sat on the floor and told us." She looked at the stone in her hand. "That's telling me first. It's late. It's so late. But it's what I asked for."

Agott took off her OPPOSITION sash, folded it neatly, and set it on the cushion beside her. Since the election, by a custom nobody had ever written down, that meant the Opposition had no objection.

"Then Article 6 applies," said Umaru, with great dignity, "to anyone Ori says it does."

"Seconded," said Agott.

Nano wrote it down.



That evening, at dinner, the common room was full and very quiet.

Everyone had come. Not for any reason. Nobody had called a meeting. They had simply all come, at the hour when the lamps went on, and sat down at the long table and on the benches and the floor, and eaten Momo's stew, and not talked very much.

Rimuru sat at the cold hearth with his bowl untouched on his knees.

They were all, every one of them, watching him without seeming to.

The light went out of the windows. The lamps took over. Somewhere in the kitchen a pot ticked as it cooled, the way one had the night before, and several people heard it and looked away from each other.

Then Rimuru's eyes went somewhere else.

The whole room stopped.

It took a moment, as it always did. Then his face changed, in a way none of them had seen it change in days, and he looked up.

"*Here*," said Rimuru.

He said it exactly the way Ciel had said it to him. One word, set down plainly, like a stone on a table.

Nobody cheered. Nobody said anything at all. But something went out of the room, all at once, the way a long-held breath goes out of a body. Momo put her face in her apron. Milim, on the floor, lay down flat on her back with her arms out and stared at the ceiling and laughed, once, a wet, cracked sound. Fern wrote it in the ledger: *Day six. Evening. Here.*

And down the corridor, in the room with the east window, Frieren heard it through two walls.

She did not come out. But she lay down on the bed, on his side, with her face in his pillow. After a while, for the first time in two days, she slept.
