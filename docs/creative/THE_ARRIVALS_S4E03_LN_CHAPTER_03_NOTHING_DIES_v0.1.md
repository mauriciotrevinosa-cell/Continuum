# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 14 — Nothing Dies

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 14  
**Continuity state:** Day 6, the falls | he does nothing all day | water parts around his hands | he can't take Infinity off | sunset: 'One.' | Ciel sends 'Here' and tells him who cried (rule five) | rain he cannot feel

He did nothing all day.

That was the plan, insofar as he had one. He had thought about it on the walk, in the dark, with the river loud beside him. Four hundred and thirty-seven times he had done something. Stood in front, or not. Run, or stayed. Asked, or not asked. Fought, or begged, or bargained, or planned. Every loop had been a problem, and he had tried to solve every one of them, and every one of them had ended the same way.

He had never once, in any of them, simply sat still and let a day go by.

So he did.



He sat on the flat rock above the highest pool and watched the water.

The sun came up over the hills and moved, very slowly, across the sky. He watched it. He watched the shadow of the pines shorten, and then, past noon, begin to lengthen again on the other side. He watched the light on the water go from grey to silver to gold to white and back again. A fish rose in the second pool, once, around mid-morning, and he watched the rings spread until they reached the stones and stopped.

Nothing happened.

That was the strangest thing. Nothing happened. Nothing came out of the trees. Nothing fell from the sky. Nobody screamed. Nobody he loved walked up the river path and stood at the bottom of the falls and called his name, so that he would have to choose. There was no white room, no door, no knife on a table. The water fell. The pines stood. The sun moved.

Every so often his body would go tight, all at once, the way it had learned to. The way it did when a scenario had gone quiet for too long and the quiet meant something was coming. His shoulders would come up and his breath would stop and he would wait for it.

It did not come.

After a while his body let go again, a little, and he went back to watching the water.



At midday he ate. One of the two dried pears from the merchant at the washing court, the ones he had bought on his first day out, weeks ago, and saved in the bottom of the backpack without knowing why. Bread Momo had put in his pocket at some point that he could not remember. A little cold water from the pool, scooped up in his hands.

He had to put his hands into the water to scoop it, and the water did not touch them.

It parted around his fingers, a finger's width away, as if his hands were made of glass. He held them there in the pool and watched it flow around them without touching. He tried to bring them together to cup it, and the water slid away from both palms at once. In the end he lay down on his stomach on the rock and put his face close to the surface and drank like an animal. The water stopped a finger's width from his lips too, and then, slowly, as if it had remembered him, let him have it.

He lay there afterward with his cheek on the warm rock.

It was letting him drink. It let air in, or he would not be breathing. It let the ground take him, eventually, when he fell. It let in what he needed. Only what reached *for* him, it stopped. A hand. A spell. Rain. Water in his palms.

It had decided, somewhere in him, that anything reaching for him was a danger.

He understood why. He did not know how to tell it otherwise.



All afternoon he tried to put it down.

He tried the way he had tried in the clearing, tearing at it, and it did not notice him. He tried the way he had learned to hold the walls for Ciel: precisely, imagining the shape exactly. He imagined the Infinity as a coat, and himself taking it off. It did not come off. He imagined it as a door, and himself closing it. There was no door.

He tried asking it.

That was what had worked, once, in the old inn, for the witches. He had held one place so precisely in his mind that the spatial leak had come when he asked for it. The only time it had ever come when he asked. He held his own hand in his mind, precisely, the back of it, the skin, the place a finger's width above it where the nothing began, and asked.

Nothing.

He tried until the sun was low and his head ached and his hands were shaking. Then he stopped, because he had learned four hundred and thirty-seven times what it looked like to keep trying something that did not work until it killed you.

He sat back against the backpack and watched the sun go down behind the pines.

It went down. That was all. It went down slowly and completely, and the sky turned orange, then red, then a deep clear blue with one star in it over the highest hill. Nothing reset. Nothing started again. The day simply ended, the way days end, and the next one was on its way.

He counted it.

*One.*

"Ciel," he said, out loud, to the dark.

*I am here.*

"It's evening."

*Yes.*

"Send it."

A small pause.

*Sending.*

He sat very still.

"Did he—" Mau stopped. "Can you tell? Whether they heard?"

*I can feel Master. Only Master.* Another pause, longer. *He said it out loud. To the whole room. They were all there. They were waiting for it.*

Mau put his face in his knees.

*He says*, said Ciel, more quietly, *that Milim lay down on the floor and laughed. And Fern wrote it in the ledger. And Momo cried into her apron.* A pause. *He did not ask me to tell you that. I am telling you anyway. Rule five.*

Mau did not lift his head for a long time.



In the night it rained.

It came over the hills an hour after dark, light at first, then steady, the cold thin rain of the end of autumn. He heard it coming in the pines before it reached him. His whole body went rigid. In the Trial, rain had meant something. Not always, but often. Rain had been the sound under a great many endings, and his body remembered every one of them before his mind could tell it otherwise.

He sat on the rock with his arms around his knees and waited for it to hit him.

It did not hit him.

It fell all around him, on the rock, on the pools, on the backpack, and every drop of it that fell toward him stopped a finger's width from his skin and slid away. He could see it in the light of the little lamp: a shape of rain around him, a shell, a perfect empty space the exact size of himself, with the rain running down its outside.

He was completely dry.

He sat in the middle of the storm, dry, untouched, and perfectly safe. He realized that he would have given anything to be wet.

His body slowly came to understand that the rain was only rain. That nothing was coming. That the sound in the pines was only water. His shoulders came down. His breath came back. The shell of nothing stayed exactly where it was.

He lay down on the rock inside it, with the backpack under his head, and listened to the rain he could not feel. After a long time, he slept.
