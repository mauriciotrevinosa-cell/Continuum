# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 16 — The House Without Him

On the seventh day, the house did ordinary things on purpose.

Nobody decided it. It happened the way the corridor outside the bay had emptied on its own, on the night of the bay, because everyone understood at once what was needed. On the morning of the seventh day the whole house got up and did ordinary things, deliberately and stubbornly, as if ordinariness were a wall they could hold up between him and everything else until he came home.



Anko went to Arrival House at the first bell with a copy of the contract.

She had made it herself, in her clearest hand, word for word from Fern's ledger: *I'm going away for two days... I am not going to the first clearing, or the road south, or anywhere the Goddess has been... I need to breathe. I need to understand that I came back here.* Under it she had written, in her own hand: *Posted with the knowledge of Rings One and Two. He said the night of the second day.*

She pinned it to the board inside the door, where everyone in the line would see it on the way in.

Rumor number seven had arrived the night before, from the river towns, faster than rumors had any right to travel. It said that the monster had gone to the Goddess at last, alone, and that the settlement was hiding it. Anko did not deny rumor number seven. She had learned a long time ago, in another life, in a city full of night, that denials only feed a rumor. So she pinned the truth next to it, in a clear hand, and let people read both.

By noon, three people in the line had stopped to read the contract. One of them was the cooper from the river towns.

He read it twice. Then he went to the old inn and asked Rem whether there was any news, and Rem said *here*, and he nodded and went back to the line.



The witches came to the old inn at midmorning.

It was their day. Every second week, at the agreed bell, for an hour, in the room with the door that stuck and the creak two steps in and the wobbly chair Umaru had broken. It had been their day since the second week after the containment. They had not missed one.

Rem met them at the door.

"He isn't here," she said. "He went upriver. For two days. He said he would be back tonight, or tomorrow morning."

The first witch looked at her for a long moment.

"We know," she said. "We read the board."

"Then—"

"It is our day," said the first witch. "We came."

She went into the session room and sat down, not in the wobbly chair, which was his, but in the one across from it. The other witch stood by the window. They stayed for exactly an hour. They did not say anything, and did not do anything. They sat in the room where every second week they asked about his seams, with the wobbly chair empty in the stripe of light from the window.

Rem brought them tea. They drank it.

When the hour was over the first witch stood up and went to the door. She paused there, with her hand on the frame that stuck.

"Tell him," she said, "that we came."

"I will."

"Tell him," said the first witch, "that we are growing tired of coming." She did not say it unkindly. She said it like an observation about the weather. "Every second week. This room. This door. This chair. Back to the forest. And again." She looked at the old inn's long room, at the stove, at the shelf of cups. Behind her, the other witch was looking at them too, with the expression Mau had not been able to name. "Tell him we would like to talk about the contract. When he is himself again. Not before."

Rem looked at her for a long moment.

"I'll tell him," she said.

The witches went out. The door did not stick for them. It never did.



In the fort, the Witch Hat girls were teaching Hakari's group a game with string. Hakari was very bad at it and enjoyed it enormously. Nano was very good at it and did not enjoy it at all. Kusuri shook her little jars and made the fort glow green at midday, for no reason. Kessoku practiced on the stage corner, quietly, the slow circling thing Bocchi had been playing all week. Today, for the first time, it had an ending. Kita had found it. It was not sad.

In the kitchen, Momo and the refuge cooks made enough bread for the settlement and then, on Momo's orders, a second batch.

"Who's that for?" said one of the cooks.

"Tomorrow," said Momo, and slammed the oven door.

At the washing court, Marin hung the winter coats out to air, all of them, in a long line, every one with a name pinned to it in Wakana's hand. The coat with MAU on it was in the middle of the line. She did not take it down. She did not move it to the end. She hung it in the middle, where it belonged, and stood looking at it for a while with her hands in her sleeves.

At the water court, Senku fixed the frozen valve. Then he fixed one that was not broken, because he wanted something to fix.

Eren sat on the wall above the bridge road, where Mikasa found him at noon, looking south. He was not looking toward the hills where Mau was. He was looking the other way, at the road where they had caught him in the dark.

"You said *I know that one*," said Mikasa. "Two nights ago. When he said he'd destroyed everything."

Eren did not answer for a long time.

"I've seen it," he said finally. "Not done it. Seen it. In my head, the way I see things that haven't happened. Everything, flattened. Because I decided it was the only way." He looked at his hands. "I haven't done it. I'm not going to. That's the whole point of me being here." He was quiet. "But I know what it looks like from inside. When you've decided you're the problem, and the answer is to be the thing that ends it. He was there. In one of them. For real, as far as his body knows."

Mikasa sat down beside him on the wall.

"He came back from it," she said.

"Yeah," said Eren. "He did." A pause. "So can I."

Mikasa did not say anything. She put her shoulder against his, and they sat there looking at the road together until the bell rang for the midday meal.



Frieren took the grimoire to the ink tree.

She did it at mid-morning, after Fern had made her eat. She took the old grimoire from the nightstand, the one with the four small pressed flowers between its pages. One for every time they had opened the door on purpose. She took a blanket from the bed, too, and went out through the side door of the Muslin to the garden, to the ring of small stakes.

The shoot inside the ring was not a shoot anymore. It was a small tree. Ten leaves now, Coco's slip on the stake said. Silver-green, a color nobody had a name for.

On the board beside it, under WHO WATERS IT and *Everyone*, were three names in charcoal. Coco had rewritten them two days ago, after the rain.

Frieren spread the blanket on the grass outside the ring, a hand's width from the nearest stake, and sat down on it with her back to the cold, and opened the grimoire.

She read beside the tree all day.

She did not read anything in particular. She turned the pages slowly, past old spells she had collected over a thousand years because they were useless and she liked them. A spell to make a field of flowers. A spell to find a lost button. A spell to make the sea shine at night, which had taken her sixty years, and which she had used once alone, and once, at the end of the summer, for him. She stopped at each of the four pressed flowers and looked at it for a while, and then turned the page.

People came and went. Coco came with the bucket, because it was somebody's turn, and it said everyone, and watered the tree without a word. Then she sat down on the corner of the blanket for a while before she went away again. Ori came at midday with bread from the kitchen and put it on the blanket, and sat beside Frieren and ate her own, looking at the tree, with one hand in her pocket around something small. Milim came and lay down on the grass on the other side of the ring with her arms behind her head and fell asleep in the cold sun, snoring very slightly. Rimuru came in the afternoon, in slime form, and climbed into Frieren's lap without asking, the way he had once on the second night after the bay. She put her hand on him without looking up from the page.

She did not say anything to any of them, and they did not say anything to her, and that was all right.

The sun went across the sky, slowly. The tree's ten leaves turned a little to follow it.

Frieren read.

It was the first time in a thousand years she had waited for someone. Not by walking away, as she had walked away from a hero's funeral and kept walking for decades. Not by going ahead and letting time pass the way it passed for elves, in great careless handfuls, until whoever she was waiting for was gone. She waited by sitting still in one place, counting the hours, beside a tree that would take a hundred years to grow. She was the one who could not move. He was the one who was coming back.

She had always thought waiting would be the hard part.

It was the easiest thing she had ever done. It was only that it hurt.
