# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 17 — The Pages

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 17  
**Continuity state:** Day 7, dawn to late morning, the falls | the deer from the north trail | one line for each death he remembers: 211 | he burns the pages; Ciel doesn't read ('Thank you for asking first') | he doesn't look south

The deer was there when he woke.

It was standing at the edge of the second pool, below him, in the grey light before the sun came over the hills. One foreleg was lifted, as if it had stopped in the middle of a step. The rain had gone in the night. The pines dripped. Everything smelled of wet stone and resin and cold.

It was looking at him.

He knew it. He did not know how he knew it, only that he did, the way he had known Frieren's face on the first morning of his life. It was the same deer. The one he had seen twice in the trees on the north trail in the first month at the old inn, and decided, on no evidence whatsoever, was following him. Yuta had told him to stop talking about it. Frieren had said, *maybe it followed you home.* On the night of the chocolate, a year and a half later, he had told her he thought it had been her. And in the summer, it had stood in the road on the way to the sea, and the whole cart had stopped for it.

The north trail. He had walked north to get here.

It was here. In the one place he had chosen because nothing had ever happened in it.

Mau lay very still on the rock and did not move.

The deer looked at him for a long time. It did not seem afraid. It did not seem anything. It looked at him the way the pools looked at the sky. Then it put its lifted foreleg down, very carefully, on the stone, and bent its head and drank from the second pool. When it had finished, it looked at him once more and walked away up the slope into the pines, unhurried, and was gone.

He did not try to follow it. He did not try to understand it. He had learned, in four hundred and thirty-seven, that some things in front of you are not puzzles. They are only there.

He lay on the rock and watched the place where it had been until the sun came up.



Then he opened the backpack.

At the bottom, under the bread and the second pear and the lamp oil, was a notebook. Not the one from the memory crisis, the small one he had carried in his pocket for weeks to check whether things had happened. That one was on the shelf at home now, where Fern had put it on the day the repair stopped. This was a different one. Plain, unlined, bought from a stall in the plaza months ago and never written in. He had packed it without thinking, the way he had packed everything in the fifteen minutes.

He sat with it on his knees for a long time.

Then he took out the pencil and began to write.



He wrote one line for each death he could remember.

Not names. Not what had happened. He did not want to write what had happened; he could not have. Only a line. A few words. Enough to say: *this one happened. I was there. I remember.*

*The bridge, the first time.*

*The plaza in winter. Snow.*

*The old inn, the stairs, Rimuru.*

*The white room. Two chairs.*

*The field with Maki. Annoying.*

*The sea. Ori.*

He wrote slowly. Some of them came easily, one after another, as if they had been waiting in a line to be written down. Some he had to sit with for a long time before they would come at all. Some he started and could not finish, and left a line half-written, and went on to the next.

He did not count as he went. He had stopped counting a long time ago.

The sun went up over the hills and across the morning. His hand cramped and he shook it out and went on. The pages filled. He turned them and went on. The pencil wore down and he took out the little knife from the backpack and sharpened it, and went on.

At some point, he did not know when, he realized he was crying. He did not stop to do anything about it. He let the tears go down his face and drip off his chin onto the pages, and he wrote around the wet places.

They were the only water that had touched him in two days.



When he could not remember any more, he stopped.

He turned back to the first page and counted the lines. He did it carefully, with his finger, the way Nano counted ballots.

Two hundred and eleven.

That was all he could find. Two hundred and eleven out of four hundred and thirty-seven. The rest were gone. Not gone the way the false memories had been gone, in the crisis, broken and rethreaded and uncertain. Gone the way a day is gone when you sleep through it. They had happened. He had been there. He simply could not reach them anymore.

He sat and looked at the number for a long time.

Two hundred and twenty-six times he had died and could not remember how.

He did not know whether that was a mercy. He thought, perhaps, it was the only one the Trial had ever given him.



He built a small fire on the flat rock, out of dry pine twigs from under the trees where the rain had not reached. He lit it with the lamp. It took a while to catch. When it did, it burned small and clear and almost without smoke, the way Wakana had taught them all to build a fire on the first winter, at the old inn.

He tore the pages out of the notebook one at a time and fed them to it.

He did not read them as they went. He had read them once, writing them. That was enough. He watched each one curl and brown and go up in a bright edge of flame and turn to grey and break. Then the next. *The bridge, the first time.* Then the next. *The white room. Two chairs.*

"Ciel," he said.

*I am here.*

"Are you reading these?"

*No.* A pause. *They are not the guardrails. You did not ask me to. I am not looking. Rule four.*

"You can," said Mau. "If you want. I'm asking."

A longer pause.

*No,* said Ciel. *I counted them once. I do not need to count them again.* Another pause, and her voice changed, very slightly. *Thank you for asking first.*

He fed the last page to the fire.

It burned. It went grey. It broke apart and lifted on the warm air and drifted out over the highest pool, a few flakes of ash, and settled on the water, and was carried over the lip of the stone and down the falls, into the second pool where the deer had drunk, and the third, and on down the long stair toward the river, and the settlement, and home.

He sat and watched them go.

From the flat rock, if he turned his head and looked south over the shoulder of the hill, he could see a long way. Past the falls and the river and the roofs of the settlement, small and grey in the morning, to the far edge of the valley, where the road south ran into the trees toward a clearing he would not go to.

He did not turn his head.

He watched the ash go down the falls instead, until there was none left, and the fire had burned down to nothing, and the notebook in his lap was empty again.
