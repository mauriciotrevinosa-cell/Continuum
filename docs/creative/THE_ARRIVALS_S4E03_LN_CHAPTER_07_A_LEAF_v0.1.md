# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 18 — A Leaf

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 18  
**Continuity state:** Day 7, afternoon | he stops tearing at it and tells it: 'over, real, here' | a leaf on the back of his left hand | water closes over his fingers | Ciel: 'It had to hear it from you' | he starts home before evening

In the middle of the day, he stopped trying.

He had tried all morning, after the fire. He had tried every way he could think of, every way he had tried the day before and some new ones, to put the Infinity down. He had held his hand in his mind as precisely as he could. He had imagined it as a coat, a door, a shell, a breath he was holding and could let go. He had asked it, politely and then not politely. He had shouted at it, once, alone on the rock, so loudly that a bird went up out of the pines in alarm.

It did not notice him.

So at midday, with his head aching and his hands shaking, he stopped. Not because he had given up. Because he had remembered something.

He had remembered Maki, kneeling on the floor of the common room with her hands on her own knees, where he could see them. Not touching him. Close enough to be there, far enough not to be another thing coming at him. *Look at me. Breathe with me. You're just doing it too fast.*

He had been trying to tear it off. In the clearing, at the wedding, on the floor of the common room, here. He had been fighting it the way you fight something that has hold of you.

It did not have hold of him. It was *his*. It had come out of him because something in him had decided, four hundred and thirty-seven times over, that anything reaching for him was the thing that would end it. A hand, a spell, the sky, the rain. Anything. It was not attacking him. It was guarding him. It was doing exactly what he had taught it, in there, over and over, with every death: *nothing gets in.*

You could not tear off something that thought it was keeping you alive.

You had to tell it.



So he sat on the flat rock in the sun, with his back against the backpack, and did nothing at all.

He breathed. In, slowly. Out. The way Maki had counted it. He did not reach for the Infinity, or push at it, or ask it for anything. He let it be there, a finger's width from his skin all over, perfect and patient.

And he talked to it.

Not out loud. Inside, in the place where he had once held the walls in his mind for Ciel, very precisely, and asked for a door.

*It's over,* he told it.

*It's over. I know you don't believe me. I know you've heard that before, in there. You heard me say it to myself a hundred times, a thousand, and then something came out of the trees. I know.*

*But it's over. This is real. That deer was real. The rain was real. The fire was real. I counted the sunset yesterday and it didn't come back. I slept, and I woke up here, and nothing started again.*

*Nothing out here wants to kill me. Not today. The things that reach for me out here are hands. Her hands. Ori's. Maki's. They're reaching to hold on, not to take.*

*You can stop now. You did your job. You kept everything out. You kept me alive, four hundred and thirty-seven times, and I know it didn't feel like it. I know you don't know how to stop. Neither did I.*

*You can stop now.*

He breathed.

In. Out.

The Infinity did not change.

He did not push it. He sat there in the sun and kept breathing, and kept telling it, over and over, not in words anymore, only in the shape of the thing: *over. real. here.*

The afternoon went on. The shadows of the pines moved across the rock. A wind came up the valley from the river, smelling of home.



The leaf came at the end of the afternoon.

He did not see it fall. It was a small brown leaf from one of the alder bushes by the pool, loosened by the wind, turning over and over in the air. It came down out of nowhere, the way leaves do at the end of autumn, the way a hundred others had come down that day and stopped a finger's width above him and slid away.

It landed on the back of his left hand.

He felt it.

He felt it the way you feel a thing for the first time: completely, all at once, with his whole body. A small dry weight, no more than a breath, settling on his skin. The rough edge of it. The faint cold of the wind still in it.

He did not move.

He sat on the rock and looked at the leaf on the back of his hand, and did not breathe, in case breathing made it go.

It did not go.

It lay there on the back of his left hand, on the place where, in some of the memories, there was a scar he did not have yet.

Then the wind came up the valley again, and another leaf came down, and another. They landed on his knees, his arm, his hair. A drop of water, shaken from a pine, landed on his cheek and ran down it, and he felt it the whole way.

He started to cry.

He did not do anything about it this time either. He sat on the flat rock above the highest pool, with leaves landing on him, and cried. It was not the way he had cried on the road, or on the floor of the common room, or over the pages that morning. It was a different kind, the kind that is mostly relief, and comes out of you like water out of a spring that has been blocked a long time.

He put out his hand, slowly, toward the pool. He let it go down into the water.

The water was very cold. It closed over his fingers, around them, against them. All the way.

He laughed, and cried, and held his hand in the freezing water until it ached, because it ached.



"Ciel," he said, when he could.

*I am here.*

"It's down."

*I know. I felt it.* A pause. *You did not tear it. You told it.*

"Yes."

*I would like it noted,* said Ciel, *that I could not have taught it that. It had to hear it from you.*

Mau wiped his face with his wet hand, and felt the wet.

"It's still there, isn't it," he said. "Somewhere. It'll come back. If something reaches for me again, the wrong way."

*Yes,* said Ciel. *It is yours now. It learned too well to forget. But now it knows the difference. Or it has started to.* A pause. *That is more than most things know.*

Mau looked at the sun. It was low over the pines, but not yet down. A few hours of light left.

He did not wait for the evening. He did not wait for the morning of the third day, either, which the contract allowed. He had said *the night of the second day, or the morning of the third*. He had meant it as a window, a promise with room in it, because he had not known whether two days would be enough.

It was enough. It was more than enough. He could feel the cold in his hand, and the wind on his face, and his own wet hair, and he did not think he could stand to be away from them for one more night.

He packed the backpack. The empty notebook. The lamp. The last dried pear, which he did not eat, because it was for someone. He kicked the ashes of the little fire into the pool and watched the last of them go over the lip of the stone.

Then he stood on the flat rock and looked around once more, at the place where he had never died.

"Thank you," he said, to nobody. To the pools, the pines, the place at the edge of the second pool where the deer had stood. To the rock.

Then he climbed down and started home.
