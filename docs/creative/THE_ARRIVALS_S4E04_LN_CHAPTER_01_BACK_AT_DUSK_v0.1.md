# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 19 — Back at Dusk

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 19  
**Continuity state:** Evening of Day 7, a night early | Frieren by the ink tree; he holds out his hand first: 'It's down. Try.' | Rimuru: 'Here. He means it literally.' | Ori returns the stone: 'It counts.' | the whole house, one at a time; Maomao: 'Acceptable'

He came down the river path at dusk with the backpack on his shoulder and his hair still wet.

He had walked fast, faster than he had walked out. Coming down was easier than going up: over the stones and through the brush, past the stepping stones, onto the path proper with the river loud beside him. When the roofs of the settlement came into sight over the last rise, small and grey in the falling light, he stopped. He stood there for a long moment and looked at them.

Nothing reset.

He kept walking.



He did not go to the front door. The river path came into the settlement from the north, past the washing court and along the side of the Muslin, and the side door was nearer. Beside the side door was the garden, and in the garden was a small ring of stakes, and in the ring was a tree.

Frieren was sitting on a blanket beside it.

She had been there all day. He could tell from the way she was sitting, the way you sit when you have been in one place long enough that your body has stopped noticing it. The grimoire was open on her knees. The light was nearly gone; she could not possibly have been reading it. She was looking at the tree.

He stopped at the edge of the garden.

She looked up.

He saw her see him. He saw it go through her: the stillness first, the way she went still at the edge of something, and then her face, all of it at once. She put the grimoire down on the blanket very carefully and stood up.

She did not come toward him.

Her hands came up a little way from her sides, the way they had in the common room, and stopped. She did not reach. He understood. She was afraid to. She was afraid of the finger's width. She was afraid of watching his face when her hands stopped short of him again. Afraid of being *her*, the false one, in the cut flowers, with her hand held up flat against nothing.

So he went to her.

He crossed the garden. He stopped in front of her, close, the way Maki had knelt in front of him: close enough to be there. And he held out his left hand, palm down, slowly, telegraphing every inch of it, between them.

"It's down," said Mau. "I think. Try."

Frieren looked at his hand.

She lifted her own, very slowly. Her fingers were shaking. They came toward the back of his hand, and he watched them come, and something in him went tight, the old tightness, *something reaching*, and he breathed. In. Out. *Over. Real. Here.*

Her fingertips touched the back of his hand.

They did not stop. They did not stop a finger's width short. They touched his skin, cold from the river, and stayed there.

Frieren made a sound he had never heard her make.

Then she was holding him. Both arms, all of her, her face in his shoulder and her hands fisted in the back of his coat, holding on as if he were the last solid thing in a world that kept dissolving. He held her back. He could feel her. All of her. The cold of her cheek, her hair, the shaking going through her, her breath on his neck. Every bit of it reached him.

"You're wet," said Frieren into his shoulder. Her voice was destroyed.

"I know."

"Your hair's wet."

"I put my head in the river," said Mau. "On purpose. I wanted to feel it."

Frieren made the sound again. It might have been a laugh.



Inside the Muslin, in the common room, the lamps had just been lit.

Everyone was there. Nobody had called a meeting. They had come at the hour when the lamps went on, as they had the night before, and sat down, and not talked very much, and waited.

Rimuru was at the cold hearth.

His eyes went somewhere else.

The room stopped.

"*Here*," said Rimuru.

And then his face changed, in a way it had not changed the night before, and he stood up so fast his bowl fell off his knees.

"She says," said Rimuru, "*here*. She says, *he means it literally*."

At the south window of the fort, Ori had already turned around. She had not been looking down the corridor. She had been looking out the side window, the one in the fort's annex that faced the garden, which nobody had thought of as a window until that moment. She was standing up. She was already moving.

"He's in the garden," said Ori.

The common room emptied.



They came out of the side door all at once, the whole house. The fort first, because the fort was nearest, then everyone else, in a crush in the narrow doorway, and stopped dead at the edge of the garden.

Frieren was still holding him. She did not let go. She did not turn around. She stood with her arms around him and her face in his shoulder, and he stood with his arms around her and looked at them all over her head, the whole house, crowded at the edge of the garden in the last of the light.

"Hi," said Mau.

Nobody moved.

They were afraid. He could see it. They were afraid of the finger's width, every one of them. They had all seen Frieren's hands stop in the common room, and Milim's, and they were standing at the edge of the garden not knowing whether, if they came closer, they would stop too.

"It's down," said Mau. "You can—" He stopped. His voice was going. "It's all right. I promise. It's down."

Ori came first.

She crossed the garden in the dusk without hurrying. She stopped in front of him and Frieren. She did not reach for him. She held out her hand instead, closed, and opened it.

The stone was in her palm. Grey, with a white band through it.

Mau looked at it.

"You told me," said Ori. "Two nights ago. On the floor. Everything. You told me first." Her voice was very steady. "It was late. It was so late. But it counts."

He took his arm from around Frieren, carefully, just one arm, and held out his hand.

Ori put the stone in it. Her fingers touched his palm, and stayed there a moment, and did not stop.

Then she put her arms around both of them at once, Frieren and him together, and held on.

After that it was everyone.

One at a time, at first, because he asked them to: *one at a time, please, I'm still learning*. Then not one at a time at all. Umaru in her sash. Coco with her hat crushed in her hand. Bocchi, who did not say anything and did not need to. Milim, who picked up all of them at once, him and Frieren and Ori and Umaru and Coco together, a whole knot of people lifted clean off the ground, and held them up in the air and cried so loudly that the refuge across the plaza heard it and came to their doors. Rimuru, in slime form, who climbed straight up the side of the knot and settled on Mau's head and did not get off. Yuta, who came last of the first wave, and stood at the edge of it, and put one hand flat on Mau's back, between his shoulder blades, and left it there.

And it let them. All of them. It let every one of them in.

Somewhere in the middle of it, someone pushed through, very determinedly, with two fingers held out.

It was Maomao.

She found his wrist inside the crush, took his pulse, counted, and frowned.

"Too fast," she said. "Much too fast." She looked up at him, at his wet hair and his wrecked face and the slime on his head and the six people holding on to him, and something in her own face gave way, very slightly, for perhaps half a second. "Acceptable," she said. "Under the circumstances. Acceptable."

She did not let go of his wrist.



Afterward, much later, when they had all gone inside and eaten Momo's second batch of bread, which turned out to have been for exactly this, Mau stood in the room with the east window and put the stone back on the windowsill.

In the clean square in the dust, exactly where it had been.

Frieren watched him do it from the bed.

"You came back early," she said.

"The contract said the night of the second day. Or the morning of the third."

"It's the evening of the second day."

"It was enough," said Mau. "Two days was enough." He turned around. "And I couldn't stand one more night of not being able to feel anything."

Frieren held out her hand.

He took it.

It did not stop. It did not stop at all.
