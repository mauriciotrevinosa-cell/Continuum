# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 20 — The Corner

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 20  
**Continuity state:** Day 8, morning, inside the fort for the first time | the Guardian: 'Which one?' / 'The idiot who accepted.' / 'Pass.' | Umaru: is it down? 'No... Could you carry a corner?' | Bocchi hands him her guitar: 'That's the difference.'

In the morning, the President of the Fort summoned him again.

It came through Suika, as before, at the door, in her most official voice. This time the summons said he was to come to the south window *and was permitted to come in*.

He went.

He had never been inside the new fort. He had heard it being built through two walls, and followed its election through Rem. He had looked through its south window twice, from the outside, crouching, while the President asked him questions he could not answer. He had never been inside.

He went in through the curtain, past the toll booth with its sign. TOLL: SNACKS. TOLL FOR REM: NONE. TOLL FOR WHOEVER COMES NEXT: ALSO NONE. Suika, at the booth, looked at him with great seriousness and held out her hand.

He reached into his pocket. He had brought the last dried pear from the backpack, because he had known, somehow, that he would need a toll.

Suika took it, inspected it, and put it in the booth.

"Accepted," she said.

Inside the curtain, under the tall peak of the roof, Milim was standing with her arms folded, in a sash of her own that said GUARDIAN. She looked down at him with enormous solemnity.

"Who are you?" said Milim.

He had known her for two years. She had picked him up and his whole family with him in the garden the night before and cried so loudly the refuge heard.

"Mau," he said.

"Which one?"

He thought about it.

"The idiot who accepted," he said.

Milim considered this for a long moment, frowning, as if it were a very difficult case. Then she reached out and patted him on the head, once, firmly.

"Pass," said the Guardian of the Fort.



The fort was bigger inside than it looked. Wakana's frame of slats, pegged without nails. Marin's curtains in every doorway. Kusuri's little jars, dark now in the morning. The hamster flag on its broom handle, just visible through a gap in the roof. The stage corner, with Ryo asleep on it. The annex, with its curtain closed, and six cushions behind it that he could not see but knew were there.

The constitution was pinned to the inside of the toll booth. He read it on the way past. *Article 6. Nobody in the fort is ever alone.*

Umaru was sitting on her cushion by the south window, in her hood and her PRESIDENT (TEMPORARY) sash, with her hands folded on her knees. Agott was beside her in OPPOSITION. Bocchi was sitting a little way off, against the north wall, with her guitar across her knees, not playing it.

He sat down where Umaru pointed, on an ordinary cushion, facing her.

"The President," said Umaru, "has a follow-up question."

"Okay."

"It's the same question." She looked at him. "You promised you'd put it down. At the ceremony. In front of everyone. *If it gets too heavy, you'll put it down, and let us carry it for a while.* And you said *I promise*."

"I did."

"And then you didn't. You went down the road with it. By yourself." Her hands tightened on her knees. "And two nights ago you put it down. On the floor of the common room. Some of it. And then you picked it straight back up and carried it upriver." She took a breath. "So the President's question is: is it down? Now? Or are you still carrying it?"

Mau thought about it for a long time.

He thought about two hundred and eleven lines in a notebook, burned on a rock. About two hundred and twenty-six more he could not reach. About a coat of nothing a finger's width thick that had come down when he stopped tearing at it, and would come back, Ciel said, if something reached for him the wrong way. About the number Rimuru had said, and the one he could see in Rimuru's face that Rimuru had not said.

"No," he said honestly. "It's not down. Not all of it. I don't think it's ever going to be all of it." He looked at her. "It's too big to put down all at once. I tried. That's what the two days were. I can't."

Umaru's face did not change.

"That's the wrong answer," she said.

"I know." He hesitated. Then, slowly, he held out both his hands, palms up, between them, the way you would hold out something heavy that you were about to set down on a table. "But I was thinking. On the walk back." He swallowed. "Could you carry a corner?"

Umaru looked at his hands.

"A corner?"

"Just one. A corner of it. I'll carry the rest. But if somebody carries a corner, then it's not—" He stopped. "Then it's not one person carrying it. Then it's not in front. Then it's *between*."

The fort was very quiet.

Umaru looked at his open hands for a long time.

Then she reached out with her own small hand and took hold of the very tip of his left forefinger, firmly, between her finger and thumb, as if it were the corner of something.

"The President," said Umaru, "will carry a corner."

Agott, without a word, reached out and took the tip of his right forefinger.

"The Opposition," she said, "will carry the other one. Somebody has to keep an eye on the President."

He laughed. It came out of him wet and surprised. They held on to his fingertips, the two of them, very seriously, for a long moment. Then Umaru let go and folded her hands back on her knees.

"That's acceptable," she said. "For now. The President will reconsider at the full moon."



Bocchi had not moved from the north wall.

When Umaru and Agott let go, Mau looked over at her. She was looking at her guitar, her hair over her face, her fingers on the strings not playing them.

He went over and sat down next to her on the floor. Not close. Close enough.

"You asked me something," he said. "Outside the music room. Before."

Bocchi's fingers went still on the strings.

"You asked if I knew the difference," said Mau. "Between being loved and being needed."

She did not look at him.

"I didn't," he said. "You were right. I didn't know. I thought they were the same thing. I thought if you needed me, that was why you loved me, and if you stopped needing me, you'd—" He stopped. "In there, I watched what happened when you didn't need me. Hundreds of times. I thought that was the test. Whether you'd still—" He shook his head. "It doesn't matter what I thought. You were right. I didn't know."

Bocchi's hands were shaking a little on the guitar.

"D-do you," she said. "Now?"

Mau thought about it.

"I'm learning," he said. "I think. Yesterday the whole house came out into the garden in the dark, and nobody needed anything from me. Nobody. I'd just been gone for two days doing nothing on a rock. I couldn't do anything for anybody. And they came out anyway." He looked at his hands. "So I think I'm learning."

Bocchi was quiet for a long moment.

Then, very carefully, without looking at him, she turned the guitar on her knees and held it out to him.

He did not understand.

"H-hold it," said Bocchi. "For a minute."

He took it. It was heavier than he expected, and warm from her hands.

Bocchi put her own hands in her lap, empty, and sat there beside him without her guitar, which nobody in the house had ever seen her do in front of anyone. Not once. She sat there with nothing in her hands, shaking a little, and leaned her shoulder against his.

"That's the difference," said Bocchi. "That's what it's like."

He sat in the fort holding her guitar, with her shoulder against his. Around them twenty-two citizens very carefully did not watch, and the President of the Fort looked at the ceiling with an expression of great dignity, and wiped her face with the end of her sash.
