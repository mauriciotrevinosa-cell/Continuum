## Chapter 23 — Frieren's Terms

**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 23

On the ninth night, Frieren told him her terms.

He had not asked. That was the thing. He had very carefully not asked, for nine days. *New skill*, he had said, at the end of the third evening, and she had said *very advanced*, and he had kept it up through the anger and the talk and the river and the roof. He had not asked what she would do, or what the Goddess had offered her, or whether, in the room with two chairs and a door, she was going to stand up.

She told him anyway.

They were in the room with the east window, in the dark, in the bed. The stone was on the windowsill. The ribbon was in her pocket, not on his ankle, because he had not floated since the rock. Frieren lay on her side facing him with her head on her arm, the way she did when she was going to say something she had decided on in advance.

"I went for my terms on the first day," she said.

Mau did not say anything.

"Day one. Before you were writing notes at three in the morning. Before the road." She looked at him in the dark. "I went into Arrival House with Fern as my witness and asked for both. Return, and Stay. And I read them."

"You don't have to tell me."

"I know I don't have to. That's why I'm telling you." She was quiet for a moment. "Return would have put me back where I was. In my world. On the road north, the way it was the morning I came through. All of it. And this would have become—" She stopped. "The Goddess wrote *dreamlike*. In my terms. *Continuum may become dreamlike in recollection.* She wrote it specially, I think. For me. Because she knew what it would mean."

Mau did not move.

"A thousand years," said Frieren, "I've been forgetting people. Not on purpose. Elves do. Someone is important, and then a few decades go by, and they're a name, and then a face, and then a feeling I can't place. I walked away from Himmel's funeral and kept walking for decades, and I barely remembered his face by the time I understood what I'd lost." She paused. "I would have forgotten you. In a year, maybe. Two. I would have been on the road north with Fern, and some night by the fire I would have had a dream about a boy in a clearing with a backpack, and I wouldn't have known who he was."

"Frieren."

"So I chose," said Frieren. "On the first day. I chose Stay. I put my name to it in front of Fern, the way you put your name to things, so that it was mine." She looked at him. "Before you ever wrote a note."

Mau went very still.

"That's the part you need to hear," said Frieren. "Not that I'm staying. You hoped I was. You told me so, in this bed, and I told you that you were allowed to hope it." Her voice was very steady. "The part you need to hear is that I'd already chosen. I chose on the first day. And on the third day, at three in the morning, you sat at that table and wrote a note about asking the Goddess to send everyone home together, so nobody would have to choose."

He understood.

"I'd already chosen," said Frieren. "You were going to take it from me anyway. Not because you didn't love me. Because you'd watched me die in four hundred and thirty-seven different ways, and in one of them I told you that if it weren't for you, I'd still be alive." She did not look away. "And you believed her. Over me. Over the one who'd already chosen."

"I know," said Mau. His voice had gone. "I know. I'm sorry."

"I know you're sorry." She reached out in the dark and put her hand flat on his chest, where the false one's hand had stopped a finger's width away. It did not stop. It rested there. "I'm not telling you so that you'll be sorry. I'm telling you so that next time, when something in you says she was right, you'll remember that I chose first. On the first day. Before you could stand in front of the door."



They lay there for a long time.

"There's something else," said Frieren.

"Okay."

"The night of the bay," said Frieren.

He went still.

"That's the rule," said Frieren. "In Fern's ledger. Nobody speaks of it unless you do, or I do." Her hand stayed on his chest. "I'm doing."

He did not say anything. He waited.

"That night," said Frieren. "When it started. I was lying on you, and I put my hand down on the bed, and it was wet. And I didn't scream. I couldn't find it." She was quiet. "And afterward, after Rimuru took you to the bay, I was on the floor beside the bed. And I couldn't breathe. It went short and high and wouldn't go anywhere. Like yours did. On the floor of the common room, the other night."

Mau could not speak.

"Maki came," said Frieren. "She knelt in front of me, close, but she didn't touch me. She put her hands on her knees where I could see them. And she said, *Look at me. Not at your hands. At me. Breathe with me.*" She paused. "I never told anyone how I came back that night. I didn't know how to say it. And then it was in the ledger that nobody speaks of it, so nobody asked."

Her hand pressed a little harder on his chest.

"And the other night," said Frieren, "I sat on the couch and watched her kneel in front of you, with her hands on her knees, and say the same words. And bring you back the same way she brought me back." She breathed out. "Both of us. She's brought both of us back from the same place. With the same words. On the same floor, nearly."

Mau put his hand over hers on his chest.

"Both of us," he said.

"Both of us," said Frieren. "That's what you said. In this bed. *If I live, and you live. Both of us. That's the only way it counts.*" She moved closer, so that her forehead was against his. "So that's my terms. That's what I'm telling you. I stayed. On the first day. For both of us. And you're not allowed to decide, ever again, that only one of us counts."

"I know."

"Say it."

"Both of us," said Mau. "Not one. Both."

"Again."

He almost laughed. It was what she did, in the room with the door shut. *Say it again.* He had not heard her ask him that since the night of the bay.

"Both of us," said Mau. "I promise. Again."

"You can't take it back."

"I know."

"You said that last time."

"I meant it last time," said Mau. "I just didn't know what it cost yet. I do now."

Frieren did not say anything else. She kept her forehead against his and her hand on his chest. After a while her breathing slowed and went even, and she slept.

He lay awake a little longer, with his hand over hers, feeling her breathe.

Then he slept too. He did not float.
