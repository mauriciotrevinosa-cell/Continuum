On the last two days of the window, everyone considered it.

That was the thing Mau would understand afterward, looking back. Nobody in the house pretended the door was not there. Nobody said *of course I'm staying* the way you say a thing you have never once let yourself think about. Every one of them, in their own way, on their own time, went and stood in front of the door and looked through it.

He did not ask any of them. He had promised. *New skill.* But he saw some of it. Some of it they showed him on purpose.



Eren stood on the road south.

He went at dawn on the ninth day, alone, and stood on the road where they had caught Mau in the dark. He looked down it for a long time, toward the trees and the first clearing beyond them, though he had no intention of going there. Mikasa found him and stood beside him. She did not say anything.

"I read my terms," said Eren eventually. "Twice."

"I know."

"I could go back. To before." He was quiet. "To before I did any of it. The terms said *personal*. They said I could go back to the moment I came through. With everything I know now." He looked at his hands. "I could go back and stop it. All of it. Before it starts."

Mikasa did not move.

"Or," said Eren, "I could go back and do it anyway. Because I'd be back there, with everything pulling the way it pulled, and I'd be *me*." He shook his head slowly. "I don't know which. That's the thing. Here, I haven't done it. Here, I'm just a person who got in line for blood and got turned away, and stood against a wall with Kaneki all night. Here, I'm a person who was voted through a door." He looked at the road. "There, I'm the person who's going to do it."

"And?" said Mikasa.

Eren turned his back on the road south.

"And I'd rather be the one who got in line," he said.

They walked back up to the settlement together. Mikasa did not say anything the whole way. At the top of the rise, without looking at him, she took his hand, and he let her.



Bocchi sat in the music room with her guitar case open on the floor in front of her.

She did not put the guitar in it. She sat on the floor and looked at the open case for most of a morning, with the guitar across her knees. Kita came in at some point and sat beside her without asking, and looked at the case too.

"Your terms said you could go home," said Kita. "To your family. Your parents. Futari."

"Yes."

"And the band. The real band. The house. The stage."

"Yes."

Kita waited.

"I miss them," said Bocchi, to the case. "I miss them so much. I didn't know I did until the Goddess said *Return*, and then I couldn't stop."

"I know," said Kita. "Me too."

Bocchi looked at her.

"You're here," said Bocchi. "Nijika's here. Ryo's here." She swallowed. "Mau's here. He's my brother. I told him. I said it out loud." She looked at the case for a long moment more. "If I went back, I'd have a family that doesn't know any of that. That doesn't know I said it."

She reached out and closed the guitar case, empty.

Then she picked up the guitar and played, and Kita listened, and after a while Nijika came and sat in the doorway, and Ryo came and lay down on the floor. None of them said anything about the case, and none of them put anything in it.



Kaneki stood on the bridge.

He went at dusk on the ninth day, and stood in the middle, where the river was loudest. He looked down at the water. Eren found him there, because Eren had started finding him, since the night against the wall. He stood beside him, leaning on the rail.

"You read yours," said Eren.

"Yes."

"Going?"

Kaneki was quiet for a long time.

"There's someone," he said finally. "In my world. My wife. Touka." He said the name the way you set down something fragile. "I don't know what she thinks happened to me. In my terms, the Goddess didn't say. *Personal.* I could go back and find out."

Eren waited.

"Or I could stay," said Kaneki, "and ask for her. The petition. One person. Someone you love." He looked at the water. "She said a petition can't override what they want. So if I asked for Touka, and she didn't want to come, she wouldn't. And if she did want to—" He stopped.

"Then she'd come here," said Eren. "Where you are. Where you're not a monster everybody's afraid of. Where you're just a guy who stood against a wall all night so his blood wouldn't hurt anyone."

Kaneki did not answer. But after a while he took his hands off the rail, and turned away from the water, and walked back toward the settlement. Eren walked beside him.

"Don't tell anyone," said Kaneki. "Her name."

"I won't."

He told nobody. He was the only person in the settlement who knew, until much later, whose name Kaneki would someday write.



Coco went to Qifrey.

She did it on the morning of the tenth day, the last day, because she had not been able to make herself do it any sooner. She found him in the atelier, alone, sharpening pens, and stood in the doorway with her hat crushed in her hands.

"My terms," she said. "The Return ones. They said I could go back to before."

Qifrey put down the pen.

"Before the—" Coco could not say it. "Before my mother. Before I drew the circle I shouldn't have drawn. I could go back to the morning before, and not draw it, and she'd be— she'd be all right. She'd never—"

Her voice gave out.

Qifrey did not say anything. He got up and came across the atelier and knelt in front of her, the way he knelt in front of an apprentice about to draw something difficult, so that his face was level with hers.

"And?" he said gently.

"And then I'd never have met you," said Coco. "I'd never have been an apprentice. I'd never have met Agott, or Tetia, or Richeh. I'd never have come here. I'd never have met Mau, or the fort, or Umaru, or—" She was crying now. "I'd have my mother, and I'd never have had any of you, and you'd all still be here, and I wouldn't even *know*."

Qifrey waited.

"And if I stay," said Coco, "I can ask for her. The petition. But Mau said— *as she was*. The Goddess said not guaranteed. I'm scared it would bring her *as she was*. Still—" She could not finish. "Still like that. Here. And I'd have to look at her every day and know I did it twice."

Qifrey put his hands on her shoulders.

"You don't have to decide the petition today," he said. "Only the door. Only whether you stay."

Coco looked at him for a long time.

"I'm staying," she said. "I already knew. I just needed to say it to someone who'd understand what it cost."

"I understand what it cost," said Qifrey.

He did. She could see in his face that he did.

"And the petition," said Coco, "I'll wait. Until I know more. Until there's a way to know whether she'd come back *better*." She wiped her face with her hat. "Until I find out."

Qifrey nodded slowly.

"Then we'll find out together," he said. "There's a great deal of knowledge in the world, Coco. Some of it is in places nobody's ever thought to look."



The rest were smaller. Mau saw them in pieces, the way you see a crowd from a window.

Milim did not go for her terms at all. When Rimuru asked her why, she looked at him as if he had said something very stupid. "My best friend is here," she said. "Where would I go?" And that was the end of it.

Diablo did not go for his terms either. When Anko, with professional curiosity, asked him whether he had considered it, he looked at her for a long moment with an expression of genuine bewilderment. "*Return*," he repeated, as if trying the word in an unfamiliar language. "To a world in which Rimuru-sama is not present." He smiled very slightly. "No."

Rimuru went for his. He went with Ciel, at noon on the ninth day, into Arrival House, and came out an hour later and sat on the step for a long time. Mau, crossing the plaza, saw him there and did not go over. Later, on the roof, Rimuru said only: "They'll be all right. Tempest. They have each other. They have Benimaru." He looked out at the dark. "There's nobody there who knows what loops cost. There's somebody here who does." He did not say anything else, and Mau did not ask.

Aira had said *ask me in a week*, on the second day of the window. On the evening of the ninth, at dinner, without anyone asking, she put down her spoon and announced to the whole table: "Staying." Then she picked her spoon up again and went on eating. Nobody said anything, because she would have hated it.

Rem had asked, in front of everyone, whether a petition could concern the dead. The Goddess had said *some*. Rem went for her terms on the eighth day with Ori as her witness, and came out very quiet. That evening she sat in the fort between Ori and Coco and did not say anything. When Ori asked, gently, she said only: "Not this time." Then she poured the tea. She did not cry. She was staying. She had decided that a long time ago, on a night with a toll sign and a cup on a hook, without deciding anything at all.

The cooper from the river towns came to the house on the tenth morning and asked for Mau. He stood at the door with his hat in his hands.

"I'm staying," he said. "Thought you should know. You, specifically." He turned the hat around once. "There's a petition, if you stay. One person. Someone you love." He looked at Mau. "My daughter'd be eleven now, near enough. If time runs the same there. Nobody knows. I'd rather she came to a place like this than I went back to a place like that." He paused. "I haven't decided when. Thought I'd find out more first. About whether it's safe. Whether she'd want to." He put his hat back on. "Anyway. It was mine to decide. I decided."

"Thank you," said Mau. "For telling me."

"Didn't tell you for your sake," said the cooper, not unkindly. "Told you because you asked, back at the inn, whether I'd go. Figured you deserved the answer."

Lise came to the old inn on the ninth day and found Rem in the kitchen and said, very quietly, with her hands knotted in her apron: "Nobody asked me if I wanted to go back. So I asked myself." She looked at the floor. "I don't."

Rem put the kettle on.

The witches did not come to the settlement at all on the last two days. But on the evening of the ninth, Rem found a single line written in the dust on the windowsill of the session room at the old inn, in a precise, unfamiliar hand:

*Return is a grave. We are staying. Tell him.*

She told him.



That was all of them. Every one, Mau thought later: the first ring, and the second, and the witches who were the whole of the third, and the people of the fourth who chose to stay. Every one of them went and stood at the door and looked through it, and saw something on the other side that they loved. And every one of them turned around.

Not for him. That was the thing he had to keep remembering, the thing Frieren had made him promise to remember. Not for him. For Kita, and for a case left empty. For a road where you had not done it yet. For a name you would write someday. For a teacher who understood what it cost. For a best friend, or a tree, or a toll sign, or a daughter who would be safer coming here.

For themselves.

They chose. Every one. And not one of them asked him first.
