## Chapter 25 — The Bridge

The ones who were leaving gathered at the bridge on the afternoon of the tenth day.

Nobody had told them to. Arrival House had said only that Return would happen at the close of the window, at sunset, for everyone who had given their final word. It would happen wherever they were standing. They could be in their beds, or the plaza, or the washing court. It did not matter.

They went to the bridge anyway. Most of them had come into the settlement over that bridge. It seemed right, somehow, to leave from it.

There were a great many of them.

More than Mau had thought. He had known, from Fern's ledger and Anko's counts and the line around the corner of Arrival House, that it would be a lot. Knowing was not the same as seeing them. They stood on the bridge and along the riverbank on either side, in the cold, in small groups and alone. Refuge families with children. Second-wave travelers who had come over in the summer. People who had arrived in the weeks after Last Defense because they had heard there was a place that took anyone. People who had heard, since the plaza, that the same place had a monster in it.

Most of them had their things with them. Packs, bundles, boxes. They did not need them. Arrival House had said: *as you are*. Whatever you are carrying when the window closes goes with you, or does not. *Personal terms.* They had packed anyway, because that is what people do when they are leaving.



Mau went down to the bridge.

The house had argued about it. Fern had said he did not have to. Anko had said it might be dangerous: there were people on that bridge who blamed him for the plaza, for Last Defense, for the rumors, for everything. Maki had said that was exactly why he should not go.

Mau had said: "Maomao's rule. From the council. I have to be seen." He had looked at the ledger. "*Mau is not protected from his own life.* You wrote it."

So he went. Frieren went with him, on his left, with her hand in his. Rimuru went on his right, in human form, with his hands in his coat pockets. Behind them, at a little distance, came most of the house, not as an escort, only because they wanted to be there.

They stood at the settlement end of the bridge.

The people on it looked at him.

Some of them looked away again at once. Some of them looked for a long time. A woman near the front of the crowd, with a child by the hand, looked at him and then deliberately turned her back. He knew her. She had been in the Arrival House line on the fourth day, holding her child's hand a little tighter when he passed.

He did not look away.



A man came off the bridge toward him.

He was young, and very angry, and Mau did not know his name. He stopped in front of Mau, close, closer than anyone had been except the house. Rimuru took his hands out of his pockets.

"You know why I'm going?" said the man.

Mau did not answer.

"Because of you," said the man. "Because of what's in your shadow. Because I was in the plaza that day, with the visitors, and I saw it come up out of the ground, and I ran, and I've been running in my sleep every night since." His voice shook. "The wheel. The black ball. The sky cutting the trees. You think we didn't see? We all saw. And then Last Defense, and people dead, and the whole valley saying *monster town*, and you, walking around the plaza washing shirts like nothing's wrong." He was nearly shouting now. "I came here to be *safe*. And you're the least safe thing I've ever seen."

The bridge had gone very quiet.

"I know," said Mau.

The man stared at him.

"I know," said Mau again. "You're right. I'm not safe. I'm sorry you were in the plaza. I'm sorry you've been running in your sleep." He held the man's eyes. "I hope where you're going, you sleep."

The man opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

Then he turned and went back onto the bridge, into the crowd, and did not look back.

Frieren's hand tightened on his.



Not all of them were like that.

A little later, an old woman came off the bridge, slowly, with a cane. Mau did not know her either. She stopped in front of him and looked up at him for a long time, the way old women look at young men they have decided something about.

"The summon," she said. "In your shadow."

"Yes."

"It never hurt anybody," said the old woman. "In the plaza. I was there. It came up, and it was terrible, and every single person got out. Every one. Because you held it." She sniffed. "Nobody says that part. In the river towns. They say *monster*, and they leave that part out."

Mau did not know what to say.

"I'm going home because my sister's alive, and she's old, and I want to see her before one of us goes," said the old woman. "Not because of you." She reached up and patted his cheek, briskly, the way you pat a child who has done something brave and stupid. "Thank you for holding it."

She went back onto the bridge.

Mau stood very still. Rimuru, beside him, put a hand on his shoulder and left it there.



The boy from the washing court was on the bridge too.

The small one, with frank enormous eyes, who had asked on Mau's first day out whether he was the one with the monster in his shadow, and whether he could see it, and whether he had been sick. His mother was the woman who had turned her back. She was holding his hand very tightly. She did not look at Mau.

The boy did.

He looked at Mau across the end of the bridge for a long moment. Then, while his mother was not looking, he lifted his free hand and waved. A small, quick wave, low down by his side, where she would not see.

Mau waved back.

The boy grinned.



The merchant came down to the bridge too, a little before sunset.

The native merchant who had moved his stall after the plaza. Who had said *I heard you were dead* and then pushed a third dried pear across the counter, *for the walk*. He was not leaving. He could not. Return was for the people who had come from somewhere else, and he had been born in the valley, and his father before him.

He stood beside Mau at the end of the bridge and watched them.

"A lot of my customers," he said.

"I know."

"They'll be missed." He was quiet. "Some of them."

"Yes."

The merchant looked at him sideways.

"You know what my father used to say?" he said. "When I was a boy, and a family moved out of the valley, and the market got quieter?"

"No."

"*Fewer mouths, more pears.*" The merchant almost smiled. "He was a hard man. But he wasn't wrong." He put his hands in his pockets. "The ones who stay are the ones who wanted to. That's a better market. In the end."

Mau looked at the crowd on the bridge, and then at the settlement behind him. The plaza. The washing court. The Muslin with its lit windows and the fort's flag on a broom handle. The house, standing at a little distance, waiting.

"Yes," he said. "It is."



Fern counted them, as the light went.

She stood at the settlement end of the bridge with the ledger open and wrote down every name she knew and a mark for every one she did not. Arrival House had their final words. She did not need to count. She counted anyway, because counting was what she did with things that mattered, the way Ciel did.

When she was finished she did not say the number out loud. She showed it to Rimuru, and Rimuru looked at it and did not say anything either.

Later, Mau would learn the number. It was large. It was nearly half of everyone who had ever come over the bridge since the settlement began. It was almost all of what the house had called Ring Four, at the council, in chalk: the comers and goers, the people who were near but not close, who had come for safety and stayed for a while.

Ring Four was going.

Not because anyone had decided it. Because the plaza had happened, and Last Defense, and seven rumors, and a wheel in a man's shadow, and a door had opened in the sky at the end of all of it. The ones who had only ever been passing through had walked through it.

"Anko," said Rimuru quietly, at the end of the bridge, watching. "Write this down. Ring Four."

Anko had her notebook out.

"What do I write?"

Rimuru was quiet for a long moment.

"That they were here," he said. "That's all. That they were here, and we were glad, and they went home."



The sun touched the hills.

On the bridge, the crowd went still. People stopped talking. Children stopped fidgeting. The woman with the boy held his hand very tightly. The angry young man stood with his back to the settlement and his face to the west.

Mau held Frieren's hand.

The light went red, and then gold, and then, all at once, the sun was gone behind the hills.

There was no sound. No light from the sky, no voice, no door. One moment the bridge was full of people in the cold, with their packs and their bundles and their children. The next, there was only the bridge, and the river loud beneath it, and the long grey shadow of the hills across the water.

Empty.

A pack lay in the middle of the bridge where someone had set it down. A child's mitten lay on the rail. That was all. Everything else they had been carrying had gone with them, or had not, according to terms nobody would ever read.

The window had closed.

The boy who had waved was gone.
