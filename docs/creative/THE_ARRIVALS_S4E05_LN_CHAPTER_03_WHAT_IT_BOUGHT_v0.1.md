# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 20
## Chapter 26 — What It Bought

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 20  
**Reading order:** 26  
**Continuity state:** Day 10, after sunset | the Goddess: one petition each, a coin; it does not expire; it may be spent, kept or given; 'It was purchased... by the one who stood in the Trial' | Frieren: 'Millennia.' | every hand closes | Mau's own coin: 'Everyone I love is already here'

They were still standing at the end of the empty bridge when the voice came.

It did not come from anywhere. That was how it had always come. There was no figure, no light, no face. Only a voice, very calm, from all around them at once: from the river and the cold air and the long shadow of the hills, and from the empty boards of the bridge where a hundred people had been standing a minute before.

Everyone in the settlement heard it. In the plaza, in the washing court, in the Muslin, in the old inn, in the forest cabin where the witches lived. Everyone.

"The window is closed," said the Goddess.

Mau's whole body went rigid. He could not help it. That voice had spoken to him in the white, at the end, in a clearing full of cut flowers with a dead woman in his arms, and his body remembered every word of it before his mind could tell it otherwise.

Frieren's hand tightened on his. He breathed. In. Out.

"Those who chose to return have returned," said the Goddess. "Those who remain, remain."

A pause. The river ran.

"To each who remains," said the Goddess, "one petition. As was said. For one loved person, within its bounds."

Mau felt something in his free hand.

He had not been holding anything. Now there was something in his palm, cold and then quickly warm. He looked down.

A coin.

It was small, the size of his thumbnail. It was made of something that was not quite silver, pale, with a faint sheen to it like the inside of a shell. There was nothing on it. No face, no mark, no writing. It was only a small, smooth, perfect disc, lying in the middle of his palm, warm as if someone had been holding it.

Around him, all down the end of the bridge, he heard people make small sounds of surprise. Frieren, beside him, opened her own hand and looked at what was in it. Rimuru took his hand out of his coat pocket with a coin between two fingers and turned it over. Behind them, the house was looking at their palms. Coco had dropped hers and was scrabbling for it on the boards. Milim was holding hers up to the last of the light with an expression of total wonder.

"It does not expire," said the Goddess. "It may be spent. It may be kept. It may be given."

A pause.

"It was purchased," said the Goddess.



Nobody moved.

"Purchased," said Anko quietly. She had her notebook open, and the pencil was not moving. "By whom?"

The voice did not answer at once. When it did, it did not sound unkind. It did not sound kind either. It sounded like a statement of account.

"By the one who stood in the Trial," said the Goddess. "Four hundred and thirty-seven times. This is what it bought."

Then it was gone.

The river ran. The hills stood dark against the last of the sky. The empty bridge creaked a little in the cold. Someone's abandoned pack lay in the middle of it, and a child's mitten on the rail.



They all looked at him.

He felt them do it. The whole house, all down the end of the bridge, and beyond them the people of the settlement who had come out into the plaza and the yards. Every one of them was holding a small pale coin in their hand, and every one of them, at the same moment, turned and looked at him.

He did not look back. He was looking at the coin in his palm.

*Purchased.*

He thought about two hundred and eleven lines in a notebook, burned on a rock. About two hundred and twenty-six more he could not reach. About a man with white hair, and a field with Maki, and a knife on a table. About a white room with two chairs, and a door. About frost, and cut flowers, and *if it weren't for you*.

This was what it had bought.

He had thought, all through the Trial, that it was for nothing. That was the worst of it, worse than any single death. Four hundred and thirty-seven times for no reason, in a test with no answer, for a Goddess who never said why. He had come out the other side believing that every one of those deaths had been wasted, because he had never found the way through.

It had not been for nothing.

It had been for this. For a small warm coin in every hand in the settlement. For Coco's mother, someday, maybe. For whoever Kaneki would someday name, whose name nobody knew. For the cooper's daughter. For whoever Maki had written on the paper she kept in her pocket and never fired. For Rem, someday, if *not this time* ever became *this time*. For every person who had chosen to stay, and would someday, if they wanted, be able to bring one person they loved to the place they had chosen.

He had bought that. With all of it.

He had not known.



Frieren took her hand out of his.

He looked at her. She was holding her own coin up between finger and thumb, looking at it in the very last of the light. Her face was doing something he could not read.

"Millennia," she said.

He did not understand.

"Ciel said a hundred years," said Frieren. "Older than me. At the short end." She turned the coin over. "And Rimuru's face said more than that. I saw it. I saw him say the small number out loud and keep the big one." She looked at Mau. "You paid for this with *millennia*. Of dying. And you didn't know you were buying anything."

Rimuru, beside them, had gone very still.

"I didn't," said Mau. "I thought it was for nothing."

"It wasn't," said Frieren.

She closed her hand around the coin and held it against her chest, where her own hand had pressed on the floor of the common room, and on his chest in the dark.

"I'm keeping mine," she said. "I'm not spending it. Not for a long time. Maybe not ever." She looked at him steadily. "Because I know what it cost. And I'm not going to spend something that cost you that on anything less than exactly the right thing."

Down the bridge, one by one, without anyone saying anything, other hands closed too.

Coco, who had found hers on the boards, closed her fist around it and held it to her chest. Ori, beside her, did the same. Then Umaru. Then Yuta, who looked at his for a long moment and then put it away, very carefully, in an inside pocket of his coat, and buttoned the pocket. Then Rimuru. Then Milim, who closed both her hands around hers and held them to her heart and burst into tears, enormously and without embarrassment. Then all of them.

Nobody spent anything that night. Nobody even talked about it.

They stood at the end of the empty bridge in the dark, with their hands closed around the thing he had bought them, until Momo said, in a voice that cracked only slightly, that dinner was getting cold. Then they went home.



Mau did not close his hand right away.

He walked back to the Muslin with the coin lying open in his palm, looking at it, while Frieren held his other hand. On the step of the side door he stopped.

"Mine too," he said.

Frieren looked at him.

"I stayed," said Mau. "I didn't go to her. But I stayed." He looked at the coin. "So I got one too. Of the thing I bought."

Frieren did not say anything.

"I don't know who I'd ask for," said Mau. "I don't remember anyone from before. I don't have anyone from before." He turned the coin over in his palm. "Everyone I love is already here."

"Then keep it," said Frieren. "Until you know."

"What if I never know?"

Frieren reached out and closed his fingers over the coin, one at a time, gently, with her own.

"Then you'll have one thing in your pocket for the rest of your life," she said, "that reminds you every single day that it wasn't for nothing."

He put it in his pocket.

He could feel it there all through dinner, small and warm against his leg.
