On the twelfth day, the house went back to work, because the house did not know how to do anything else.

Fern was at the head of the long table before anyone else was awake. Mau knew this because he was awake too. He had been awake since the hour before dawn, which was the hour his body had decided, somewhere in the white, was the hour when things began again. He had lain still with Frieren's hair across his arm and waited for the ceiling to change. It had not. Then he had got up, very carefully, and gone down the family corridor in his socks, and found Fern already sitting in the common room in the grey light, with the ledger open in front of her and a pen in her hand, not writing.

She looked up.

"You're up early."

"So are you."

"I'm always up early." She looked back at the page. "I don't know what to write."

He came and sat on the bench at her elbow. The ledger was open to a clean page. At the top, in Fern's small square hand, was the date, counted the way the house counted now, from the window: *Day 12.* Under it were the headings she had written on every page for as long as there had been a ledger. *Arrival House. Intake. Terms. Guests. Departures. Notes.*

Nothing under any of them.

"There's nothing to put under Intake," said Fern. "There's nothing to put under Terms. The terms are over. Departures—" She stopped. "Departures happened yesterday. All at once, at sunset. I wrote every name I had on yesterday's page. Jinshi had the rest." She turned the pen over in her fingers. "I don't know what goes here."

Mau looked at the empty headings.

"Maybe nothing," he said.

"The ledger is never nothing."

"It could be. Once."

Fern considered this with the expression she kept for proposals she suspected were stupid and could not yet prove were.

"Once," she said at last. "Maybe."

She did not write anything. But she did not close the ledger either. She left it open on the table, with its clean page and its empty headings, and went to put the kettle on, and Mau sat beside it in the grey light and watched the page not fill up.



Arrival House opened at the eighth bell, the way it always had.

Jinshi unlocked the front door himself. He had done it every morning since the building went up, and he did it now with the same small flourish of the key, which he did not know he did and which half the settlement imitated behind his back. The staff came in behind him: four from the first wave of refuge, two from the second community, and the girl from the river towns who kept the guest register and had stayed. They took their places. The intake table. The waiting benches. The two terms rooms, which had been witness rooms for ten days and were now, suddenly, only rooms. The second witness room, which had not existed a fortnight ago, had a table in it with nothing on it, and a chair pushed back from the table as if someone had just stood up.

They waited.

Nobody came.

At the ninth bell, Jinshi went out onto the step and looked up the road toward the bridge. The road was empty. Frost in the ruts. A crow.

At the tenth bell, the girl from the river towns asked whether she should start taking the terms notices down from the board in the hall. Jinshi said yes. Then he said no. Then he said yes, but to keep them, in a folder, in case anyone ever asked what the terms had been. She took them down one by one and put them in a folder, and wrote on the folder in careful capitals, THE TEN DAYS, and put it on the shelf with the guest registers.

At the eleventh bell, Mikasa walked past on her way to the bridge-side court, stopped, looked in through the open door at the empty benches, and kept walking.

Nobody came all day.



The coins went where people kept their important things.

Mau did not watch anyone do it. He did not ask. But the house was small enough, even after the Muslin, that you could not help seeing where things ended up, and over the course of the twelfth day he saw.

Yuta's went into his sword case, under the cloth, beside the sword. He did it in the morning without a word, and closed the case, and that was all.

Maki kept hers in her pocket, with the folded paper she had been carrying since the window opened. Mau saw her hand go to that pocket twice at breakfast, the way a hand goes to a pocket with a letter in it.

Coco put hers in the little tin where she kept her best pen nibs. Then she took it out again and put it on a string. Then she took it off the string. In the end she gave it to Qifrey to keep "until I find out," and Qifrey put it in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left side, and did not say anything about the choice of pocket.

Bocchi put hers in her guitar case, in the little compartment under the neck where spare strings live. She closed the compartment, and then the case, and then she sat on the case for a while.

Umaru put hers under her pillow, then on her windowsill, then in the toll booth, then back under her pillow. By evening it had been in eleven places, and she had not stopped moving it.

Milim tried to hang hers on a cord around her neck and could not get the knot right, and asked Rimuru, and Rimuru tied it. She wore it outside her shirt all day where everyone could see it, like a medal.

Rem put hers in her blue-rimmed cup on its hook in the toll booth. Then she thought better of it, because a cup was for tea, and kept it in her apron.

Frieren put hers in her grimoire, between two pages, next to the pressed flowers. She did it in the evening, by the lamp, and closed the book over it gently. Mau, watching from the bed, thought that of every place in the house, it was the one it would be hardest to lose it from.

Mau kept his in his coat pocket.

He had not taken it out since the morning in the plaza. He did not take it out now. Sometimes during the day he put his hand in his pocket and felt it there, small and warm. *Everyone I love is already here.* He had said that on the bridge, with the coin in his palm, to the Goddess or to nobody. It was still true. It was the only coin in the house, he thought, with no name waiting for it.

He did not know yet that this was going to matter.



The cooper came to the house in the afternoon.

He had stayed. He was not house, and he knew it, and he stood at the front door with his hat in his hands, the way he had stood on the tenth morning to tell Mau so. He had come to ask whether there was work. Not charity. Work. He had been a cooper in the river towns before the roads went bad. The settlement had barrels, and barrels broke.

Fern found him four broken barrels before he had finished explaining, and Senku found him six more. By the time Momo came out of the kitchen with bread, the cooper had a corner of the yard and a pile of staves and a look on his face that Mau recognized, because he had seen it in the mirror his first month at the old inn. The look of a man who has stopped waiting for someone to tell him to leave.

He took the bread. At the door, on his way back out to the yard, he stopped and looked across the common room at the fort, at the flag on its broom handle and the toll booth and the six cushions in the annex with NEXT in front of them.

"My girl would've liked that," he said, to nobody in particular. "Eleven, near enough. If time runs the same there."

Umaru, who had been listening from the toll booth, came out in her sash.

"Applications," said Umaru, with enormous dignity, "are considered by the full assembly."

The cooper looked at her.

"She isn't here," he said. "Not yet. Maybe not ever. I'm finding out first. Whether it's safe."

"Then the form will be ready," said Umaru, "when she is."

The cooper looked at her a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, the way he had nodded on the tenth morning when he said *it was mine to decide*, and went out to his staves.

From the far end of the fort, Agott said, "We don't even have an application form."

"We do now," said Umaru, and went to make one.



At dinner, Fern read the ledger.

She did it every night: the day's entries, aloud, in her clear even voice, before anyone was allowed to eat, so that the whole house knew what the whole house had done. Tonight she stood at the head of the long table with the ledger open and looked at the page for a long moment.

"Day twelve," said Fern. "Arrival House opened at the eighth bell and closed at the sixth. Intake, none. Terms, none. Guests, two, both staying. Departures, none."

She paused.

"Arrivals," said Fern, and stopped again.

The table waited.

"Zero," said Fern.

She wrote it. Everyone heard the pen. It was the first time in the history of the ledger, which was the history of the settlement, that the number had been zero, and Fern wrote it down in her small square hand exactly as she would have written forty. Then she looked up at all of them.

"That's all," she said. "Eat."

Nobody moved for a second.

Then Hakari said, "Zero's a good number," and Karane said, "Zero's a *terrible* number, it means nobody wants to come," and Nano said, "It means nobody *needed* to come, which is different," and Shizuka wrote something in her book and held it up, and it said *BOTH*. The table started eating, and arguing, and the noise came back.

Mau sat beside Frieren and ate his soup.

At some point Rimuru, at the cold-hearth end of the table, looked down the length of it at all of them, the way he did sometimes, counting without counting.

"Here," said Rimuru. Not loudly. To nobody in particular.

It had been the word for the two days. The word on the contract. The word Mau had sent every evening from a rock by the falls, so that someone would know he was still alive. Rimuru had said it at this table on the first night, to an empty chair.

Mau put down his spoon.

"Here," he said.

Ori said it, across the table. Then Bocchi, very quietly. Then Umaru, with her mouth full. Then all of them, down the long table, one after another, raggedly, the way a roll gets called in a school where nobody takes the roll seriously. It reached Seiko in her armchair at the far end, who said nothing and lifted her fan, and that counted.

Fern did not write it in the ledger.

But later that night, when everyone had gone to bed, Mau came back for a glass of water and found the ledger still open on the table under the lamp. At the very bottom of the page, under *Arrivals: 0*, in small square letters, it said:

*Everyone here.*
