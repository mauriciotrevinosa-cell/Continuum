# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 2 — Ugly Days

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 2  
**Continuity state:** Days 13-18 | the reset hour; Frieren's foot: 'One. Still here.' | sounds; standing in front | Day 17 he can't get up: 'Something's wrong' | the house one at a time; Maki: 'Breathe with me' | Maomao: 'Ugly days are allowed. Hiding them is not.'

Maomao named them on the fifteenth, at the long table, in front of everyone. She had the bluntness of someone who believed that a thing with a name was a thing you could treat.

"Ugly days," said Maomao. "That's what these are. You'll have them. Not every day. Some days. They are allowed." She looked down the table at Mau over the rim of her cup. "Hiding them is not."

That was the fifteenth. The ugly days had started on the thirteenth, and nobody had called them anything yet.



The first thing was the hour.

Every morning, in the dark before dawn, his body woke. Not slowly, the way a body wakes from sleep, but all at once, the way it had woken at every anchor: whole, unhurt, already braced. In the white, that moment had always meant the same thing. It had happened again. Begin.

He would lie still with his eyes open and wait for the room to change.

It never did. The ceiling stayed the ceiling. The east window stayed grey. Frieren's hair stayed across his arm. But his body did not believe the room, not for a long time, and he lay there each morning with his heart going and his hands flat on the blanket, waiting for a road, or rain, or a white room with two chairs.

On the third morning, Frieren woke with him.

He did not know how she knew. He had not moved. But in the dark, without opening her eyes, she slid one cold foot across the bed and put it against his shin, deliberately, the way she did in the evenings to make him yelp.

He did not yelp.

"One," said Frieren, into the pillow. "Still here."

He let his breath out.

After that she did it every morning. She did not always wake for it. Sometimes she did it in her sleep, a cold foot finding his shin in the dark at exactly the hour, as if some part of her had learned the hour too. He never asked whether she was awake. It did not matter. It worked either way.



The second thing was sounds.

On the fourteenth, Momo dropped a pot in the kitchen. It was a big iron pot and it hit the stone floor like a bell. Mau, at the long table with a cup of tea, was on his feet with his hand out and his body turned toward the door before the echo had finished. His tea went across the table.

Momo stood in the kitchen doorway with the pot in one hand and looked at him.

"It's a pot," she said.

"I know."

"It's my pot. I dropped it."

"I know." He sat down. He looked at the spilled tea. Ori was already mopping it with her sleeve. "I know it's a pot."

His hands shook for an hour afterward. He kept them under the table. Nobody said anything about it, which was kind, and then Momo came out of the kitchen and put a new cup of tea in front of him and said, "I'll drop it more quietly next time," which was kinder, and then went back in and dropped a ladle, on purpose, very gently, so that it went *tink*. He laughed despite himself, and his hands shook a little less.

There were other sounds. A door slamming in the wind. A crow, sudden and close. Milim landing in the yard. Each time his body was already moving before his mind had caught up, turning toward the sound, hand out, weight forward, the way four hundred and thirty-seven scenarios had taught it. It was faster than thought. It was the fastest thing about him now, and he hated it.



The third thing was standing in front.

At the washing court on the sixteenth, a handcart got away from somebody on the slope and came rolling down, empty, rattling, not fast, not dangerous. Coco was standing in its way, looking at something in her basket. Mau was between her and the cart before he knew he had moved. He stopped it with one hand. It was a small cart. It would not have hurt her.

Coco looked up at him, and at the cart, and at him again.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"I know."

"It was an empty cart."

"I know."

She did not say anything else. She took his hand off the cart, held it for a moment, and then put the cart back where it belonged, and they went on with the washing. But he saw her look at him twice more that morning, carefully, the way she looked at a glyph she was not sure she had drawn right.



The seventeenth was the day he could not get up.

He woke at the hour. Frieren's foot found his shin. *One. Still here.* The room did not change. And then the grey went to light in the east window, and the house woke around him, and he lay there and found that he could not make himself sit up.

Nothing hurt. Nothing was wrong with his body. He could move his hands. He could turn his head. He simply could not do the next thing, which was sitting up, or the thing after that, which was standing. Somewhere between the hour and the morning, all of it had become impossibly far away.

Frieren, already dressed, stood at the end of the bed and looked at him.

He had a sentence for this. He had learned it in the autumn, in Senku's workroom, when the Noise had begun to rise and he had said it instead of running. It was the best tool he owned.

"Something's wrong," said Mau.

Frieren waited.

"Nothing's wrong with my body," he said. "I'm not sick. Ciel's not saying anything. I just can't get up today." He looked at the ceiling. "I'm telling you. That's all. That's rule five. Both ways."

Frieren considered this for a moment.

Then she took off her boots, got back into the bed fully dressed, and lay down beside him on top of the blanket with her hands folded on her stomach.

"Then I can't either," she said.

He turned his head and looked at her.

"You have things to do."

"Fern will do them. Fern likes doing them." She closed her eyes. "Go back to sleep, if you can. If you can't, don't."



The house found out the way the house found out everything: Fern wrote it in the ledger.

She did not make it a rule. There were no rules on the board anymore. There was only the Ministry's *THE DAY AFTER IS NOT FOR WORK*, which Umaru had decided was permanent. But at the morning reading Fern stood at the head of the table and read, in exactly the voice she used for barrel counts and bread: "Mau. In bed today. He told us." And she wrote it down, and the house went on.

They came one at a time. Nobody organized it. It simply happened that way, the way water finds its level.

Ori came first, and sat on the floor by the bed with her back against it, and read her book, and did not speak, and stayed an hour.

Umaru came in the middle of the morning with a paper bag of potato chips from the toll booth, which she said were the emergency reserve, which the President was authorized to release in a crisis. She put the bag on his chest and stood back with her arms folded, as if supervising.

"Eat one," said Umaru.

He ate one.

"Good," said Umaru, and left the bag, and went.

Bocchi did not come in. Bocchi sat outside the door in the corridor with her guitar and played three notes. The same three, over and over, slowly. The ones from the refuge, from the night of Last Defense, when the children had been too frightened to move and Kita had said *three notes* and Bocchi had played them until the children followed the sound. She played them for an hour, outside the door, and then she stopped and went away without knocking.

Milim came in after lunch and lay down on the floor beside the bed, flat on her back, and stayed there. When Mau looked over the edge of the bed at her, she said, without opening her eyes, "I'm being slow. At the speed of a normal person. You can be slow too."

In the afternoon, the panic came.

He had known it would. It came the way it had come on the night of the talk: out of nowhere, out of a ceiling and a blanket and a quiet afternoon, his breath going short and fast and wrong, his chest tight, the room starting to tilt toward the white. Frieren sat up beside him. Rimuru, in the loft above the closet, went very still.

Maki was at the door before anyone called her. She had been in the corridor. She did not say how long.

She did not touch him. She knelt by the bed where he could see her, and put her hand flat on the blanket a little distance from his, not touching.

"Look at me," said Maki. "Breathe with me."

He looked at her. He breathed with her. In, and hold, and out, the way she had taught him on the night of the talk, the way someone had once taught her. It took a long time. She did not hurry it. When it was over she stayed kneeling there a little longer, and then she got up, and said, "Same time tomorrow, if you need it," as if it were an appointment, and went back out into the corridor.

That night, Yuta and Rimuru did not go up to the roof.

They came into the room with the east window instead, and sat on the floor by the bed in the dark, with their backs against the wall, one on each side of the door. Yuta had brought three cups and a jar of something of Seiko's. Rimuru had brought nothing.

"The roof's cold tonight," said Yuta.

"The roof's always cold," said Rimuru.

"Then the roof's here," said Yuta, and poured three cups, and handed one up onto the bed. And they sat in the dark and did not talk about anything that mattered, for an hour, until Mau fell asleep with the cup in his hand, and Frieren took it out of his hand and put it on the windowsill beside Ori's stone.



On the eighteenth he got up.

It was not triumphant. It was only that at the hour his body woke, and Frieren's foot found his shin, and the room did not change, and then the grey went to light, and when he tried to sit up, he could. So he did. And then he stood. And then he went to breakfast.

Nobody clapped. Nobody said anything. Momo put soup in front of him. Fern read the ledger. Under his name, today, she read nothing at all, because there was nothing to read.

Maomao, at the end of the table, looked at him over the rim of her cup.

"Ugly day yesterday," she said.

"Yes."

"Told us."

"Yes."

Maomao nodded once, and wrote something in the little notebook she kept in her apron, and went back to her breakfast.

That was the whole of it. That was how the ugly days went, through the rest of the month: not gone, but fewer. One one week, and two the next, and then none for a while, and then one again, out of nowhere, on a morning when nothing had happened. Each time he said the sentence. Each time Fern wrote it in the ledger in the voice she used for barrels. Each time the house came one at a time, the way water finds its level.

And each morning, at the hour, a cold foot found his shin in the dark.

*One. Still here.*
