# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 3 — Looked. Quiet.

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 3  
**Continuity state:** Days 14-20 | rule four: she tells him every time she looks | at breakfast, through Rimuru: 'Looked, quiet.' | Senku's QUIET tally; 'an emergency of morale' | it spreads to the washing court | Ciel: 'I do not count alone anymore'

Rule four was his.

The guardrails had been written by the house while he was unconscious, in the autumn of the containment, and pinned inside his door, and he had read them when he woke and changed one. Rule four had said, at first, that Ciel would watch the guardrails. Mau had added the second half in pencil, in his own hand, under the household's ink: *and she tells him every time she looks.*

He had added it because he did not want to be watched without knowing. It had seemed, at the time, like the kind of rule a person writes for himself. It had not occurred to him then that he was also writing a rule for her.

Since the Trial, she had looked every morning.

He knew because she told him. Every morning at the hour, while his body was still waiting for the room to change and Frieren's foot was finding his shin, a voice in his head said two words, quietly, the way you would say them to someone in the next bed so as not to wake anyone else.

*Looked. Quiet.*

Nothing more. She did not explain what she had looked at, because he knew. She did not say what quiet meant, because he knew that too. The seal was holding. The guardrails were not bending. Nothing inside him was pressing against anything. Two words, every morning, and then she was gone again, back to whatever Ciel did with her day.

He had come to wait for them. That was the strange part. In the white, the only thing that ever crossed the wall had been a number, once per death, in her voice. Now the only thing that crossed in the mornings was this. *Looked. Quiet.* Sometimes it came before Frieren's foot, sometimes after. He had begun, without meaning to, to count it as part of the hour. The room did not change. The foot came. The two words came. Then he could get up.



On the fourteenth, at breakfast, he asked her to say it out loud.

Not in his head. Out loud, through Rimuru, at the long table, so that everyone could hear.

Rimuru looked at him across the bread.

"She's asking why," he said.

Mau looked down the table. At Fern with the ledger. At Momo coming out of the kitchen. At Maki with her hand near her pocket, and Yuta beside her, and Coco, and Umaru, and the whole loud breakfast of them, every one of whom had spent the last two weeks watching him over their cups and trying not to look as if they were.

"Because they've been checking," he said. "All of them. Every morning. They look at me to see if I'm all right, and they try to do it so I won't notice, and I notice, and then they notice I noticed." He shrugged. "If she tells them, they can stop doing that. Or at least they can stop pretending they're not."

Rimuru was quiet for a moment, listening to something nobody else could hear.

"She says," said Rimuru, "that is a reasonable request, and she is somewhat embarrassed not to have thought of it."

"Tell her thank you."

"She heard you."

The next morning, before anyone had picked up a spoon, Rimuru cleared his throat at the cold-hearth end of the table.

The whole table turned.

"She says," said Rimuru, "*looked, quiet.*"

There was a short pause.

Then Momo said, "Good. Eat," and put the bread down, and everyone ate.



It took three days to become a thing.

On the first day, people waited for it without admitting they were waiting. The spoons hovered. When Rimuru said it, the spoons came down.

On the second day, Milim asked what *quiet* meant.

"Quiet like sleeping?" she said, with her mouth full. "Or quiet like hiding?"

Rimuru listened.

"She says: quiet like nothing pressing against anything."

Milim considered this.

"Boring," she said.

"She says: yes."

"Good boring or bad boring?"

"She says," said Rimuru, with the faint resignation of a man who has been an interpreter for too long, "that she is not aware of a bad boring, and would like to be informed if one is ever observed."

On the third day, Senku drew a column on the board, in the corner below the four rings, and labelled it in small square capitals: QUIET. Under it he made three tally marks. He did not explain. He added one every morning after that, while Rimuru was still saying it, so that the chalk squeaked on the second word.

On the fourth day, Anko came to breakfast with her notebook and copied the tally into it. That afternoon she pinned a copy in the evidence room, beside the seventh rumor, the one from the river towns that said the monster had gone to the Goddess alone and the settlement was hiding it. She did not write anything on it. She only pinned it there, next to the rumor, where anyone who read the one would have to see the other.

On the fifth day, Ciel said something else.

"She says," said Rimuru, "*looked, quiet,*" and paused, and his face did something complicated. "And she says that this is the fifth consecutive quiet, and that she considers it an emergency."

There was a silence at the table.

"An emergency of what?" said Fern.

Rimuru listened.

"Of morale," he said.

"She means she's happy," said Mau.

"She says," said Rimuru, "she means exactly what she said."

Down at the north end of the table, Hakari burst out laughing, and then Karane, and then half the fort, and Ciel, who had been treating good news as an emergency since the containment, did not say anything else at all. But Mau, who could hear her, could tell that she was pleased.



After that it spread.

He did not notice it happening. He noticed it only afterward, the way you notice a path that has worn across a lawn.

The cooper, splitting staves in the yard within earshot of the open door, heard it said at the table every morning, in a tone that made people put down their spoons and start eating. He did not know what it meant. He concluded, reasonably, that it was what you said in this house when the day was all right, and he started saying it to the girl from the river towns who kept the guest register at Arrival House, when she came past in the mornings, instead of *good morning*.

She started saying it back.

Then the rest of the staff at Arrival House, who heard it from her. Then the washing court, who heard it from the staff. By the end of the second week, Mau walked across the plaza in the morning and heard a woman he had never spoken to say to a man he did not know, over a basket of wet sheets, "Looked, quiet?" And the man said, "Looked, quiet," and they went on with the sheets.

Neither of them had ever heard Ciel's voice. Neither of them knew there was a Ciel. They only knew it was what you said when nothing was wrong.

He stood in the plaza and did not know what to feel about it.

That night, he told Frieren.

She was reading in bed, with her grimoire propped on her knees. She listened to the whole thing without looking up, and turned a page, and said, "Good."

"Good?"

"They used to say *the monster town*," said Frieren. "In the plaza. At the bridge. Anko had six rumors on the wall." She turned another page. "Now they say *looked, quiet*. They don't know what it means. But what it means is that you're all right." She glanced at him over the top of the book. "They're saying you're all right to each other, every morning, without knowing it. That's better than knowing it."

He thought about that for a long time.



On the twentieth, at the hour, it came the way it always came.

*Looked. Quiet.*

And then, after a pause, which was unusual, because Ciel did not pause, the voice came again. Quieter. Not through Rimuru. Only to him.

*And thank you,* said Ciel. *For letting me say it aloud.*

He lay still in the dark.

*In the Trial,* said Ciel, *I counted alone. I said the numbers to a wall, and I did not know if anything heard. Four hundred and thirty-seven times.* A pause. *I did not like it. I did not know I did not like it until I could stop.*

Beside him, in her sleep, Frieren's foot found his shin.

*Now when I look,* said Ciel, *thirty people hear the answer. And a cooper from the river towns. And a woman at the washing court who has never heard my voice.* Another pause, longer. *I find that I do not count alone anymore. I wanted you to know.*

"I know," said Mau, very softly, into the dark, so as not to wake anyone.

*I know you know,* said Ciel. *I wanted to say it anyway.*

At breakfast, Rimuru cleared his throat at the cold-hearth end of the table, and everyone turned.

"She says," said Rimuru, "*looked, quiet.*"

Senku's chalk squeaked on the second word.

And everyone ate.
