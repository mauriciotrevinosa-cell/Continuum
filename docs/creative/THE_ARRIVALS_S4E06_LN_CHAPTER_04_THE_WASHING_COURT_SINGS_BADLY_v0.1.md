## Chapter 4 — The Washing Court Sings Badly

There was no rule anymore that sent him to the washing court.

There had been one. Maomao's, from the council, before the window: *he has to be seen.* A man in the plaza with a summon he was still training, washing his own shirts like anyone else, so that the settlement could look at him and see ordinary. It had been on the board with the others, and it had come off the board with the others on the morning of the eleventh day, wiped clean by the Ministry, along with the supervision rotation and the list of places he could not go.

He went anyway, on the eighteenth.

He went because the shirts needed washing, which was true. He went because he liked it, which was also true, and which he would not have admitted to anyone except Frieren. The washing court was the most ordinary place in the settlement. Nobody in it had ever been a hero or a demon lord or a dragon. It was steam and cold water and lye and people talking about their neighbours.

The Trial had used it anyway. It had used everything he loved: the old inn and the plaza and the bridge and the sea and the room with the east window, and this. Somewhere in the white there had been a washing court exactly like this one, steaming in the cold, and he had died in it. That was why, on the worst night, he had walked north to the only place he had never died.

He went anyway. The real one had people in it.

And he went alone.

That was new. For ten days there had always been someone half a step behind him: Mikasa, Maki, Yuta, someone, keeping their body between him and the road south. On the morning of the eighteenth he picked up his basket in the yard, and Frieren, on the step with her tea, looked at the basket and at him, and said nothing at all. She did not get up. She drank her tea.

He walked to the washing court by himself, with the basket on his hip, in the cold.



It was steaming when he got there. It always was, in winter: the big vats over their fires sending up white clouds into the frozen air, so that the whole court looked like the inside of a kettle. The stones were wet. The people at the troughs were red-handed and loud and wrapped in three layers apiece, and they slapped the wet cloth on the boards and wrung it and argued over the drying lines, exactly as they had in autumn, exactly as they would in spring.

And someone was singing.

Badly. Very badly. A song with a simple, stubborn tune, the kind of song you sing to keep a rhythm when you are wringing sheets. Whoever was singing it could not hold the tune at all. The voice went up where the song went down and down where it went up, cracked on the high note, and came back in a beat late on every line. It was the worst singing Mau had ever heard.

Nobody at the troughs seemed to mind. One or two of them were humming along, also badly, as if bad were the correct way to sing this song, and anyone who sang it well would be doing it wrong.

He knew who it was before he saw her.

She was at the far trough, the big one by the drain, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her arms red to the elbow, wringing a sheet the size of a sail. The large woman from the memorial. Dessa's friend. Beside her on the stones, half full of grey water, was a blue bucket.

He had heard about the blue bucket. Coco had told him, at the board by the ink tree, with charcoal in her hand. *She had a blue bucket. She'd stop and pour a little bit and then keep going.*

The woman had promised to sing badly every morning so somebody did. In front of the whole settlement, at the memorial, she had said that the washing court would be too quiet now, and that starting tomorrow she would sing badly herself. That had been weeks ago. Before the Trial. Before everything.

She had kept it. Every morning, through all of it.

He stood at the edge of the court with his basket and did not know whether to go in.



She saw him.

The song stopped in the middle of a line.

It was very noticeable. The humming at the other troughs stopped with it, raggedly, one voice and then another. People looked up. They looked at her, and then at what she was looking at, and then they looked carefully back down at their washing. The court went quiet except for the slap of wet cloth and the hiss of the fires.

The last time she had spoken to him, she had told him that if Dessa had never met him, she would be alive. That he had been at the back. That she had come to hear him say it was not his fault, and he had not been able to.

He did not leave.

He went to the near trough, the small one by the steps, as far from hers as the court allowed. He put his basket down on the wet stones. He took out a shirt and put it in the water. It was cold enough to make his hands ache. He started washing it.

He did not look at her. He did not apologize. He did not have anything to say that he had not already failed to say at the bread table. He only washed his shirt, at the small trough by the steps, in the steam, while the court held its breath around him.

It took a long time.

Then, from the far trough, the song started again.

Louder.

Much louder than before. And worse, which he would not have believed possible. She came in on the first line a full beat early, and went up where it went down with a kind of furious deliberation, and hit the high note so hard and so wrong that somebody at the middle trough laughed out loud and then clapped a wet hand over her mouth.

The woman did not stop. She sang the whole verse, badly, at the top of her voice, wringing her sail-sized sheet as if it had personally offended her, with her eyes on her work and not on him. Then she sang the second verse. By the second verse the humming had started again at the other troughs, and then somebody was singing along under their breath, and then two people, all of them badly, because there was a correct way to sing this song now, and this was it.

Mau washed his shirt.

He did not understand what was happening. Then, slowly, he did.

She had not forgiven him. He did not think she was going to. That was hers, and he would not ask for it. But she was singing. Dessa's song was being sung, terribly, every morning, in the steam. And he was in the room where it was sung. She had seen him come in, and she had stopped, and then she had started again, louder.

She had not told him to leave.



He finished his shirts. He wrung them and stacked them in the basket and stood up, with his hands red and aching from the cold water, and turned to go.

She was coming the other way with her bucket, toward the drain at the steps, to empty it. She had to pass him. The court was not wide enough for her not to.

She did not stop. She did not look at him. She went by close enough that the blue bucket swung against his knee, and as she went by she said, flatly, to the air in front of her:

"You're wringing them wrong."

He stopped.

"Other way," said the woman. "You twist against the weave, you'll have holes by spring." She tipped the bucket into the drain without breaking stride. "Dessa used to say it. You never listened."

Then she went back to her trough, and picked up her sheet, and started the third verse.

He stood on the steps with his basket for a moment.

Then he went back to the small trough, and took the shirts out of the basket, and put them back in the water. He wrung them again, one by one, the other way, with the weave. It took a long time. His hands hurt.

She did not look at him once. She sang the whole third verse, and the fourth, which he had not known the song had, badly and at the top of her voice.

He walked home alone with the basket on his hip, in the cold, with the shirts wrung the right way. Frieren was still on the step with her tea, which had gone cold, and she had not moved.

She looked at the basket. Then at his hands, which were red to the wrist. Then at his face.

"You went to the washing court," she said.

"Yes."

"And?"

He thought about how to answer.

"There's a song," he said. "Somebody sings it badly. Every morning." He sat down on the step beside her. "I'm going to go again tomorrow."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment. Then she handed him her cold tea, and he drank it.
