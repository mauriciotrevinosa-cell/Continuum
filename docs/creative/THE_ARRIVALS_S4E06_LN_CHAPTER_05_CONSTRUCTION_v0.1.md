*When: The night of Day 18, the end of the first week after the window. The same day as the washing court, at the end of the ugly days that started on the thirteenth.*

His hands shook at dinner.

It was nothing. It was a cup. Okarun knocked his own cup over at the far end of the long table, and it rolled, and hit the floor, and did not even break. It was not loud. It was nothing like Momo's pot on the fourteenth. But Mau's body heard it before he did. His hand jerked, and his own cup jumped in it, and tea went over the back of his knuckles. By the time he had set it down, both his hands were shaking, and they did not stop.

He put them under the table.

Nobody said anything. That was the house's way, that week. Nobody said anything, and everybody saw. Frieren, beside him, moved her knee against his under the table and left it there. Fern, at the head, went on reading the ledger aloud without changing her voice. Maomao, halfway down, glanced once at the place where his hands had gone, and then at her notebook, and wrote something small.

And at the far end of the bench by the window, Mai looked at him for a long time.

He did not see her looking. He was looking at the table.



The common room emptied slowly, the way it did. Kessoku went first, to the music room, because Bocchi wanted to try something on the guitar before she lost it. The fort went behind its curtain. Seiko was carried off in her armchair by Okarun and Momo. Frieren went up at ten. She stood at the bottom of the corridor for a moment and looked back at him, sitting alone at the long table with his hands still under it, and then she went up, because she knew he would come when he could.

He sat there a while longer.

Then someone sat down on the bench across from him.

It was Mai.

She did not say anything at first. She put something on the table between them, on the scrubbed wood, under the lamp. Small. Heavy. Dull grey. It made a tiny click as she set it down.

A bullet.

He looked at it. Then at her.

She had her elbows on the table and her chin in her hand and her usual expression, which was the expression of someone who had been made to sit through a very long and stupid meeting and was not going to pretend otherwise. But her eyes were not on him. They were on the place under the table where his hands were.

"Made it this morning," said Mai.

Mau did not say anything.

"I make one every morning," said Mai. "You know that. Since before." She turned the bullet a quarter-turn with one finger. "There's no door to stand at this week. No refuge. Nobody coming. Nobody's going to need it." She looked at it. "I made it anyway."

"Why?"

Mai shrugged.

"So it exists," she said. "If somebody needs it, it's there. If nobody does, it's still there." She turned it another quarter. "That's the point of making it every morning. Not the morning somebody needs it. Every morning."

He looked at the bullet for a long time.

"Construction," he said quietly.

Mai's mouth moved. It was not a smile. It was the place a smile would have gone on someone else.

"You remembered."

"You told me. After Last Defense. On the floor." He looked at her. "You said it's what they call mine. And yours."

"It is." She picked the bullet up and held it between her finger and thumb, in the lamplight. "Mine's this. One a day. Out of my own body. Bigger than this and I'd be in Maomao's room for a week." She put it down again. "That's what it costs. I know exactly what it costs. I've known since I was a kid. I could tell you in my sleep."

She looked at the place under the table again.

"What's yours?" said Mai.

Mau did not answer.

"What does yours cost?" said Mai. "You said it costs. On the floor. You said *it costs*." She did not look away. "So what does it cost?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it.

He did not know.

That was the truth of it, sitting at the long table at eleven at night with his hands under the wood. He did not know. He knew the ugly days. He knew the hour before dawn when his body woke and waited for the ceiling to change. He knew the pot on the fourteenth, and the cup tonight, and the way his body moved before he did, faster than thought, the way four hundred and thirty-seven of something had taught it. He knew Maomao had a notebook. He knew Ciel counted.

He did not know what any of it cost. He had never once sat down and counted it himself.

"I don't know," he said.

Mai looked at him for a long moment.

"Then find out," she said.

"How?"

"Count it." She tapped the bullet. "Every day. Like this. Not the bad days. Maomao's got the bad days. She's counting them. I've seen the notebook." She tapped it again. "Count the cost. What you did. What it took. How long your hands shook after. What helped." She paused. "If you know what it costs, you can decide whether to pay it. If you don't know, it just takes it. Whenever it wants. And you find out afterward, on a floor."

Mau sat very still.

"That's all mine is," said Mai. "A bullet a day and knowing exactly what it takes. That's the whole technique." She leaned back. "Everyone thinks the technique is the bullet. It's not. It's the counting."



For a while neither of them said anything. The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a door closed. In the music room, very faintly, through the buffer wall, someone was playing three notes over and over, and stopping, and starting again.

Then Mai put her right hand flat on the table, palm down, next to the bullet. In the lamplight.

It was shaking.

Very slightly. Not much. You would only have seen it if you were looking. A fine fast tremor in the fingers, the kind a hand has after it has carried something heavy a long way.

"Made it at six," said Mai. "It's eleven." She looked at her own hand, unembarrassed, the way you look at weather. "It'll stop around midnight. It always does. Five or six hours, for one." She left it there, on the table, in the light. "I used to keep it in my pocket. Under the table. In my sleeve. When I was little, and the clan was watching, and a shaking hand meant you were weak." She paused. "Maki made me stop. When we were twelve. She said if they were going to call us weak anyway, they could call us weak to our faces."

Mau looked at her hand on the table. At the tremor in it. At the bullet beside it that she had made out of her own body that morning, for a door nobody was going to come through.

Slowly, he took his hands out from under the table.

He put them on the wood, next to hers. Palms down. In the light.

They were shaking. Worse than hers. Much worse. The tea had dried on the back of his knuckles in a pale stain.

Mai looked at them. She did not say anything for a while.

"Worse than mine," she said at last.

"Yeah."

"From a cup."

"From a cup."

Mai nodded slowly, as if he had told her a number and she was writing it down.

"Okay," she said. "That's one. That's the first one you've counted." She looked up at him. "Tomorrow you count the next one."

And then she did something he had never seen Mai do, in all the time since she came over the bridge behind her sister with her revolver and her flat bored face and her refusal to hug anyone first.

She moved her shaking hand an inch across the table, and laid her little finger against the edge of his.

Just that. Not holding. Just touching. Two shaking hands on a long table under a lamp at eleven o'clock at night, and the place where they touched shook a little less.

Neither of them looked at it.



They sat like that until midnight. Mai's hand stopped first, at about a quarter to, exactly as she had said it would. She took it back and flexed the fingers and looked at them with mild professional interest. His stopped at a quarter past.

"An hour and a half longer than mine," said Mai. "From a cup."

"I'll count it."

"Good." She stood up. She picked up the bullet, looked at it, and then, after a moment's thought, put it down again in front of him, on the table.

"Keep it," she said.

"It's yours. You made it."

"I make one every morning." She shrugged. "I've got a tin of them. Every one I didn't need." She pushed the bullet an inch closer to him. "That's a day of mine. You can have one. For your pocket." She paused. "So you remember to count."

Mau looked at it for a long time.

Then he picked it up, and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left, against his ribs. Where, eight days ago, on the bridge, a small warm coin had appeared in his palm, and where it still was. The coin was warm. The bullet was cold. They clicked together once, very faintly, and lay side by side.

Mai watched him do it. Her face did not change.

"Don't tell anyone I did that," she said.

"Which part?"

"Any of it." She turned to go. Then she stopped, with her back to him. "And don't put your hands under the table again. If they shake, they shake. Let them shake where people can see." A pause. "Maki was right. They can call you weak to your face."

She went up the stairs.

Halfway up, in the dark, Mau saw another shape move: someone sitting on the stairs, who had been sitting there for some time. Maki. She did not say anything to her sister as Mai went past her. She only moved her knees aside to let her by, and watched her go up, with an expression Mau could not see in the dark but could guess. The expression of someone who has watched her sister not hug anyone first for her entire life, and has just seen her lay one finger against someone else's hand.

Then Maki looked down the stairs at Mau, at the long table, under the lamp.

She did not say anything either. She gave him one short nod. Then she got up and followed her sister.

Mau sat at the long table alone for a while longer. Then he took the stub of pencil from beside the ledger, and a corner of scrap paper from the kindling box, and wrote, very small:

*Day 18. Cup. Hands: shook 3 hours. Helped: Mai.*

He folded it and put it in his pocket with the bullet and the coin.

The next morning, at breakfast, when Hakari asked whether anybody had decided anything, and the long table went quiet, his hands were on the table, where people could see them. They were not shaking. But they would have been allowed to.
