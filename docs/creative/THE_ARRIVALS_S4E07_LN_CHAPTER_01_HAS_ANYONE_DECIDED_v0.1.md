## Chapter 5 — Has Anyone Decided?

It was Hakari who asked, because Hakari asked the things everyone else was thinking, usually at breakfast, usually with her mouth full.

"Has anyone decided?"

The long table went quiet.

It was the nineteenth day. Rimuru had already said *looked, quiet*, and Senku had already squeaked the chalk, and the bread had already gone round once. Hakari was holding a piece of it in one hand and her coin in the other. She had been turning the coin over and over on the table beside her plate all through breakfast without seeming to notice.

Nobody answered.

"I'm just asking," said Hakari. "Because I haven't. And I keep thinking everyone else has, and I'm the only one who hasn't, and that's stupid, probably. So I'm asking."

"It's not stupid," said Nano.

"It's a little stupid," said Karane.

"It's a reasonable data-gathering question," said Nano, "asked at an inefficient time."

Shizuka held up her book. It said: *I haven't either.*

Fern put down her cup.

"Nobody's spending one this month," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"I'm proposing it," said Fern. "Now. For the ledger. Rings One and Two." She looked down the table. "The window closed eight days ago. We've had ugly days. We've had zero arrivals. We don't know what the world outside is going to do now that the window's shut. We don't know what bringing one person here does to the person, or to us, or to the place. I don't want anyone deciding the most important thing they'll ever decide in the same month they found out what it cost." Her eyes went, very briefly, to Mau, and away. "One month. Nobody spends. Then we talk. All of us, at this table. And then people decide."

There was a silence.

"Seconded," said Rimuru.

"Seconded," said Anko, and wrote it.

Down the table, one by one, people nodded. Nobody argued. Mau, watching their faces, thought that most of them looked relieved. As if Fern had taken something heavy off their hands and put it on a shelf where they could see it, and told them they did not have to carry it for a while.

Fern wrote it in the ledger.

*Coins: held. Until Day 40. Then the table.*

Hakari let out a breath and put her coin in her pocket and ate her bread.

But the question stayed. It stayed all day. Mau heard it again, and again, in different rooms, in different voices, the way a tune stays after the music has stopped.



At the toll booth, mid-morning, Umaru asked Ori.

"Have you decided?"

Ori was sitting on the booth's counter with her legs hanging down. She did not have a coin in her hand. She had a stone, the small grey one from the tidepool, which she had given to Mau once and which had come back to her and then gone to his windowsill, and which she sometimes borrowed.

"I don't have anyone from before," said Ori. "Before is a place I didn't have."

Umaru was quiet.

"You?" said Ori.

Umaru took her coin out of her sash. It was the twelfth place she had put it. She looked at it for a long time.

"No," she said, and put it back, somewhere else.



At the atelier, in the afternoon, Coco did not ask anyone anything.

She sat at the drawing table with a book from Qifrey's shelf, a heavy one about crystalline glyph structures, and read it with the stubborn slowness of someone reading a language she was only half fluent in. Qifrey sat across from her and did not ask what she was looking for. He had her coin in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left side.

At one point Coco looked up.

"Qifrey. Has anyone ever reversed it?"

He knew what *it* was.

"Not that I know of," he said. "Not where I'm from."

"But you don't know everything."

"No," said Qifrey. "Nobody does. That's why there are libraries." He paused. "And places nobody has thought to look."

Coco went back to her book.



At the bench under the window, at dusk, Yuta did not ask Maki.

He sat beside her and polished nothing in particular, and she sat beside him with her hand in her pocket, on the folded paper she had been carrying since before the window closed. He knew what was written on it. Or he knew who. He had known since the night she wrote it, because she had written it in the bed beside him with her back turned, and then folded it very small, and then lain awake with it in her fist.

He did not ask. She did not tell him. After a while she took her hand out of her pocket and put it on his knee instead, and they watched the light go out of the yard together.

His own coin was in the sword case, under the cloth. He had not looked at it since the twelfth. He did not need to look at it. He knew exactly whose face it had on it, even though it had no face at all.



In the medical room, Kusuri asked Maomao, who was the wrong person to ask anything.

"What if they say no?"

Maomao did not look up from her mortar.

"Then they say no."

"But what if I bring them, and they say no, and then I've used it, and—"

"The Goddess said a petition can't override what they want." Maomao ground something green into something greener. "So if they say no, they don't come, and you've lost nothing except finding out. Which is information." She tapped the pestle on the rim. "Information is never lost."

Kusuri sat on the end of the examination table and swung her feet.

"That's the worst thing anyone's ever said to me," she said.

"You're welcome," said Maomao.



At the toll booth again, in the evening, Rem said a name.

She had not meant to. She was rinsing her cup at the little basin behind the booth, the blue-rimmed one, and Umaru was doing the accounts of the fort on the counter in crayon, and Ori was still sitting on the counter with her stone, and nobody had asked Rem anything. Then Umaru said, without looking up, "Rem, have you decided?" the way she would have asked whether Rem wanted more tea.

Rem's hands stopped in the basin.

She had been asked about the dead at the Goddess's door, on the first day of the window, and the answer had been *not this time*. Everyone knew that. Everyone had been very careful with her since, the way the house was careful with things it did not want to break.

"Ram," said Rem.

Umaru looked up.

Rem was looking at the cup in her hands, not at either of them.

"My sister," said Rem. "Her name is Ram." She turned the cup in the water. "I haven't said it out loud. Here. Since I came. I thought if I said it here, I would—" She stopped. "I don't know what I thought."

Ori climbed down off the counter, very quietly, and stood beside her.

Umaru put down her crayon.

"Is she nice?" said Umaru.

Rem laughed. It surprised all three of them. It was a small, wet, startled laugh, the first that anyone in the house had heard from her in a long time.

"No," said Rem. "She's not nice at all. She's rude, and she's lazy, and she thinks she's better than everyone, and she's usually right." She put the cup on its hook. Her hands were shaking a little. "She's the best person I know."

Umaru thought about this very seriously, the way she thought about all matters of state.

"The fort," she said at last, "will need a bigger annex."

Rem put her face in her hands and laughed again, and this time she was also crying, and Ori put her arms around her from one side, and Umaru came round the counter and did it from the other, and the three of them stood behind the toll booth in the lamplight with Rem's coin in her apron pocket between them, and nobody said anything about the month.



Nijika did not tell anyone.

She had decided on the first night. On the bridge, with the coin still warm in her palm, she had looked at it and known whose name went with it, the way you know which way is up. She had not said it. She had not put it anywhere special. She kept it in the pocket of her jacket, loose, with a guitar pick and a hair tie, and when Hakari asked at breakfast she had looked at her bread and said nothing, because Bocchi was sitting next to her.

Bocchi, who had closed her guitar case on the bridge with nothing in her hands. Bocchi, who had put her coin in the compartment under the neck and sat on the case. Bocchi, who had a mother and a father and a little sister and a dog in another world, and who had said to Kita, once, on the night of the window, that if she took one of them she did not know what she would be leaving the others.

Nijika was not going to say *I've decided* in front of Bocchi. Not this month. Maybe not next month either.

But at night, before she slept, she took the coin out of her jacket pocket and held it for a minute in the dark, and said a name to it, under her breath, the way you check that a word still works.

Then she put it back with the pick and the hair tie, and went to sleep.



And Mau, who heard the question in every room that day, was asked it only once.

The cooper asked him. He was in the yard, sitting on his stack of staves with his pipe, and Mau came past with the wood for the stove.

"Have you decided?" said the cooper. He had heard everyone else say it all day.

Mau stopped. He put his hand in his coat pocket and felt the coin there, small and warm.

"I don't have to," he said. "Everyone I'd want is already here."

The cooper considered this around the stem of his pipe.

"That's cheating," he said.

"Yes," said Mau. "I think it is."

"Mine's eleven, near enough." The cooper took the pipe out of his mouth and looked at it. "I told you. I'm finding out first. Whether the roads are safe. Whether this place stays what it is." He put the pipe back. "Your president's making her a form."

"I heard."

"Don't let her put a sash on it," said the cooper. "She'll want one."

Mau went in with the wood. Behind him, in the yard, the cooper took his coin out of his waistcoat pocket and turned it over once in his thick fingers, slowly, the way a man checks a stave for a crack he already knows is not there.
