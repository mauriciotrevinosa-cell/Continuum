There was a word written on a strip of paper pinned to the foot of the second bed in Maomao's room, and the word was *temporary*.

Maomao had written it herself, in the autumn, on the evening the plan for the real house was cut. She had drawn Jinshi's bed into her room on the plan, as far from her own as the walls allowed, after an entire evening of objections on grounds of space, hygiene, chemistry and principle. Then she had gone back to the Muslin and moved his cot into her actual room, in the same spirit, against the opposite wall, and pinned the word to it so that there could be no misunderstanding.

That had been before the Trial. Before the window. Before everything.

The cot was still there. So was the word. The paper had gone soft at the corners from being touched, though Maomao had never touched it and Jinshi swore he had not either.



The clinic was busy in winter.

It was not busy the way it had been after Last Defense, with the triage tags and the boiling water and Kaneki at the door saying *internal* before anyone else could see it. It was busy the way a village clinic is busy in winter, which was steadily, stupidly, all day long. Chilblains. Coughs. A child from the refuge families who had put a dried bean up her nose to see whether it would fit, and had discovered that it would. The cooper, on the twenty-first, with a split thumb from a stave that had sprung back at him, who sat on the end of the examination table while Maomao stitched it and said nothing whatsoever, which she appreciated more than she would ever tell him.

Kusuri worked the bench by the window, where the light was best for her chemistry, and argued with Maomao about dosages in a low continuous mutter, the way two people who have worked together a long time argue, without heat and without stopping.

And there was a notebook.

Maomao kept it in her apron. It was small and plain and very full, and in it she had written down every ugly day since the thirteenth. Not what happened, but when. The hour. What he had said. What had helped. Frieren's foot. Bocchi's three notes outside the door. Maki's breathing. Yuta and Rimuru on the floor with Seiko's jar. Maomao had been a pharmacist in a palace once, among people who poisoned each other for sport, and she had learned there that the most useful thing a healer could have was not a remedy. It was a record.

She did not show the notebook to anyone. She did not tell Mau she kept it. But every evening she wrote in it, and every evening she read it back from the beginning, and she watched the gaps between the entries get longer.

That was the medicine, she thought. The gaps.



The difficulty was Jinshi.

Arrival House had nothing for him to do. Nobody came over the bridge. The terms were in a folder on a shelf. The intake table had been wiped and wiped again until there was nothing left to wipe. Jinshi, who had run the most complicated piece of administration in the settlement through the worst ten days in its history without once raising his voice, found himself in the second week of winter with a clean desk and a quiet building and absolutely nothing to administer.

He did not take it well.

He came to the clinic. He stood in the doorway. He asked whether there was anything he could do. Maomao said no. He went away. He came back an hour later and asked again. Maomao said no again, with more feeling. He went away.

On the twenty-second, while she was at the washing court, he reorganized her shelves.

She came back to find every jar and bottle and paper packet in the medical store rearranged, in perfect alphabetical order, with new labels in his beautiful courtly hand, and Jinshi standing in the middle of it with the expression of a man who has finally been useful.

Maomao looked at the shelves for a long time.

"Aconite," she said, "is next to *arnica*."

"Alphabetically, yes."

"One of them is for bruises. The other one stops your heart."

Jinshi's expression changed slightly.

"They had very similar packets," he said.

"They have very similar *names*." Maomao put down her basket. "That is why they are on opposite ends of the room. With the thing that stops your heart on the top shelf, where a child who has just put a bean up her nose cannot reach it."

There was a pause.

"I'll put them back," said Jinshi.

"You'll put them back," said Maomao, "*by lethality*. I'll write you the order."

She wrote it for him, on the back of one of his beautiful labels, and he spent the rest of the afternoon putting every jar back in order of how quickly it could kill a person. He did it without complaint, very carefully, and when he had finished he stood back and looked at the shelves, from the harmless salves at the bottom to the small dark bottles at the very top, and said, "This is a much more interesting system."

"It's the only system," said Maomao.

She did not say thank you. But she left his labels on, the beautiful ones, every one of them. Kusuri, at the bench by the window, saw this, and said nothing, and smiled into her mortar for an hour.



At night the room was very quiet.

Jinshi lay on the cot against the far wall. Maomao lay in her bed against the near one. Between them was the width of the room and a small table with a lamp on it, and on the table, most nights, two cups. He made the tea. He had started doing it in the autumn, without asking, and she had stopped objecting in the autumn, without saying so. It was very good tea. He had brought it from somewhere, or traded for it, or simply known where it was, the way he knew where everything was.

On the twenty-third, she was writing in the notebook when he brought the cups, and she did not close it fast enough.

He was too polite to read it. But he was not too polite to see the heading at the top of the page, which was not about ugly days, and which was underlined twice.

*Revenge.*

He put the cup down beside her.

"Who?" said Jinshi.

Maomao closed the notebook.

"Two people," she said, "who think I didn't notice."

Jinshi sat down on the end of his cot with his own cup.

"Notice what?"

Maomao considered whether to tell him. Then she decided that a man who had spent an afternoon putting poisons in order of lethality had earned something.

"Once," she said, "in the long storm, someone raised the dose of a certain chocolate I had prepared at half strength, so that it would last an entire week instead of an evening. Without telling me." She sipped her tea. "And later, two people blamed a certain evening of theirs on that same chocolate. To my face. And I let them. Because it was not the moment." She put her cup down. "It is nearly the moment."

Jinshi was quiet for a while.

"Frieren," he said. "And Fern."

Maomao did not answer, which was an answer.

"May I help?"

"No."

"I only ask," said Jinshi, looking into his cup, "because the merchant who comes up from the south has not been able to sell cocoa since the roads went bad. Nobody wants luxuries when the bridge is empty. He has three sacks in his cellar going stale." He paused. "I could ask him. As a matter of administration."

Maomao looked at him across the width of the room for a long moment.

Then she opened the notebook again, turned to the page that said *Revenge*, and wrote something under it. She turned the book round so that he could read it.

*Assistant: J. (temporary).*

Jinshi read it. His face did not change at all, which was how she knew it had done something to him.

"Temporary," he said.

"Everything is temporary," said Maomao, "until it isn't." She took the notebook back. "Go to sleep."

He blew out the lamp.

In the dark, after a while, she heard him say, very quietly, from the far wall: "Three sacks."

"Go to *sleep*."

He went to sleep. She did not, for a while. She lay in the dark and listened to him breathe across the width of the room, and thought about chocolate, and about doses, and about the strip of paper at the foot of his bed, which had gone soft at the corners from being touched, though neither of them had ever touched it.

In the morning, she took it down.

She looked at it for a long moment. Then she took a pen from her apron, and under *temporary*, in small square letters, she wrote *(renewed)*, and pinned it back exactly where it had been.

Jinshi did not mention it. But he made the tea that evening a little stronger, the way she liked it, which he had never been told.
