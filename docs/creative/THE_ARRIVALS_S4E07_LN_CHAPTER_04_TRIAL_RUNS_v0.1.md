The trial runs had begun on a Saturday in the autumn, the week the plan for the real house was cut, and nobody had mentioned them since.

That was the arrangement. In the Muslin, walls were hooks and cloth, and moving a wall meant moving two hooks, and two hooks had moved. Fern's room and Stark's, side by side near the top of the main corridor, had become one room with a dotted line down the middle of the floor in chalk, where the wall used to be. And at the east end, Ori's bay, the one beside Mau and Frieren's with the door through the wall, had acquired a second bed and a dragon.

Nobody mentioned either. It was the most carefully unmentioned thing in the house. The Trial had come, and the window, and the ten days, and the trial runs had simply gone on through all of it underneath, the way a stream goes on under ice.

On the twenty-fourth, Wakana mentioned them.



He did it at breakfast, after the chalk squeak, with a pencil behind his ear and the plan of the real house rolled under his arm. He had been working on the plan all week, now that the window was closed and there was time to think about building again.

"I need to know about the walls," said Wakana.

The table went quiet.

"Not now," said Wakana. "Not today. But Rimuru's been talking about the ground, and Senku's been talking about the stone, and sooner or later I'm going to have to ink the upstairs." He unrolled the plan on the table between the bread and the soup, and put his finger on two places. "Here. Fern and Stark. Dotted. And here. Ori and Milim. A door, and a second bed, in pencil." He looked up. "I don't need an answer. I need to know when I'll get one."

Fern, at the head of the table, did not look up from the ledger.

"When the trial is over," she said.

"When's that?"

"When it's over."

Wakana considered this, and nodded, as if it were a perfectly reasonable engineering specification. He looked at the other end of the table.

"Ori?"

Ori had been eating her porridge. She put down her spoon.

"She doesn't kick," said Ori.

The whole table turned to look at Milim.

Milim, who had been trying to eat a third bowl without anyone noticing, stopped with the spoon in her mouth.

"I told you I don't kick," she said around it.

"You don't," said Ori. "I was surprised. Everyone said you would. Rimuru said you used to kick him out of bed when you stayed in his room." She picked up her spoon again. "You don't kick."

"Thank you," said Milim, with enormous dignity.

"You float," said Ori.

There was a pause.

"I don't float."

"You float. Every night. Around the middle of the night. You go up off the bed, very slowly, still asleep, and you go up until you touch the ceiling, and then you stay there." Ori ate a spoonful of porridge. "Like a balloon."

Milim had gone pink to the roots of her hair.

"I do *not*—"

"The first night, I didn't know what to do. I woke up and you were on the ceiling. I thought you'd fall." Ori looked thoughtful. "You didn't fall. You came down by yourself just before dawn. Very gently. Like a leaf."

At the other end of the table, Mau put his cup down carefully.

He knew this. He had done it himself, back when the leaks were bad and the flight pattern got out in his sleep. Frieren had tied his ankle to the bedpost with her hair ribbon so that he would not drift into the rafters, and he had woken up every morning that week with the ribbon round his ankle and her hand round the ribbon.

"So I fixed it," said Ori.

"How?" said Rimuru, who looked as though he were trying very hard not to laugh.

Ori reached into the pocket of her pinafore and took out a ribbon. Red, and long, and a little worn at one end where it had been knotted and unknotted.

"I tie it round her ankle," said Ori. "And round mine. So when she goes up, I know. And she can't go far." She put the ribbon back. "She doesn't mind. She sleeps better. She doesn't know she does it."

There was a silence at the long table.

Then Frieren, very quietly, beside Mau, said, "That's a good method."

"I learned it from you," said Ori.

Frieren looked at her across the table. Then she looked down at her own bowl, and her ears went faintly pink, and she did not say anything else.

Milim, who had been staring at Ori with her mouth slightly open through all of this, said suddenly and very loudly: "I'm not moving out."

"Nobody asked you to," said Ori.

"I'm just saying. If anyone was going to suggest it. I'm not."

"The trial," said Ori, "is going well."

Wakana looked at the two of them for a long moment. Then he took the pencil from behind his ear and, on the plan, beside the pencilled second bed in Ori's room, he drew a very small mark that might have been a balloon. He did not explain it. He rolled the plan up again.

"And Fern?" said Hakari, who never knew when to stop.

Every head turned the other way.

Fern did not look up from the ledger.

"The trial," said Fern, in exactly the voice she used for barrel counts, "is proceeding."

Stark, beside her, looked into his porridge with extraordinary concentration.

"Proceeding how?" said Hakari.

"Normally."

"Normally like—"

"Hakari," said Karane, and pulled her down by the back of her collar, and that was the end of that.



But Mau saw something, that night, that he did not tell anyone.

He was coming back from the roof late, past the top of the main corridor, in his socks, carrying his boots so as not to wake anyone. The cloth wall of Fern and Stark's room was not quite closed. There was a gap at the end, where the last hook had worked loose. Through it, in the faint light from the corridor lamp, he could see the chalk line on the floor down the middle of the room, where the wall had been.

On one side of the line, Stark was asleep, flat on his back, snoring very gently. He slept the way a rock sleeps.

On the other side, Fern was awake.

She was sitting up in bed with her knees drawn up and her arms around them. She was not reading. She was not doing anything. She was looking across the chalk line at Stark, asleep, with an expression Mau had never seen on her face. It took him a moment to place it, because he had only ever seen it on one other person, and that person was Frieren, at the end of a long day, when she thought nobody was looking.

He stood very still.

Fern looked at Stark for a long time. Then, very quietly, so as not to wake him, she got out of bed. She took hold of the foot of her bed, which was on castors, because Wakana put castors on everything, and pulled it. Slowly. One handspan. Across the chalk line.

Then she got back in, and lay down, and closed her eyes.

Mau went on down the corridor with his boots in his hand and did not make a sound.



At breakfast the next morning, the chalk line in Fern and Stark's room had been carefully redrawn, a handspan further over, so that it was once again exactly in the middle of the floor between the two beds.

Nobody knew this except Mau, and Mau did not say anything.

But when Wakana came to breakfast with the plan under his arm, Fern looked up from the ledger for the first time in two days and met his eyes.

"Pencil," said Fern. "Still pencil."

"Still pencil," Wakana agreed.

And then, because he was Wakana, and could not help it, he said, very gently: "But I'll leave the wall dotted."

Fern went back to the ledger. Her ears were red. Stark, beside her, ate his porridge, and did not look up, and was smiling at it.
