# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 10 — The Roof, Laughing

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 10

The roof had snow on it.

They had to clear a patch before they could sit, the three of them, on the twenty-sixth night: Yuta with the edge of his hand, Rimuru by simply flowing over the snow in his slime shape and then flowing back, so that the shingles came out wet and black and steaming faintly in the cold. Mau did not help. Rimuru told him he was not allowed to help, because the last time Mau had arrived on a roof in a hurry, he had stepped off a pantry ladder straight onto it in the middle of a spatial leak, about four seconds after Ciel had announced that this could not happen, and Ciel had never quite forgiven him.

*I have forgiven him,* said Ciel, to two heads. *I have not forgotten.*

"She says she's forgiven you," said Rimuru.

"I heard."

"She says she hasn't forgotten."

"I heard that too."

They sat down on the wet black patch with their backs against the chimney, which was warm from the kitchen stove underneath, and looked out over the settlement under the snow.

It was very beautiful. Mau had not expected that. He had been on this roof every night since the eighth day of the window, and every night it had been dark and cold, and he had sat between the two of them and talked about the white, or not talked about it, until Yuta said they could go down. Tonight the snow had changed everything. The yard was a clean white square. The ink tree stood in it, small and dark, with its ten leaves holding little caps of snow. The plaza beyond was white, and the roofs of Arrival House, and the long road to the bridge, a pale line with nobody on it, going off into the dark between the white fields. Here and there a window was lit. The washing court was a dark shape with a faint glow over it where somebody had banked a fire under the vats for the morning.

It was very quiet.

"It looks like a postcard," said Yuta.

"What's a postcard?" said Mau.

Rimuru and Yuta looked at each other.

"It's a picture," said Rimuru, "of a place, that you send to someone, to say you wish they were there."

Mau thought about this.

"That's sad," he said.

"It's not supposed to be sad."

"You only send it if they're not there."

"That's—" Rimuru stopped. "Huh," he said. "I never thought about it like that."



They did not talk about the white that night.

They had stopped needing to, Mau realized, somewhere in the last week. On the first nights of the window the roof had been where he said the things he could not say anywhere else. The white room. The knife. Maki's loop. The inn. Yuta and Rimuru had sat on either side of him and let him say them, and said nothing back, and that had been the point. But the things had been said now. All of them, at the long table, on the night of rule five, in front of everyone. There was nothing left that only the roof could hold.

So instead they talked about nothing. And at some point, because it was cold and late and the snow made everything strange, Yuta started telling a story about Gojo.

He did not tell stories about Gojo often. When he did, he told them carefully, the way you carry something that has already been broken once.

This one was not careful. This one was about the time Gojo had taken his whole first-year class, Maki, Panda, Toge and Yuta, on what he had sworn was a dangerous mission in the countryside. They had travelled for six hours. They had arrived at a small town in the hills, at night, braced for a curse of terrible power. And it had turned out to be a festival. A sweets festival. Gojo had bought out an entire stall of a particular kind of rice cake, eaten most of them himself, and then, when Maki demanded to know where the curse was, pointed at the last rice cake and said with total seriousness, *that one's cursed, I'll take care of it*, and eaten it.

"And then," said Yuta, "on the way home, there actually *was* a curse. On the train. A real one. A big one." He spread his hands. "And he couldn't fight it, because he'd eaten so many rice cakes he was lying across three seats with his blindfold over his face, groaning. So Maki fought it. And Panda. And me. And the whole time Gojo was lying there going *you're doing great, I'm so proud of you, I'm going to be sick.*"

Rimuru made a sound.

"That's Veldora," said Rimuru. "That's exactly Veldora. I left him alone with my manga collection for one afternoon. One. When I came back he'd read every volume of everything, out of order, and he was furious with me. *Furious.* Because I hadn't told him that the main character's friend dies in volume nine, and he'd read volume twelve first, and the friend was alive in twelve, because of a flashback, and he'd got attached." Rimuru's voice had gone very wounded. "He blamed me. For a year. He used to bring it up in meetings. In front of foreign ambassadors. *Rimuru, who let me believe a man was alive.*"

And Mau laughed.

It came out of him the way it had in the plaza on the eleventh morning, without warning, from somewhere very deep. But it was not like that laugh. That laugh had been mostly the other thing, the crying, with laughter on top of it. This was only laughing. At a dragon, in another world, furious about a flashback. At a man he had never met, lying across three train seats, telling his students he was proud of them. He laughed with his head back against the warm chimney and the snow going past and his breath going up white into the dark, and he could not stop, and he did not try.

Yuta was watching him.

So was Rimuru.

Neither of them said anything until he had stopped. Then Yuta said, quietly, "That's the first time."

Mau wiped his eyes.

"First time what?"

"You've laughed up here," said Yuta. "Every night since the eighth. This is the first time."

Mau thought back. He realized it was true.



It was getting very cold. Yuta flexed his hands, and Mau saw him do the thing he always did when his hands were cold. A thin film of cursed energy, barely visible, a faint shimmer over the skin like heat over a road, keeping the blood moving. Yuta had done it on the roof a dozen times. Mau had watched him do it a dozen times without thinking about it.

Mau's own hands were cold. He flexed them too, without thinking.

A faint shimmer came up over his skin. Like heat over a road.

He did not notice. He was looking at the snow.

Yuta noticed.

He went very still. Mau did not see it. Rimuru did. Rimuru turned his head, slowly, and looked at Mau's hands, and then at Yuta, and the two of them looked at each other over Mau's head for a long moment in the dark.

*Looked,* said Ciel, to two heads, very quickly, before anyone asked. *Quiet.*

Rimuru frowned.

*The seal is quiet,* said Ciel. *Nothing pressing. Nothing out. It did not come from the seal.* A pause. *I do not know where it came from.*

"Mau," said Yuta.

"Mm?"

"Look at your hands."

Mau looked. The shimmer was already fading, the way Yuta's always faded once his hands were warm. In a moment it was gone.

"What?" said Mau.

"You just did my thing," said Yuta.

"What thing?"

"The hands. The cursed energy. The warming." Yuta's voice was very level. "You just did it. Exactly how I do it. You've watched me do it all week."

Mau looked at his hands for a long time.

"I didn't do anything," he said. "I was cold. I just—" He stopped. "I didn't decide to."

"I know," said Yuta. "That's what I mean."

There was a silence on the roof.

Mau felt the old thing start, the cold in his chest. Something else he had not chosen. Something else inside him, working without him.

Then Yuta did something unexpected. He reached over and took one of Mau's hands in both of his and turned it over, the way you would look at a hand that had just learned to do something.

"It's warm," said Yuta.

Mau looked at him.

"That's all it did," said Yuta. "It made your hand warm. It's the most harmless thing I know how to do. It's the first thing I ever learned." He let go. "Rule five. I'm telling you because you'd want to be told. Not because it's bad."

"Ciel says it didn't come from the seal," said Rimuru.

"Then where—"

"She doesn't know." Rimuru looked at him steadily. "She says she'll look. And she'll tell you every time she does. Rule four."

Mau breathed out. The cold in his chest did not go away, but it got smaller.

"Tell Maomao," he said. "Tomorrow. So it's in the notebook."

"She keeps a notebook?" said Yuta.

"She thinks I don't know," said Mau.

Yuta laughed, a short surprised breath, and the moment broke, and the three of them sat with their backs against the warm chimney and watched the snow.



They went down a little after midnight. At the hatch, Yuta stopped.

"We can stop coming every night," he said.

Mau looked at him.

It had been in the contract, the roof. Not written, but understood: every night, until Yuta said. Mau had never asked when that would be. He had thought it might be never.

"Once a week," said Yuta. "Same as before. Before all of it."

"Is that because of the laugh?" said Mau.

Yuta looked at him in the dark for a moment, with snow on his shoulders.

"That's because of the laugh," he said.

He went down the ladder. Rimuru went after him, flowing down the rungs in his slime shape, because it was faster. Mau stayed at the top of the hatch a moment longer, looking out at the white settlement, and the pale empty road, and the ink tree with its ten leaves holding up their little caps of snow.

He flexed his hands. Nothing happened. They were only cold.

He went down.
