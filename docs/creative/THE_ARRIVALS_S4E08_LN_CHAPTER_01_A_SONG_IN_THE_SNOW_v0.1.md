# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 11 — A Song in the Snow

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 11  
**Continuity state:** Day 27 | Kessoku rehearse in the snow; Ryo: you can't hide in the reverb | refuge children watch; Bocchi freezes; Kita: three notes | the three notes become the start of the song; eleven times | the girl with the bean names it: 'Looked, Quiet' | Bocchi's first line: 'Every morning, someone looks' | Nijika and the live house

The music room was too warm for Ryo's opinions.

That was how Nijika explained it, afterward, to anyone who asked why Kessoku had been found rehearsing in the yard in the snow on the twenty-seventh day, in coats and scarves and fingerless gloves, with their breath going up in clouds.

The music room was downstairs at the kitchen end of the Muslin, with the thick buffer wall that Wakana had built so that Bocchi could play at three in the morning without waking anyone. In winter, with Momo's stove going on the other side of the wall all day, it was the warmest room in the house. It was so warm that Ryo had fallen asleep against her bass in the middle of the first verse, three days running, and on the third day had woken up and announced that the song was too slow, which it was not, and that she could not possibly form an opinion about tempo in a room this comfortable.

"Then where *can* you form an opinion?" said Nijika.

Ryo considered this. She looked out of the window at the yard, which was deep in fresh snow.

"Somewhere I'd rather die than fall asleep," said Ryo.

So they went outside.



It was very cold. That was the first thing. Bocchi's fingers went stiff on the strings within a minute. Kita's voice cracked on the first high note and came out as a sort of yelp. Nijika had brought her practice pad and two sticks, because the real drums would not survive the snow, and every time she hit it the sound went out flat and dead across the yard and did not come back.

"It's the snow," said Ryo, who was wide awake, which was alarming. "It eats sound. Listen."

They listened. The yard was very quiet. The kitchen chimney smoked. Somewhere in the Muslin a door closed, and the sound arrived softened, as if wrapped in cloth.

"Nothing echoes," said Ryo. "Everything just stops. You can't hide in the reverb." She plucked one string. The note went out and stopped. "If you play wrong out here, you hear it. Exactly. Nowhere to put it."

Bocchi had gone very pale under her scarf.

"That's a terrible place to play," she said, in a small voice.

"That's why it's good," said Ryo.

Nijika looked at Ryo for a long moment. Ryo, who could not be relied upon to pay rent, or remember to eat, or stay awake through a meeting, sometimes said things about music that were so exactly right that Nijika wanted to hit her with a drumstick.

"Okay," said Nijika. "From the top."



The song was the one Bocchi had been playing all week during the window. The slow circling thing, the one she had played in the corridor outside Mau's door on the days nobody could reach him, and on the crate in the plaza on the eleventh morning. It had no words yet. Kita had found the ending for it, the last turn where it stopped circling and went somewhere, on the eighth day, and they had been trying to build the rest of it around that ever since.

Out in the snow, with nowhere to hide, it was much harder.

They played it four times, badly. Bocchi's cold fingers missed the change in the bridge every time. Kita came in early. Ryo played it perfectly and with visible boredom, which was worse. Nijika kept time on a practice pad that made a sound like someone hitting a cushion.

On the fifth time, Bocchi looked up and saw that they had an audience.

There were children at the edge of the yard. Four of them, from the refuge families, the ones who had come through Arrival House after Last Defense and stayed. They were bundled to the eyes in mismatched coats, some of them Marin's, and they were standing in a row at the edge of the snow, watching. One of them was the girl who had put a bean up her nose. One of them was very small, and holding the hand of one of the others.

They were not saying anything. They were only watching, with the total, unembarrassed attention of children who have found something interesting.

Bocchi's hands stopped on the strings.

Mau, who had been splitting wood at the other side of the yard with the cooper and was pretending not to listen, saw it happen. He saw her go stiff. He saw her eyes go to the children, and then to the gate, and then to the door of the Muslin, measuring the distance, the way she measured distances to every exit in every room she had ever been in.

Then he saw Kita lean over, very casually, without stopping her own playing, and say something to her. He could not hear what. He did not need to. He had seen the evacuation. He knew what it was.

*Three notes.*

Bocchi looked at Kita. Then back at the children.

She played three notes.

Not the song. The three notes from the refuge, from the worst night in the settlement's history, when the children had been too frightened to move and Bocchi had played the same three notes over and over until they followed the sound. Slowly, out across the snow, with nowhere to hide them.

The smallest child, the one holding someone's hand, looked up sharply.

Mau saw her recognize it. She could not have been more than four. She would not have remembered much of that night. But something in her knew those three notes. She let go of the other child's hand and took a step forward into the snow, toward the sound, the way the children had in the refuge.

Bocchi saw it too.

She played the three notes again. And then, without stopping, she went from the third note straight into the first line of the song. The circling one. As if the three notes had always been how it started.

Kita came in with her. Then Ryo, who had stopped looking bored. Then Nijika, hitting the practice pad as if it were a real drum. And this time, out in the snow, with nowhere to hide, nobody came in early, and Bocchi did not miss the change in the bridge. When they got to the last turn, the one Kita had found, the place where it stopped circling and went somewhere, the four children at the edge of the yard were standing much closer than they had been, and the smallest one was sitting down in the snow with her mittens over her ears, not because it was too loud but because she had seen someone do it once and thought it was what you did when you liked something.

The song ended.

The snow ate the last note. It went out across the yard and stopped. Nothing came back.

Nobody said anything.

Then the girl who had put a bean up her nose said, in a carrying voice, "Again."



They played it eleven times. The children stayed for all eleven. By the sixth, two of the mothers from the refuge families had come out looking for them, and stayed too. By the ninth, Momo had come out of the kitchen with a tray of something hot, and Seiko had had her armchair carried to the door so that she could hear without getting cold. By the eleventh, there were nearly thirty people standing in the snow in the yard of the Muslin, with their breath going up white, listening to four girls in fingerless gloves play a song with no words.

Mau stopped pretending to split wood around the fourth time. The cooper had stopped around the second.

After the eleventh, Bocchi's fingers would not move anymore. She held them up to show everyone, red and stiff, and the children laughed, and she went pink to the ears and did not run away.

"What's it called?" said the girl with the bean.

Kessoku looked at each other.

"It doesn't have a name," said Kita.

"It has to have a name."

"It doesn't have words yet either," said Nijika. "We're working on it."

The girl considered this with great seriousness.

"Looked, quiet," she said.

The yard went very still.

"That's what you say," said the girl, "when everything's all right. Everyone says it. My mum says it in the mornings." She pointed at Bocchi with a mitten. "And it was quiet. And then you played. And it was all right." She shrugged, as if this were obvious. "Looked, quiet."

Bocchi looked at Kita. Kita looked at Nijika. Nijika looked at Ryo.

Ryo, who could not be relied upon for anything, said: "That's the name."

So it was.



That night, in the warm music room, with the stove going on the other side of the buffer wall, Bocchi sat with her guitar on her knee and a pencil behind her ear and a page of paper on the floor in front of her, and wrote the first line of the words.

She did not show anyone. She would not, for weeks. But Kita, coming in late to say goodnight, saw the page upside down from the doorway before Bocchi covered it with her hand. She saw only the title, at the top, in Bocchi's small crabbed writing. And under it, the first line, crossed out and written again three times, the same words each time:

*Every morning, someone looks.*

Kita did not say anything. She said goodnight, and went to bed, and lay awake for a long time smiling at the ceiling.

Across the corridor, Nijika had heard them through the wall, both of them. She lay in the dark with one hand in the pocket of her jacket, on a guitar pick and a hair tie and a small pale coin.

She was thinking about someone who used to run a little live house, in a basement, in another world. Someone who would have hated playing in the snow, and would have made them do it anyway, and would have stood at the back with her arms folded through all eleven times, pretending she was not listening.

She said the name under her breath, the way she did every night, to check that the word still worked.

Then she smiled at the ceiling too.
