# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 13 — The Watermelon and the Scientist

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 13  
**Continuity state:** Day 29 | Suika breaks her glasses; the watermelon helmet | Senku grinds a new lens all day | 'Has Senku decided?'; Chrome has someone; Gen would hate the cold | 'Senku's dad made Suika's village': Byakuya; 'some' | she sees a snowflake's six arms | 'Ten billion percent irrelevant'; he doesn't put the coin away

Suika fell over in the snow on the morning of the twenty-ninth and broke her glasses.

It was not a dramatic fall. She was crossing the yard with a basket of pears for the toll booth, and the snow had a crust on it that held for three steps and gave way on the fourth, and she went down sideways into a drift with the basket flying one way and the glasses the other. When she found them, a long time later, by feel, the left lens had a crack across it like a frozen river.

She did not cry. Suika did not cry about things like that. She put the watermelon on instead.

It was the old one, the shell helmet she had worn the whole of her childhood, before anyone had made her glasses, so that she would not have to see how blurry the world was. She kept it on the shelf above her bed in the room she shared with Umaru, and she had not worn it in a very long time. She put it on now, and pulled it down to her ears, and went to find Senku.



Senku was in the workshop, which was where Senku always was, and he looked up when the door opened and saw a watermelon.

He put down what he was holding.

"Glasses," he said.

"Suika fell," said the watermelon. "In the snow. Left lens." It held them out, roughly in his direction. It was about thirty degrees off. "Suika is sorry."

Senku took the glasses and held them up to the light. The crack went all the way across.

"Don't be sorry," he said. "Glass breaks. That's what glass is for." He turned them over. "I can grind you a new one. It'll take the day. I've got the blanks. The hard part's matching the curve to the right lens, so you don't see double."

"Suika can help."

Senku looked at the watermelon for a moment.

"You can't see," he said.

"Suika can hold things," said the watermelon, with dignity. "Suika is very good at holding things."

So Suika held things.



Grinding a lens takes a long time. Senku had told her this once, in another world, in another workshop, the first time he had made her a pair. He told her again now, because she liked hearing it, and because his hands knew the work well enough that his mouth needed something to do.

You start with a blank of good clear glass, as clear as you can make it, without bubbles. You fix it to a holder. You grind it against a curved tool with grit and water, round and round and round, coarse grit first, then finer, then finer, until the curve is right. Then you polish it. That takes the longest. Hours. You cannot hurry it. If you hurry, you scratch it, and then you start again.

Suika held the water jug. Then the jar of fine grit. Then the polishing cloth. She could not see any of it properly, only shapes and colours through the eyeholes of the watermelon. But she could hear the lens going round and round on the tool, a low gritty hiss, very steady. She could hear Senku breathing. She could smell the wet stone and the oil and the cold coming in under the workshop door.

"Senku," she said, around the middle of the afternoon.

"Mm."

"Has Senku decided?"

The hiss of the lens did not stop. But it changed, very slightly. A little slower.

"Fern's rule," said Senku. "Nobody spends till Day Forty."

"Suika knows. That's not what Suika asked."

The hiss went on.

"I'm thinking about it," said Senku. "Logically. You don't spend the most valuable resource in the settlement on a feeling. You spend it on whoever increases the survival odds of the most people by the largest margin." He tilted the lens to the light and looked at it, and put it back on the tool. "Chrome, maybe. He's the best partner I ever had. We'd have this place on electricity in a year." A pause. "Except Chrome's got someone. In his world. And I'd be pulling him out of it to come and build a generator for me." The lens went round. "That's not logical. That's just selfish with extra steps."

Suika did not say anything. She held the polishing cloth.

"Gen, maybe," said Senku. "Gen could talk the whole river country into trading with us again." Round and round. "Except Gen's happy where he is, probably. And he'd hate the cold."

"Senku," said Suika.

"Mm."

"Senku's dad made Suika's village."

The lens stopped.

It was the first time in the whole afternoon it had stopped. The hiss cut off and there was only the sound of the wind under the door, and the slow drip of water from the edge of the grinding tool onto the bench.

Suika could not see Senku's face. She could only see a shape, sitting very still.

"Byakuya," said Suika. "That was his name. The village told stories about him. Everybody's great-great-great-grandfather was in the stories. Suika didn't know he was a real person until Senku came, and Ruri told the very last story, and it was his dad, talking to him." She held the cloth very carefully in both hands. "And Senku listened to all of it. And didn't say anything. And then said it was ten billion percent irrelevant, and went back to work."

The shape did not move.

"Byakuya's dead," said Senku. His voice was perfectly level. "Thousands of years. In my world. Long before I woke up."

"The Goddess said *some*," said Suika. "Rem asked, about the dead. The Goddess said some."

The silence went on for a long time.

Then the lens started going round again. Slowly. The low gritty hiss.

"It's ten billion percent irrelevant," said Senku.

"Suika knows," said Suika.

She did not say anything else. She held the polishing cloth, and the lens went round, and they did not talk about it again all afternoon.



It took until dusk.

Senku polished the new lens until there was no scratch on it anywhere that he could find with the magnifier, and then he polished it some more, and then he fitted it into the frame beside the old good right lens and tightened the little screws with a tool so fine Suika had never seen it, and held the glasses up to the lamp and looked through them at the far wall, first one eye and then the other.

"Done," he said. "Take that thing off."

Suika took the watermelon off.

The workshop was a blur of lamplight and shadow. Then Senku put the glasses on her nose, carefully, with both hands, and settled them behind her ears.

And the world came back.

Sharp. All at once. The bench. The tools. The grain in the wood. Senku's face, very close, tired and smudged with grit, looking at her with an expression she did not have a name for.

"Can you see?" he said.

She did not answer. She was looking past him, at the workshop window.

It was snowing again. Fat flakes, the size of a thumbnail, coming down slowly past the glass in the last blue light of the day. And some of them were landing on the window, on the cold outside of the pane, and sticking there for a moment before they melted.

Suika went to the window. She put her face very close to the glass.

The flake on the other side of the glass was not a blur. It was not a white dot. It was a shape. Six arms. Each arm with smaller arms coming off it, and smaller arms coming off those, all exactly the same, all perfectly balanced, like a tiny frozen tree that had grown in six directions at once.

She had never seen one. Not properly. Not in her whole life. In the village, before the glasses, snow had been white blur. Then there had been glasses, but in summer. And then she had come here.

"Senku," she said, very quietly.

"It's a crystal," said Senku, behind her. "Water freezes in a hexagonal lattice. Six sides. It's the shape the molecules want to be. Every arm grows the same because every arm's in the same air at the same temperature at the same time." He came and stood beside her. "No two are exactly the same, because no two fall through exactly the same air. But they all follow the same rule."

Suika looked at the snowflake until it melted. Then she looked at the next one.

"Suika can see them," she said.

"Yeah," said Senku.

"All the arms."

"Yeah."

"Suika didn't know they had arms."

Senku did not say anything. He stood at the window beside her and watched the snowflakes land on the glass and grow their six arms and melt. Neither of them moved for a long time. The lamp burned down.

Then Umaru came looking for Suika for dinner, and found the two of them standing at the workshop window in the dark like a pair of statues, and had to drag them both out by the sleeves.



Later that night, after everyone had gone to bed, Senku went back to the workshop.

He did not light the lamp. He sat at the bench in the dark, among the tools, and took his coin out of his pocket and held it, small and pale, in his palm.

He sat there for a long time.

"Ten billion percent irrelevant," he said, to nobody.

He did not put it away.
