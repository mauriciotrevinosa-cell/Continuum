# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 14 — Ink, Counted

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 14  
**Continuity state:** Day 30 | Agott counts: one full bottle left; two winters, maybe three | Coco asks for one drop to test an inverted crystal-binding lattice; Qifrey: no to the ink, not the shape | Agott: draw it in water | eighty-one times; the last ten right | the ink tree: 'Ninety-nine. And some months.' | 'For WHEN there's ink'

Agott counted the ink again on the thirtieth, because Agott counted the ink every month, whether anyone asked her to or not.

She did it the way Qifrey had done it the first time, on a clean cloth on the atelier floor, every bottle and vial and small sealed pot in a row. There were fewer than there had been the first time. The second-fullest bottle, the one that had been most of the shadowless light, was nearly empty now: a dark ring at the bottom, a skin of silver on the glass where the light over the common-room scratches had drawn from it, night after night, every night since. The cheap little bottle that had made Kaneki's air glyph was gone altogether. There was one full bottle left. One. And four others in various states of not-full, and the one at the end of the row that was barely a stain, which nobody had ever been willing to throw away.

Agott wrote the new total on the back of her hand, under the old one, and crossed the old one out.

The others watched her do it. Coco, Tetia and Richeh, cross-legged on the floor around the cloth. Qifrey, in his chair by the window, with his hands folded and his face unreadable.

"That's everything," said Agott.

Nobody needed to ask everything of what.



Richeh broke the silence, because Richeh was a guest, and guests were allowed to say things.

"How long does that last?"

"At this rate?" Agott looked at the back of her hand. "The light uses a little every night. A very little. But every night." She did the arithmetic in her head, which she had already done three times that morning. "Two winters. Maybe three. If nobody draws anything else."

"And if somebody draws something else?"

"Then less."

Richeh looked at the row of bottles.

"And then?"

Agott did not answer. Nobody answered. Then was the thing they did not talk about. Then was the day the light over the scratches went out, and the five lamps came back, and the house went back to oil and long shadows. Then was the day Coco and Agott and Tetia and Richeh stopped being witches, and were only four girls in a strange world with a teacher who could not teach them anything, because the one thing their art needed was gone.

"And then the tree," said Coco.

They all looked at her.

"There's a tree," said Coco. "It's growing. It has ten leaves." She said it very steadily. "Qifrey said the tree he came from took a hundred years before anyone could draw ink from it. So we plan for a hundred years. I said that. At the start. Everyone heard me."

"A hundred years," said Tetia softly.

"None of us will be here in a hundred years," said Richeh, the way she had said it the first time.

"Frieren will," said Coco. "She said she'd wait."

Agott was looking at the back of her hand, at the number that would run out in two winters, maybe three.

"That's a long time to wait," she said, "with no ink."



Coco had a request.

She made it in the afternoon, after lunch, when the others had gone back to their own work: Tetia to her water circles, Richeh to the drainage glyph she had been half-inventing for months, Agott to a book. Coco came and stood by Qifrey's chair at the window with the heavy book about crystalline glyph structures held against her chest, and Qifrey looked up from his own reading and waited.

"One drop," said Coco.

Qifrey did not say anything.

"From the little bottle at the end. The one that's nearly empty. It's barely anything. Nobody would even—" She stopped. "I want to test something. A shape. I found it in here." She held up the book. "It's not a glyph exactly. It's a structure, the way the old crystal-binding glyphs were built. I think if you drew it backwards, inverted, the way you'd undo a binding instead of making one—" She stopped again. Her knuckles were white on the book. "I think it might do something. To crystal. To—" She did not finish.

Qifrey knew what she did not finish. He had her coin in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left side.

"On a guess," he said gently.

"It's not a guess, it's—"

"Coco." He did not raise his voice. He never did. "If you had ten bottles, I would give you the drop. I would give you a bottle. I would sit beside you and watch you draw it and argue with you about the angles." He looked at the row of bottles on the cloth across the room. "We have one. And four that aren't. And the light."

Coco's eyes had gone bright.

"I know," she said.

"I'm not saying no to the shape," said Qifrey. "I'm saying no to the ink. Those are different things."

"How can I test the shape without the ink?"

From across the room, without looking up from her book, Agott said: "Water."

Coco turned round.

"Draw it in water," said Agott. She turned a page. "Richeh's been drawing a drainage glyph in water on the floor for months. It doesn't *do* anything. Water isn't ink. But she knows the shape now. She could draw it in her sleep." Another page. "Draw yours a hundred times in water. Get the angles right. Get every line right. Then, when there's ink, you won't waste a drop finding out you drew it wrong."

Coco stared at her.

"When there's ink," Coco repeated.

"When," said Agott, and did not look up.

Tetia, at her water circles by the far wall, had stopped moving.

Richeh, at the drainage glyph, said, very quietly, "She's right, you know. I've drawn mine about four hundred times. I know where every line goes. It's the only thing I'm sure of in this whole world."



So Coco drew it in water.

She knelt on the atelier floor with a bowl and a brush and drew it, and watched it dry, and drew it again. The shape was complicated. A lattice, six-sided, like the snowflakes Suika had been talking about at breakfast, with lines inside lines, folding back on themselves, every angle exactly opposite to the binding it was meant to undo. She got it wrong the first time, and the second, and the tenth. The water dried pale on the boards and left nothing. She drew it again.

Around the fifteenth time, Tetia came and knelt beside her with a second brush.

Around the thirtieth, Richeh came too. She looked at the shape for a long time, and then she said, "That angle's wrong. There. If it's undoing, the corner has to open the other way, or it just binds again." Coco looked, and drew it Richeh's way, and Richeh was right.

Around the fiftieth, Agott put her book down.

She did not come over. She sat in her chair across the atelier with her arms folded and watched, and every so often she said something sharp and short without looking as if she had meant to: "Third line's too long." "You're rushing the inside." "That's better." Coco did not look up. But she did what Agott said, every time.

Qifrey watched all four of them from his chair by the window, with his hands folded and his face unreadable, and did not say anything at all. Mau, coming past the open atelier door with a load of wood around the seventieth time, saw his face and kept walking, quietly, because it was not his to see.

By dusk, Coco had drawn it eighty-one times. The last ten had been right. She knew they were right the way Richeh knew her drainage glyph was right, in her hands and not her head. She could have drawn it with her eyes closed.

It did nothing. It was water. It dried pale on the boards and left no mark.

But she knew the shape now.



At dusk the four of them went out to the ink tree.

Nobody suggested it. They put on their coats and went out across the yard through the snow, Coco in front, then Tetia, then Richeh, then Agott last with her hands in her pockets, as if she had happened to be going the same way. Qifrey stood at the atelier door and watched them go.

The tree stood in its ring of stakes in the middle of the yard, small and thin and stubborn, with its ten leaves holding up little caps of snow. Behind it was the board on Wakana's stake. WHO WATERS IT. *Everyone.* And three names in charcoal, which Coco had rewritten after every rain, and would keep rewriting.

Coco took off one mitten and touched a leaf, very gently, with one finger. The snow fell off it. Underneath, the leaf was silver-green and cold and perfectly alive.

"A hundred years," she said.

"Ninety-nine," said Agott, behind her. "And some months."

Everyone turned to look at her.

Agott shrugged, very slightly, with her hands still in her pockets.

"Mau said it," she said. "To Frieren. *Ninety-nine years and some months to go.*" She looked at the tree. "She called him an idiot. I heard. Everyone heard. They weren't quiet."

Coco laughed. It came out wet, because she had been very close to crying all day without letting herself. Tetia laughed too, and Richeh, and after a moment, very briefly, so that anyone not looking would have missed it, Agott did.

They stood round the tree in the snow until it was dark.



That night, Coco took out her notebook. Not her drawing book. The other one, the private one, the one she did not show anyone. She opened it to a clean page and, with a pencil, very carefully, so that every angle was right, she drew the shape. The lattice. Six sides. Every line opening the other way.

Under it she wrote: *For when there's ink.*

Then, after a moment, she crossed out *when* and wrote it again, harder.

*For WHEN there's ink.*

She closed the notebook and put it under her pillow and lay in the dark in the Witch Hat room, with Tetia breathing on one side of her and Agott pretending to be asleep in her alcove on the other, behind the dark green curtain with the silver stars.

Somewhere out in the yard, in the snow, the tree stood in its ring of stakes with its ten leaves, growing at the speed trees grow, which is the speed of a hundred years.
