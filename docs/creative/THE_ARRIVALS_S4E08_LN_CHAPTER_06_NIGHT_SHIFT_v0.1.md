# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 16 — Night Shift

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 16  
**Continuity state:** Night of Day 32 | Nazuna, Anko and Kaneki awake on purpose | Kaneki's coffee like a funeral | walking the edge: huge tracks from the north turn forty paces from the last cottage | Anko: the Goddess's protection, seen; it does not cover the road | Kaneki: 'It isn't one person.' / 'So you'd need two.' | two cups on the rack

There were three people in the house who were awake at three in the morning on purpose.

Nazuna was the first, because Nazuna was always awake at three in the morning. She slept in the high loft in the room on the dark north side, behind the triple-lined black curtain, from dawn until the middle of the afternoon, and then she came down and was a person until dawn again. In summer this had meant she missed most of the house. In winter, with the nights so long, she had nearly fourteen hours of dark, and she spent them wandering the Muslin in a borrowed coat, reading other people's books, eating other people's leftovers, and occasionally sitting on the end of somebody's bed until they woke up and screamed.

Anko was the second, because Anko did not sleep much. She never had. In another life, in a city full of night, she had been a detective who hunted things that only came out in the dark, and she had learned to be awake when they were. She had stopped hunting them. She had moved into a room on the north side of a house in another world with one of them, and taken the bed under the loft, *always in the same room*, and she still did not sleep much. She sat up with her notebook and a lamp turned very low, and wrote things down.

Kaneki was the third.

Nobody knew exactly why Kaneki was awake at three in the morning. He did not explain. He came into the kitchen, most nights, around the hour when the stove was banked and the room was dark and warm, and made himself a cup of coffee with a care that was almost ceremonial, and sat at the kitchen table and drank it very slowly, and did not seem to want anything else.

On the thirty-second night, all three of them were in the kitchen at once.



It was not the first time. But it was the first time anyone had said anything.

Nazuna said it, because Nazuna would say anything to anyone at any hour. She was sitting on the kitchen counter with her legs swinging and a cold potato in her hand from the evening's leftovers, and she looked at Kaneki across the dark kitchen, at his careful coffee, and said:

"Why are you always up?"

Kaneki looked at her over the cup.

"Why are you?"

"I'm a vampire," said Nazuna. "It's literally my whole thing."

"I'm a ghoul."

"Ghouls sleep at night. I've read about you. I read everything." She bit the potato. "You don't sleep at night because you're thinking about something. You make coffee like it's a funeral."

Anko, at the end of the table with her notebook and her low lamp, did not look up. But her pencil stopped.

Kaneki turned the cup round in his hands.

"Habit," he said.

"Liar," said Nazuna cheerfully.

He did not deny it.



Outside the kitchen window, the snow had stopped. The sky had cleared, and the moon was up, very bright, so that the whole yard was silver and the shadows were black and sharp as cut paper. The ink tree in its ring of stakes cast a shadow like a small dark hand.

Anko closed her notebook.

"I'm walking the edge," she said. "Anyone coming?"

She did this most nights. Not a patrol. There were no patrols anymore, not since the window closed. Only a walk, alone, around the edge of the settlement where the last houses gave way to the white fields, the way she had once walked the edges of a city, because she could not sleep, and because walking the edge of a place told you things about it that sitting in the middle never would.

"Me," said Nazuna, and jumped off the counter.

Kaneki finished his coffee. He rinsed the cup. He put it upside down on the rack, exactly where it went.

Then he got his coat.



They walked the edge in the moonlight, the three of them, in the snow.

It was very cold and very quiet. Their boots squeaked. Nazuna did not seem to feel the cold at all, and walked ahead of the other two with her borrowed coat open, kicking at the drifts. Anko walked with her hands in her pockets and her eyes everywhere. Kaneki walked beside her and said nothing, and his breath went up white in the moonlight.

They went round the back of the washing court, and past the slope, where the hay bales sat at the bottom under a new skin of snow, and the trays were stacked against the wall for the morning. Past the last cottages of the refuge families. Out to where the plaza road became the bridge road, and the bridge road ran away north between the white fields toward the river, a pale line with nobody on it.

Anko stopped there. She stood at the edge of the last cottage and looked down the road for a long time.

Then she crouched and looked at the snow.

"Nazuna," she said. "Come and look at this."

Nazuna came back. Kaneki came with her.

There were tracks in the snow.

Not on the road. Beside it. Out in the white field, perhaps fifty paces from the last cottage, a line of tracks came down out of the dark from the north, from the direction of the hills. They were large. Very large. Each print was the size of a cartwheel, deep and wide and splayed, with marks at the front that might have been claws. They came down the field in a long straight line toward the settlement, steady, unhurried, the tracks of something that knew where it was going.

And then, about forty paces from the edge of the last cottage, they stopped.

Not stopped. Turned. The line came down to a certain point in the snow and then bent, sharply, as if whatever had made it had walked into something it could not see, and stood there for a while, and changed its mind. The prints at the turn were churned and doubled, as if it had paced back and forth. Then the line went away again, east, along the edge of the fields, keeping the same distance from the cottages all the way, and disappeared into the dark toward the forest.

The three of them looked at it for a long time.

"Something big," said Nazuna, quietly, for once.

"Something big," Anko agreed. "That came to have a look." She stood up. "And couldn't."

Kaneki was looking at the place where the tracks turned. His face had gone very still, the way it went still at triage, when he could hear something under the skin that nobody else could.

"There's a line there," he said. "In the snow. I can't see it. But it walked into one."

Anko had her notebook out. She was writing by moonlight, quickly.

"The Goddess's protection," she said, without looking up. "In the Trial. She took it away when they said no, and gave it back when he went in. Nobody's ever seen where it starts." She looked at the turned tracks. "Now we have."

"So it works," said Nazuna.

"It works," said Anko. "On us." She closed the notebook. "It doesn't go to the bridge. Look." She pointed down the road. "The road runs out past the line. Straight through it. Out there—" she nodded at the white fields beyond the turn, at the dark hills where the tracks had come from "—there's nothing at all."

Nobody said anything.

Behind them, the settlement slept. Inside the line.



They walked back slowly.

Halfway back, by the slope, Nazuna said, without looking round, "You've got someone, haven't you."

Kaneki did not answer.

"You make coffee like a funeral," said Nazuna, "and you're up every night, and on the bridge you told Eren a name, and you haven't told anybody else." She kicked a drift. "Has anyone decided. Everybody's been asking it all week. You're the only one who hasn't answered."

Kaneki walked for a while in silence.

"It isn't one person," he said.

Nazuna stopped walking.

Anko did not stop. But her head came round.

"The Goddess said one," said Kaneki. He was looking at the snow in front of his feet. "One person. Someone you love. One coin, one person." He breathed out, white. "It isn't one person."

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

"So you'd need two," said Anko quietly.

Kaneki did not confirm it. He did not deny it either. He walked on, past the slope, past the washing court, toward the Muslin with its one lit window in the kitchen where nobody was.

Nazuna and Anko looked at each other.

"Don't," said Anko.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to tell the whole fort by breakfast."

"I was going to tell *you*," said Nazuna. "Which I just did. Which you already knew." She hunched her shoulders in the borrowed coat. "I'm not telling anyone. It's his."

Anko looked at her for a long moment in the moonlight.

Then she took one hand out of her pocket and put it, very briefly, on the back of Nazuna's neck, under her hair, where it was cold. Nazuna went still under it, the way a cat goes still.

"Good," said Anko, and took her hand back, and walked on.



In the kitchen, Kaneki made a second cup of coffee.

He did not usually make a second. He made it with the same ceremonial care as the first, and sat at the kitchen table with it, and did not drink it. He held it in both hands, and looked at the dark window, at the moon on the snow, at the ink tree's small black shadow.

Anko came in and sat down across from him. She did not open her notebook. Nazuna sat on the counter and did not eat anything.

The three of them sat in the dark kitchen together until the window began to go grey.

Then Nazuna yawned, enormously, and said, "That's me," and went up to her loft behind the black curtain. Anko went to the room on the north side and lay down on the bed under the loft and, for once, slept. Kaneki rinsed both cups, the drunk one and the undrunk one, and put them upside down on the rack, side by side, exactly where they went.

He stood looking at the two cups for a moment.

Then he went to bed.

In the morning, Anko's notebook was on the long table, open, in her clear hand, where anyone who read the evidence wall would read it too:

*Night of 32. Tracks, large, north field. Turned 40 paces from last cottage. Line holds. Line does not cover road.*

And under it, smaller, the beginning of a second line, crossed out so thoroughly that nobody could read what it had been.
