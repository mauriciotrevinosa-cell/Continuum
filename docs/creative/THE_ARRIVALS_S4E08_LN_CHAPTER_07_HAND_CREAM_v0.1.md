The three sacks of cocoa arrived on the thirty-third day, on the back of the southern merchant's cart, and Senku noticed them before they were even unloaded.

He noticed everything that came into the settlement on a cart. It was a habit from another world, where every delivery had been a resource and every resource had been counted. He was standing at the workshop door with a cup of tea when the merchant's cart came creaking up from the bridge road, and he watched the merchant and Jinshi unload three fat burlap sacks and carry them, not to the kitchen, not to the stores, but round the side of the Muslin to the door of the medical room.

He finished his tea.

Then he went to the medical room.



Maomao was standing over the three sacks with one of them open, running a handful of dark brown beans through her fingers and smelling them, with an expression Senku had last seen on her face when she discovered a new kind of mushroom that could kill a horse.

Kusuri was at the bench by the window, watching her with narrowed eyes.

"Cocoa," said Senku.

"Cocoa," Maomao agreed, without looking up.

"Three sacks."

"Three sacks."

"What for?"

Maomao let the beans run back into the sack. She dusted her hands. She looked at him with an expression of perfect, serene innocence, which was the most alarming expression Maomao possessed.

"Hand cream," she said.

There was a pause.

Senku looked at the sacks. Then at Maomao. Then at Kusuri, who was looking at Maomao with her eyes narrowed even further.

"Hand cream," said Senku.

"The washing court," said Maomao. "Everyone's hands are cracking. Red to the elbow. Chilblains. I've had eleven split knuckles through the clinic this week. The lye does it, and the cold does the rest." She tapped the open sack. "Cocoa butter. You press the beans, you get the fat, and the fat is the best thing in any world for skin that's been in cold water. It melts at body temperature. It seals the crack. It doesn't go rancid for a year."

Senku looked at her for a long moment.

"That's true," he said slowly.

"I know it's true."

"Cocoa butter's a genuinely excellent emollient. Stable. High in saturated fat. Low oxidation rate." He was frowning. "That's actually a really good idea."

"I know it's a good idea."

"So why," said Kusuri, from the bench, "do you need *three sacks?*"

Maomao did not even blink.

"Because there are a lot of hands," she said.



They spent the afternoon making hand cream, the three of them, and it was the most dangerous afternoon the medical room had seen since the shelves were arranged alphabetically.

Not because anything went wrong. Because nothing did.

The trouble with Maomao and Senku, as the whole house had learned long ago, was not when they disagreed. When they disagreed they argued, loudly and at length, and the house could get on with its business around them. The trouble was when they agreed. When Maomao and Senku agreed about a piece of chemistry, they agreed completely, at speed, in a language of half-sentences that nobody else could follow. Then they built something, and by the time anyone else understood what it was, it was finished. Usually it was wonderful. Occasionally it was on fire.

Kusuri made it worse. Kusuri was a pharmacologist, a genius, and seventeen, and she had been trained in a world where a girl her age could cure or poison a city with what she carried in her satchel. When Maomao and Senku agreed, Kusuri understood what they were agreeing about about a second and a half before they did, and said so, and then all three of them were agreeing.

"Roast first," said Senku. "Low. Just enough to—"

"—loosen the shell," said Maomao, "yes, not enough to—"

"—scorch the fat," said Kusuri, "because then it smells like—"

"—burnt toast, yes," said all three of them, and the beans went into the low oven.

They winnowed the shells off in the yard, in the cold, tossing the cracked beans in a wide basket so that the light shells blew away on the wind and the heavy nibs fell back. The refuge children came to watch, because it looked like a game, and Maomao let them toss the basket, because the wind did the work anyway and children liked feeling useful. Then the nibs went into Senku's press in the workshop, the one he had built for seed oil. He turned the screw, and a thick golden liquid began to seep out at the bottom and drip into the bowl, and harden almost at once in the cold into a pale cream-coloured fat that smelled, overwhelmingly, of chocolate.

The whole workshop smelled of chocolate. Then the yard. Then, by the middle of the afternoon, the entire Muslin.

People began to appear. Milim first, with her nose in the air like a hound. Then Umaru. Then most of the fort, drifting into the yard on various pretexts, looking hopeful.

"It's hand cream," Maomao told them, flatly, from the workshop door.

"It smells like chocolate," said Milim.

"It's hand cream that smells like chocolate."

"Can I eat it?"

"You can put it on your hands," said Maomao, "and then lick your hands, if you are an animal." She looked at Milim. "I can't stop you."

Milim considered this seriously.



By dusk they had forty small clay pots of it. Kusuri had blended the cocoa butter with a little beeswax from a woman in the refuge families who kept bees, and a few drops of something from her satchel that she said would make it sink in faster and would not tell anyone the name of. Senku had worked out the ratio of butter to wax so that it would stay solid in a cold coat pocket and melt the instant it touched skin. Maomao had tested it on her own hands, and then on Kusuri's, and then on the cooper's split thumb, which had nearly healed, and pronounced it acceptable, which from Maomao was a medal.

They lined the forty pots up on the workshop bench.

"The washing court," said Maomao. "Tomorrow. One pot per trough. And the clinic keeps ten."

"That's twenty-five pots," said Kusuri.

"Yes."

"We made forty."

"We'll make more," said Senku. "There's two and a half sacks left."

Kusuri looked at Maomao.

Maomao looked at the forty pots, with her serene innocent face, and said nothing.

"Two and a half sacks," Kusuri repeated, very slowly, "of cocoa."

"For hand cream," said Maomao.

"For *hand cream.*"

"A lot of hands," said Maomao. "It's a long winter."

Senku, who had been cleaning the press, looked up. He looked at Maomao, and at Kusuri, and at the two and a half sacks against the wall. Something passed over his face, the expression of a scientist noticing a variable he had not accounted for, and then, very deliberately, deciding not to account for it.

"Ten billion percent hand cream," said Senku, and went back to cleaning the press.

Kusuri stared at him.

"You know," she said. "You *know* it's not—"

"I know cocoa butter is an excellent emollient," said Senku, scrubbing, "and I know three people just made forty pots of it, and I know there's two and a half sacks left." He did not look up. "What anyone does with the rest is not a chemistry question."

Maomao smiled.

It was a very small smile. Kusuri had worked with her for over a year and had seen it perhaps twice. It was the smile of a woman who had just found out that her co-conspirator had been co-conspiring all along, without being asked, out of what could only be called professional courtesy.

Kusuri put her head down on the bench.

"I'm going to have to watch this happen," she said, into the wood. "Aren't I. I'm going to have to sit at dinner and watch it."

"Yes," said Maomao.

"You could at least tell me who."

Maomao picked up a pot of hand cream, and opened it, and smelled it, and put a little on the back of Kusuri's hand, and rubbed it in, slowly, with her thumb.

"Two debts," said Maomao. "And whoever's sitting next to them."



In the morning, Mau carried a crate of twenty-five clay pots to the washing court, because he was going anyway.

He put one by each trough, without saying anything, the way Maomao had told him to. The women and men at the troughs looked at the pots, and opened them, and smelled them, and their faces changed. Somebody laughed. Somebody said, "It smells like a sweet shop." Somebody tried it on her cracked red knuckles and went very quiet, and then said, "Oh," in the voice of a person who has been in pain for a month and has stopped being, all at once.

He put the last one by the big trough at the far end, by the drain, next to a blue bucket.

The large red-handed woman was singing, badly, wringing a sheet. She did not stop. She did not look at him. She looked at the pot.

He went to the small trough by the steps and did his washing.

When he looked up, a long time later, the pot by the big trough was open. And the woman's hands, wringing the sheet, red to the elbow and cracked across every knuckle, were shining very faintly in the steam, the way skin shines when someone has just put something on it.

She was still singing. Badly. A little louder than yesterday.

He wrung his shirts the right way, with the weave, and went home.
