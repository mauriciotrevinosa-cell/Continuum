# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 18 — The Party

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 18  
**Continuity state:** Day 34 | Himmel's quest: the old inn's roof leak; six grimoires on the top shelf | 'We were four': Heiter's wine, Eisen's one word, Himmel's posing | the roof fixed; 'a strategic descent' | Frieren makes room on the bench between her and Himmel | Himmel watches the northern hills | 'I want to remember both'

It was Himmel's idea, which was how everyone knew it was going to involve posing.

"A quest," said Himmel, at breakfast on the thirty-fourth day, after the chalk squeak. "A proper one. Like old times."

Fern did not look up from the ledger.

"There's a leak in the old inn's roof," she said. "Rem sent word with the cart. Snow got under the shingles over the long room. Somebody has to go and fix it."

"Exactly," said Himmel. "A quest."

"It's a roof."

"It's a roof," Himmel agreed, "four hours away by road, in deep snow, in winter, in need of brave and skilled adventurers to restore it." He looked down the table. "I need a party."

Stark, beside Fern, had already put his spoon down with the resigned expression of a man who knew exactly where this was going.

Frieren, beside Mau, turned a page of her grimoire.

"I'm busy," she said.

"Your book will still be there tomorrow."

"I'm busy reading it today."

"Frieren," said Himmel, gently. "There's a shelf in the old inn's long room. The top one. Behind the cups. Where you put the grimoires you'd finished, in the first winter, because there wasn't room in your bag." He paused. "Rem says they're still there. Six of them."

Frieren closed her grimoire.

"I'll get my coat," she said.



So they went: Himmel, Frieren, Fern and Stark, the four of them, and Mau, because Frieren took his sleeve on her way to the door and did not let go of it.

They took the cart and the old horse from the stables by Arrival House, and a ladder, and a box of new shingles Wakana had split the week before, and a bag of nails, and a basket of food from Momo that was enough for twelve people for a week. The road north out of the settlement was white and empty and very quiet. Snow lay deep in the fields on either side. The horse plodded. Stark drove. Fern sat beside him with a blanket over both their knees, very upright, pretending not to be leaning on him. Himmel sat on the tail of the cart with his legs hanging off, looking at everything with the bright, pleased attention of a man on his way to an adventure. Frieren sat in the back among the shingles with her grimoire, not reading it, and Mau sat beside her.

About a mile out, Himmel turned round.

"We were four," he said.

Nobody answered. He was not really talking to anyone.

"The four of us," said Himmel. "When we set out. Me. Frieren. Heiter. Eisen." He was smiling, but it was the careful smile. "Heiter would have brought wine. Far too much wine. In a bag that clinked, so that everyone in every village knew we were coming."

"Eisen wouldn't have said anything," said Stark, from the driver's seat, without turning round. "The whole trip. Not one word. And then when we got there he'd have said one thing, and it would have been exactly the right thing, and then nothing again for a week."

Fern glanced at him.

"That's what he was like with me," said Stark. "Anyway."

"And I'd have posed," said Himmel. "On every hill."

There was a pause.

"You still pose on every hill," said Frieren.

"I know," said Himmel happily. "Some things a man doesn't give up."



The old inn was smaller than Mau remembered.

That was the first thing. He had not been back since before the window. In his memory it was enormous: the long room where they had all eaten together in the first year, the corridor of rooms where the household had first become a household, the yard where he had split wood the first month and watched for a deer that never came close. In his memory it was the whole world.

It was a small, low, sturdy building with snow on its roof, in a clearing, with smoke coming out of the chimney. That was all.

Rem was at the door. Lise was behind her. Rem looked at the five of them getting down from the cart, with the ladder and the shingles and Himmel already looking at the roof with a gleam in his eye, and said, "The leak's over the long room. The tea's ready." She looked at Frieren. "They're still on the shelf."

Frieren went in without a word.

The rest of them went up on the roof.



It was not, in the end, much of a quest. The leak was where Rem had said: a patch of shingles over the long room where ice had built up in the gutter and forced its way underneath. Stark knocked the ice off. Mau and Fern pulled the rotten shingles. Himmel nailed the new ones on, with great care and much commentary, and stopped every few minutes to stand up straight on the ridge of the roof, with one foot on the chimney and the hammer raised, and look out over the white country.

"Stop doing that," said Fern, every time.

"I'm surveying."

"You're posing."

"A hero can do both."

"A hero can fall off a roof."

"I've never fallen off a roof."

"You fell off a roof," said Stark. "At the Muslin. Wakana had to catch you."

"That was a *strategic descent*."

Mau, kneeling on the cold shingles with a nail in his mouth, laughed so hard he nearly swallowed it.

And from the long room underneath, through the roof, very faintly, he heard Frieren laughing too.



She had all six grimoires spread out on the long table when they came down. She was sitting in the middle of them in the late light from the window, with her coat still on and a cup of Rem's tea going cold at her elbow, and she was turning the pages of the oldest one very slowly, the way you turn pages that you already know by heart.

She did not look up when they came in. But she said, to the book:

"I left these here the first winter. When I still thought of every place as somewhere I'd be leaving soon." She turned a page. "I put them on the top shelf because I didn't want to carry them. I thought, I'll get them before I go."

She turned another page.

"I never went," said Frieren.

Nobody said anything.

Fern sat down on the bench beside her. Stark sat beside Fern. Himmel, after a moment, sat on Frieren's other side, and looked at the grimoire in her hands, and Mau saw something in his face that he did not have a word for. Something old. Something from before any of them except Frieren had been born.

"You used to do this," said Himmel quietly. "At every inn. Spread them all out on the table. We'd come down for dinner and there'd be nowhere to put the plates."

"Heiter used to put his wine on them," said Frieren.

"He did."

"I used to make him pay for the stains."

"He never did."

"No," said Frieren. "He never did."

She closed the oldest grimoire.

Then she did something Mau had never seen her do. She moved along the bench, slightly, so that there was a space between her and Himmel, and she looked up at Mau, still standing in the doorway, and looked at the space.

He sat down in it.

They sat there, the five of them, on one bench, in the long room of the old inn, in the late light, with six grimoires on the table and Rem's tea going cold, and Lise in the kitchen doorway watching them with her hands knotted in her apron, as if she were looking at something she had heard about in a story.



They started back before dark.

Frieren had the six grimoires in a bag on her lap. She sat in the back of the cart among the empty shingle box and the ladder, with Mau beside her, and the horse plodded, and the light went blue over the snow.

Halfway home, Himmel, on the tail of the cart, stopped talking.

He had been talking the whole way: about other roofs, other inns, other quests, the time Eisen had carried the whole party's luggage across a river on his back without being asked, the time Heiter had blessed a village's well while drunk and it had given sweet water for a year. Then, at a bend in the road where the white fields opened out toward the dark line of the northern hills, he went quiet. He sat very still on the tail of the cart, looking at the hills.

Mau followed his eyes. He did not see anything. Only the hills, and the snow, and the long blue dusk.

"Himmel?" said Fern.

"Nothing," said Himmel. He smiled. "An old habit. You get used to watching the hills."

He did not look away from them until the bend was behind them. Then he started talking again, about a dragon that had turned out to be a very large lizard, and nobody asked.

Mau saw Frieren look at Himmel's back for a long moment in the dusk. Then she looked at the hills herself.

Then she leaned her head against Mau's shoulder.

"He and I used to sit like this," she said, very quietly, so that only he could hear. "In the back of carts. On the way home from things."

Mau went still.

"Do you want me to move?" he said.

Frieren was quiet for a while.

"No," she said. "I want to remember both."

The horse plodded. The settlement's lights came up out of the blue dusk ahead of them, small and warm and yellow, inside their line. And Frieren, who had once left six grimoires on a high shelf because every place was somewhere she would be leaving soon, held a bag of them in her lap, and her head on his shoulder, and did not let go of either.
