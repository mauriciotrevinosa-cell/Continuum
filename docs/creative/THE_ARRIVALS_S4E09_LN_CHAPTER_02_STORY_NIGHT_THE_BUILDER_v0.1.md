It was Marin's fault, the way most good things in the house were Marin's fault.

"The movie room," said Marin, at story night on the thirty-seventh, with her knees up on the bench and a blanket round her shoulders and her eyes very bright. "In the real house. When we build it. We're going to need movies."

Story night was older than the Muslin. At the first one, long ago, Mau had sat at the edge and had nothing to tell, because he had no before. By the long storm he had found that he did have stories after all: the deer, the north trail, the first month at the old inn. Now it happened whenever enough people were in the common room after dinner with nothing to do, which in winter was most nights. Somebody would start. Somebody else would interrupt. By the end half the house would be lying on the floor in front of the cold hearth in a heap, arguing about the ending.

"We don't have movies," said Fern.

"We have *people who remember movies*," said Marin. "Rimuru remembers about a thousand. Yuta remembers some. Okarun remembers a lot of very bad ones about aliens." She looked round the room. "We could write them down. Tell them. Act them out. Rimuru said he used to draw manga from memory for his dragon. Senku could figure out how to project something on a wall. And then when the movie room's built, we'll have a library ready to go."

Senku, on the floor by the hearth, opened one eye.

"Projection's easy," he said. "A lens, a light, something to put between them. Moving pictures are hard." He closed the eye. "Discs are very hard. Give me a year."

"I'll give you two," said Marin generously. "Rimuru. Tell us one."

Rimuru was sitting by the cold hearth in his place, with his back against the stones, half in slime shape the way he sat when he was comfortable. He looked at Marin. He looked round the room, at all of them, waiting.

"I don't want to tell you a movie," he said.

Marin's face fell.

"I'll tell you something else," said Rimuru.



"Before I was a slime," said Rimuru, "I built things."

The common room went quiet in the particular way it went quiet when Rimuru talked about before. He did not do it often. Everyone knew there had been a before, a world without magic, a life as a man that had ended and turned into this one. Very few of them had heard him say anything about it.

"I worked for a construction company," said Rimuru. "A general contractor. That means we didn't do one thing. We did all the things. Somebody wanted a building, and we made it happen: the ground, the steel, the concrete, the pipes, the people, the schedule." He smiled a little. "Mostly the schedule. I spent about fifteen years being yelled at about schedules."

"What did you build?" said Umaru, from the fort's curtain.

"Office buildings, mostly. Apartments. A shopping centre once. A bridge, a small one." He shrugged. "Nothing anyone would remember. I was thirty-seven. I had a good job and no girlfriend and an apartment I mostly slept in." He looked at the cold hearth. "It wasn't a bad life. It just wasn't anything."

"What happened?" said Kita, very quietly.

Rimuru was quiet for a moment.

"I was meeting a friend from work. A younger guy. He'd just got engaged, and he wanted me to meet his fiancée, because I was sort of his senior and he looked up to me, for some reason." He smiled again, not at anyone. "And there was a man in the street with a knife. He'd robbed a bank, or something, and he was running, and he ran at them. At the two of them. So I pushed them out of the way."

Nobody said anything.

"That's it," said Rimuru. "That's how it ended. In the street, on a cold day, bleeding out, worrying that I'd never cleared the browser history on my computer." There was a startled laugh from somewhere in the room, and Rimuru grinned. "And then I woke up in a cave, as a slime, and there was a dragon."

Mau, on the bench beside Frieren, had gone very still.

*He pushed them out of the way.* A man with a knife, two people who mattered, and someone who stepped in front. He looked at Rimuru by the cold hearth, smiling, and thought about a field, and a man with four arms, and a line he had stepped into because it was him or her.

Rimuru caught his eye across the room. Just for a second. He did not say anything. He did not need to.

*He knows,* said Ciel, very quietly, to one head only. *He has always known. That is why the roof.*



But it was not a sad story, the way Rimuru told it. That was what Mau remembered afterward. It was a story about building.

He told them about the first thing he had built in the new world: a village, for the goblins in the forest who had nothing, because a slime who had just been a construction manager could not look at a group of people sleeping in the rain without thinking about drainage. He told them about learning that you could not build a city by building houses. You built the ground first. You found the water. You decided where the roads would go before anyone lived there, so that when they came, the roads would already be in the right place. You put the drains in before the streets, because the streets would be there for a hundred years and nobody would ever want to dig them up again.

"Foundations," said Rimuru. "Everyone thinks building is walls. It's not. It's the stuff under the walls that nobody sees. If the ground's right, people can build anything on it, and it'll stand. If the ground's wrong, it doesn't matter how beautiful the walls are."

He told them about Tempest. How it had started as a village and become a town and then a city, and then, somehow, without his quite meaning it to, a nation. Roads. Hot springs. A hospital. A school. Ten thousand people, then a hundred thousand. Ambassadors. Trade.

"I didn't plan it," said Rimuru. "I just kept fixing the next problem. And then I looked up one day and it was a country."

The room was very quiet.

Wakana was sitting forward with his elbows on his knees, listening with his whole face. Senku had both eyes open now. Mikasa, who did not usually come to story night, was standing at the back by the window with her arms folded, and had been for some time.

"You do it in stages," said Rimuru. "That's the thing nobody understands. You don't need a thousand workers and ten years. You need the ground. And then you start. And then you keep upgrading. You build the thing you need now, and you leave room for the thing you'll need next, and then when the next thing comes, you build that. Especially when people are living there while you build. Especially then. You build around them. You build *with* them." He smiled. "Senku said *years* when Mikasa drew the tunnels. And he's right, the way he thinks about it. His world started from stone. It took years for everything." He looked at Senku. "But you don't have to dig the whole thing before anyone moves in. You dig what you need. Then you dig the next part."

Senku looked at him for a long moment.

"That's," said Senku slowly, "actually a really good point."

"I spent fifteen years being yelled at about schedules," said Rimuru. "I learned some things."



He stopped talking in the middle of a sentence.

Nobody noticed at first. He had been telling them about the drains under Tempest's main street, how he had made them three times wider than they needed to be, because he knew the city would grow. Then he stopped. His eyes went somewhere else, the way they went when Ciel was speaking, and stayed there.

Mau felt it too. A voice, to two heads. Very calm. Very precise. With something underneath it that Mau had only heard once before, on the fifth morning of looked-quiet, when she had called good news an emergency.

*A temporary skill has appeared,* said Ciel.

Rimuru did not move.

*It derives from what you have just been describing. Your memory of building. The knowledge of ground and water and stages. It has been latent since you came here. Since before the Trial. There was nowhere to put it. You have just given it somewhere.* A pause. *It is single-use. It is not large. It cannot build a city. It cannot build a house.*

*Then what—* said Rimuru, silently.

*It can prepare ground,* said Ciel. *Once. In the way you described. The things under the walls that nobody sees.* Another pause. *As it is, it is crude. If you give me a month, I can make it better. Not larger. Better. More precise. Worth the one use.*

Rimuru's eyes came back into the room.

The whole house was looking at him.

"Rimuru?" said Milim, from the floor by his feet.

"Ciel says she has something," said Rimuru slowly. "A skill. Something new. She needs a month to finish it."

"What kind of skill?"

Rimuru looked round the common room. At the long table, and the fort, and the cold hearth, and the board with the four rings, and the plan of the real house that Wakana had pinned to the wall beside it, with its dotted wings and its tunnels and its room with no name.

"The kind that goes under walls," he said.



Milim looked up at him from the floor, with her chin on her knees.

"Are we still doing the city thing?" she said.

The room went quiet again.

Everyone knew what she meant. The rumours had called it *Mau's city* for a year. The western papers had called it a settlement. The second community called it the main settlement. Anko's evidence wall called it, in a column of crossed-out words, everything from *refuge* to *monster town*. And Rimuru had built a city once. A nation. With roads and hot springs and ambassadors. It was what he knew how to do. Everyone had been half expecting it, Mau realized: that sooner or later Rimuru would roll up his sleeves and the settlement would become another Tempest.

Rimuru looked at Milim for a long moment.

Then he looked past her, round the common room. At Umaru in her sash at the fort's curtain. At Fern with the ledger on her knee. At Mau and Frieren on the bench, their hands together between them. At Bocchi with her guitar, and Ori with her stone, and Seiko in her armchair with her fan closed, and all the rest of them, lying on the floor in front of the cold hearth in a heap, the way they always ended up on story night.

"No," said Rimuru. "A home."

Nobody said anything.

Then Milim nodded, very seriously, as if this were the answer she had been hoping for and had not been sure she would get. She put her head back down on her knees.

"Good," she said. "Cities have ambassadors."

Rimuru laughed. The room laughed with him, and the quiet broke, and Marin said, "So that's a no on the movies?" and Rimuru said, "That's a not tonight on the movies," and Okarun immediately started telling everyone the plot of a very bad film about aliens, and was shouted down, and story night went on.

But later, after everyone had gone to bed, Mau came back for a glass of water and found Rimuru still sitting by the cold hearth in the dark, in slime shape, looking at Wakana's plan on the wall.

Mau sat down beside him.

They did not say anything for a while.

"A month," said Mau, at last.

"A month," said Rimuru.

*Thirty days,* said Ciel, to two heads. *I have already started.*
