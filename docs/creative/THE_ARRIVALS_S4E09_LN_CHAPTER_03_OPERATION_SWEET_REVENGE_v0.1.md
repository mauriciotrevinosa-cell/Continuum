## Chapter 22 — Operation Sweet Revenge

On the evening of the thirty-eighth day, after dinner, Maomao came out of the kitchen carrying a plate.

It was a small plate. On it were four chocolates. Small, dark, square, each one dusted very lightly with something pale, each one exactly the same. She carried it down the length of the long table without hurrying, past the fort and the bread and Senku's QUIET tally, and set it down on the table between Frieren and Fern, who were sitting side by side, with Mau on Frieren's other side and Stark on Fern's.

"Dessert," said Maomao. "Hand-cream leftovers. There was some cocoa over."

Frieren looked at the plate.

Fern looked at the plate.

Then, at exactly the same moment, they looked at each other.

"Thank you," said Fern carefully.

"Don't mention it," said Maomao, and went back to her seat at the far end of the table, and picked up her cup, and drank her tea, with an expression of perfect, serene, terrifying innocence.



*Three weeks earlier.*

The notebook was small and plain and very full, and the page that said *Revenge* had been underlined twice.

Maomao had started it on the evening of the day Frieren and Fern came back from their walk, looked her in the eye across the long table, and said, together, that their evening had been *Maomao's chocolate*. There had been no chocolate. There had not been chocolate in the house for weeks. Maomao had looked at the two of them, very pink, very solemn, very obviously lying, and she had understood exactly what had happened on that walk, and she had said, "I see." Then she had gone to her room and opened her notebook and written *Debt (2): F. and F. The lie.*

*Debt (1)* was older. Debt (1) was the long storm. Maomao had made a batch of her chocolate for the house at half strength, carefully measured, so that its effect would last a single evening. It had lasted a week. She had found out why only afterward: Frieren had gone into the medical store in the night and raised the dose. Without asking. Without telling her. And then she had sat through the whole week watching Mau follow her around like a golden retriever, with an expression of quiet, enormous satisfaction.

Maomao had not said anything at the time. Maomao had been a poison taster in a palace where people killed each other over hairpins. She had learned there that you did not take revenge at the moment of the offence. You took it at the moment of your choosing, when the offender had forgotten there was anything to take revenge for.

Under *Debt (1)* and *Debt (2)* she had written one line, and underlined it.

*Next time, the chocolate will also have an aphrodisiac.*



*Two weeks earlier.*

She had needed cocoa.

There had been none in the settlement since the roads went bad. Then Jinshi, who had nothing to do, had seen the notebook, and asked, and been told, and offered, as a matter of administration, the southern merchant's three stale sacks.

She had not asked him to keep it secret. She had not needed to. Jinshi had grown up in a palace too. He brought the sacks to the medical room door himself, on the back of the merchant's cart, at a time of day when almost nobody was in the yard. Then he went back to Arrival House and organized the empty intake table for the fourth time, and said nothing to anyone.

*Assistant: J. (temporary)*, said the notebook.



*Ten days earlier.*

She had needed a cover.

Hand cream had been the cover. It was a real need. The washing court's hands were cracking, the clinic was full of split knuckles, and cocoa butter really was the best thing for it. That was what made it a good cover: it was true. She had made forty pots of hand cream with Senku and Kusuri in the medical room in an afternoon, and the whole settlement had smelled of chocolate for a day, and everyone in the house had assumed that the smell was the hand cream.

Senku had known. She had seen him know, at the press, looking at the two and a half sacks against the wall. He had looked at the variable, and then, deliberately, he had decided not to account for it. *Ten billion percent hand cream*, he had said, and gone back to scrubbing.

Maomao had not thanked him. She had made a note.

*Co-conspirator: S. (voluntary).*

Kusuri had known too. Kusuri had known from the first sack. She had put her head down on the bench and said, *I'm going to have to watch this happen, aren't I*, and Maomao had said yes.

*Witness: K. (reluctant).*



*A week earlier.*

She had needed a recipe.

The recipe was old. She had first made it in another world, in a palace, for reasons she did not discuss, and she had made it again in this one, at the old inn, in the first year, when it had loosened the tongues of half the house for one long evening. It did not make anyone do anything. That was the rule. Maomao had stated the rule at the time, and she stated it again now, in her notebook, in her neatest hand, because it mattered:

*It does not make you want what you do not already want. It only takes away the reason you were not saying it.*

She made a single batch. Four pieces. She measured every grain of the active ingredient herself, twice, on the little brass scale she had brought from the palace, and set the dose at exactly half the strength of the storm batch. One evening. No more. Then she dusted each piece very lightly with powdered sugar, so that they looked like any chocolate from any shop in any world, and put them in a tin, and put the tin on the top shelf of the medical store, between the aconite and the thing that stopped your heart, where nobody, not even a child with a bean, would ever think to look.

She did not add an aphrodisiac.

She had never intended to.

She wrote that down too.



*Three days earlier.*

She had needed a seating plan.

This was the hardest part. The long table had no assigned seats. People sat where they sat. Frieren always sat next to Mau, and Fern often sat next to Frieren, and Stark always sat next to Fern, but *often* was not *always*, and Maomao did not leave things to *often*.

She went to the President.

Umaru was in the toll booth, doing the fort's accounts in crayon, with Rem beside her and Ori on the counter. Maomao stood at the booth's window and paid the toll, which was one snowball, and then said, without preamble:

"I need four people sitting together at dinner on the thirty-eighth. In a particular order."

Umaru put down her crayon.

"Which four?"

Maomao told her.

There was a long silence in the toll booth.

Then Umaru said, very slowly, with dawning delight, "Is this about *the chocolate?*"

Maomao did not answer.

Rem put her hand over her mouth.

Ori said, "Oh, no," with great interest.

"The fort," said Umaru, sitting up very straight in her sash, "does not take sides in personal matters." She paused. "The fort will, however, be redecorating the long table on the thirty-eighth. For reasons of national importance. With name cards." She picked up the crayon again. "Would you like the cards in crayon, or in ink?"

Maomao looked at her for a long moment.

"Crayon," she said. "Ink looks like planning."

*Accomplice: the Ministry*, said the notebook.



*The evening of the thirty-eighth.*

The long table had name cards on it. In crayon. Every seat. The fort had declared a festival of seating, for reasons of national importance, and nobody had questioned it, because the fort declared things all the time. Mau had found his card next to Frieren's, as always, and sat down without thinking about it. Fern had found hers between Frieren and Stark, and sat down. Neither of them had noticed that the cards were in a slightly different hand from the rest, or that the President had personally escorted each of them to their seat.

Now there were four chocolates on a plate between Frieren and Fern.

Maomao drank her tea at the far end of the table.

She watched Frieren look at the plate. She watched Fern look at the plate. She watched them look at each other. Two people who had once lied to her face about a chocolate that did not exist, looking at four that did.

It was very quiet at that end of the table.

"It's hand cream," said Stark helpfully. "Leftovers. Maomao said."

"Yes," said Frieren.

"Yes," said Fern.

Neither of them moved.

Then Mau, who had not been there for Debt (2), and had been on the receiving end of Debt (1) without ever knowing it was a debt, and had no idea why the two of them had gone so still, said, "They look good," and took one, and ate it.

Frieren turned her head and looked at him.

"Mau."

"What? It's good. Try one."

There was a long pause.

Then, because it would be strange not to, and because Mau had already eaten one and nothing had happened, and because she was Frieren, and Frieren had never once in a thousand years been able to resist something sweet put in front of her, Frieren took one too.

Fern watched her eat it.

Then Fern looked at Stark. Stark had already taken his, and was chewing it with an expression of uncomplicated happiness, because it was chocolate and Stark liked chocolate.

Fern took the last one.

At the far end of the table, Maomao put her cup down, opened her notebook under the edge of the table where nobody could see it, and wrote one line under the heading *Revenge*:

*38th. 20:14. All four.*

Beside her, Kusuri put her face in her hands.
