# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 23 — Subjects

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 23

Nothing happened for eleven minutes.

Maomao knew it would be eleven minutes, because she had made the recipe before, and she had written the onset time in her notebook in another world and again in this one. She drank her tea and watched the four of them at the other end of the long table and waited.

At first they were careful. Frieren and Fern sat very upright, the way people sit when they are listening to their own bodies for symptoms. Fern kept glancing at Frieren. Frieren kept glancing at the empty plate. Stark, who did not know there was anything to be careful about, was talking cheerfully to Mau about Senku's catapult, and Mau was laughing, and nobody else at the table was paying the slightest attention, because the fort was arguing about whether the Milim Wall counted as a permanent structure under Article Two.

At nine minutes, Frieren relaxed a little.

At ten, Fern did.

At eleven, Stark said, in the middle of a sentence about the catapult, "You moved your bed."

He said it to Fern. He said it in exactly the same cheerful, ordinary voice he had been using for the catapult. And then he stopped, with his mouth slightly open, as if he had heard himself say it from a long way off and did not recognize the voice.

Fern went absolutely still.

"What," she said.

"You moved your bed," said Stark. He sounded faintly surprised, the way you sound when a door you have been pushing on for weeks swings open. "Across the line. A handspan. On the twenty-fourth. At about three in the morning." He blinked. "And then you redrew the line in the morning so I wouldn't notice."

Fern's face had gone a colour Mau had never seen on a human being.

"You were *asleep*."

"I wasn't asleep," said Stark. "I'm never asleep when you are. I just lie very still so you don't feel like you have to talk." He looked at her with a kind of dazed tenderness. "I didn't say anything. I thought if I said anything you'd move it back."

At the far end of the table, Maomao wrote something in her notebook.

Fern opened her mouth. Nothing came out. She closed it. She opened it again.

"I moved it," she said, in a small voice that was nothing like her ledger voice, "because it was too quiet. On my side. And you were right there. And I could hear you breathing, and I wanted to be—" She stopped. Her eyes had gone wide, the way they went when she realized she had made an error in the ledger. "I wanted to be closer to the breathing," said Fern, very fast, as if it were coming out of her by itself, "that's all, it's not complicated, it was a *handspan*—"

"I know," said Stark.

"*Why are we saying this,*" said Fern.

"I don't know," said Stark, "but I don't want to stop."



Frieren had been watching this with an expression of slowly dawning horror.

She turned to look at the empty plate.

Then she turned to look down the long table at Maomao, who was drinking her tea.

Maomao raised her cup very slightly, in a small salute.

"Oh," said Frieren. "Oh, *no*."

"What?" said Mau, beside her.

Frieren turned to him. She had the face of a woman who had just understood that a trap she had set a long time ago had been reset, very patiently, and had closed.

"The chocolate," she said. "It's her chocolate. Her *real* chocolate. The one from the inn. The one from the storm."

Mau looked at the empty plate.

Then he looked inside himself, carefully, the way he had learned to look in the autumn, checking for anything that was not his.

He did not feel strange. That was the thing. He did not feel drugged, or dizzy, or different. He felt exactly like himself. Only there was something gone. A small, familiar weight that he carried around all day without noticing, the weight of all the things he did not say because there was a reason not to say them. It had been sitting on his chest for so long that he had stopped knowing it was there. And now it was gone, and his chest felt very light.

He looked at Frieren beside him, in the lamplight, with her face doing something horrified and furious and pink.

"You raised the dose," he said. "In the storm. That was you."

Frieren froze.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody. I just know." He was smiling. He could not stop. "That's why it lasted a week. Maomao made it for one night and it lasted a week, and you were so pleased the whole time. You had that face. That one. The one you have right now."

"I do not have a face."

"You have a face."

"*Mau.*"

"You followed me around for a week watching me follow you around," said Mau, "and you looked so *happy*, and I didn't know why." The words were coming out of him easily, lightly, the way water comes out of a spring. "I thought it was because of me. It was because of me. But it was also because you'd *cheated*."

Frieren put her hands over her face.

At the far end of the table, Maomao wrote something in her notebook. Kusuri, beside her, had slid down her chair until only her eyes showed over the edge of the table.



It went on for an hour.

Nobody else at the table seemed to notice at first. The fort had moved on from the Milim Wall to a dispute about the toll on snowballs. The five from the big room were discussing the second kotatsu. Senku was explaining something to Suika with a spoon. The four at the end of the long table were simply talking, rather a lot, rather quickly, in voices that kept going up at the ends of sentences.

Stark told Fern that he liked it when she scolded him, because he had worked out a long time ago that she only scolded people she had decided to keep. Fern told Stark that she wore the silver-wire bracelet he had made her for her birthday every single day, under her sleeve, even at the washing court, even in the lye, and that she checked it was still there about forty times a day, and that she had never told him, because if she told him he would make that face. Stark made that face. Fern said, "*That* face," and then put her hand over her own mouth as if to stop it, too late.

Frieren took her hands away from her face and looked at Mau for a long moment.

"I don't want to wait a hundred years," she said.

Mau went still.

"For the tree," said Frieren. Her voice was very quiet, and very clear, and nothing like the way she usually talked about time. "Everyone thinks I don't mind waiting. Because I'm an elf. Because I've waited a thousand years for things and I'll wait a thousand more. They think a hundred years is nothing to me." She looked at her hands on the table. "It's not nothing. I want to sit under it *now*. With you. While—" She stopped. "I'm tired of waiting for things," said Frieren. "I've been waiting for things my whole life."

He did not know what to say. So he said what was true, because there was nothing in the way of saying it.

"Me too," he said.

Frieren looked at him.

"I'm older than you now," said Mau. "Ciel said. A hundred years older. At least. I've waited longer than you have, for some things."

There was a pause.

"Say that again," said Frieren, "and I'll Zoltraak you."

"I'm older than you."

"*Mau.*"

"I'm *older* than—"

She kissed him, mostly to make him stop, at the long table in front of the entire house. The fort stopped arguing about the snowball toll. The five from the big room stopped talking about the kotatsu. Senku's spoon stopped in mid-air. Somewhere near the cold hearth, Rimuru made a sound like a kettle.

Frieren let him go. She looked round the suddenly silent common room. Then she looked at the empty plate, and then, very slowly, the length of the long table, at Maomao.

Maomao put down her cup.

She stood up. She did not hurry. She smoothed her apron. She looked down the long table at the four of them at the end: Fern with her hand over her mouth, Stark scarlet, Frieren very pink, and Mau beside her, smiling, entirely unrepentant.

"I should mention," said Maomao, in her clear, flat, carrying clinic voice, so that the whole common room could hear, "that batch also contained an aphrodisiac."

The silence became total.

Four people at the end of the long table went rigid at exactly the same moment.

Then, very slowly, with enormous care, the way you move away from a sleeping animal, Fern moved her chair four inches further from Stark.

Stark moved his four inches further from Fern.

Frieren took her hand off Mau's arm as if it had burned her.

Mau looked down at himself, alarmed, checking.

"I don't—" he said. "I don't feel—"

"It takes longer," said Maomao serenely. "Onset is variable. Some people notice nothing at all. Some people notice—" she paused, delicately "—later."

She sat back down and picked up her tea.

At the end of the long table, the four of them sat in absolute silence, very straight, very far apart, not looking at each other, and not looking at anyone else, and listening, with horrified attention, to their own heartbeats.

Kusuri, beside Maomao, had disappeared under the table entirely.



They went to bed early.

All four of them, at the same moment, as if by agreement, without saying goodnight to anyone. Fern and Stark went down the main corridor to the room with the chalk line, walking very carefully a full arm's length apart, and the cloth wall closed behind them, and from inside, after a moment, everyone in the common room heard Fern's voice say very clearly, "*Stay on your side of the line,*" and Stark's voice say, "I *am* on my side of the line," and then a long silence.

Frieren went down the family corridor toward the room with the east window, and stopped at the door, and turned, and looked at Mau, who was following her at a careful distance.

"I'm monitoring you," she said. "For symptoms. That's all."

"That's all," Mau agreed.

"Medically."

"Medically."

The door closed.

At the long table, the whole house sat in silence for a long moment.

Then Umaru, in her sash at the north end, said, with deep satisfaction, "The fort declares the festival of seating a success," and the common room exploded.

Maomao sat through all of it with her cup in her hands and her face perfectly serene. She did not laugh. But when she opened her notebook to write the last line of the evening, her hand was shaking very slightly, and Kusuri, emerging from under the table, saw that it was shaking because she was trying not to.

*38th. 21:30. Aphrodisiac announced. Subjects in bed.*

She looked at the line for a moment.

Then, underneath, in very small letters:

*Tomorrow, the truth.*
