# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 24 — Exactly As They Already Wanted

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 24  
**Continuity state:** Day 39 | four people who didn't sleep | Maomao's correction: no aphrodisiac, ever; 'I don't need to' | 'Subjects behaved exactly as they already wanted to' | debts settled | Fern moves her chair four inches toward him | Frieren and Maomao shake hands: 'Until next time' | AGENT HANDSPAN rejected; still pending

They came to breakfast separately, all four of them, and all four of them looked as though they had not slept.

Fern came first, with the ledger under her arm and dark circles under her eyes, and sat down at the head of the table very straight, and opened the ledger, and did not look at anyone. Stark came a minute later and sat at the far end of the bench from her, nowhere near his usual place, and looked at his porridge with the expression of a man who had spent the entire night lying perfectly still on his side of a chalk line, listening to his own heartbeat and wondering whether it was going faster than usual.

Frieren came in with her hair not brushed, which never happened, and sat down next to Mau, and then moved six inches away from him on the bench, and then, after a moment, moved three inches back.

Mau came in last, and sat down, and was smiling.

He had slept. He had slept very well. He had lain down beside Frieren in the room with the east window, at a careful medical distance, and she had lain rigid on her side of the bed all night, monitoring him for symptoms with an intensity he could feel through the blanket. He had felt absolutely nothing at all except that he loved her, and that she was ridiculous, and both of those had been true before the chocolate. At some point he had started laughing quietly into the pillow, and she had said, *What, what is it, is it starting*, and he had said, *No*, and laughed harder, and she had hit him with her pillow, and then he had fallen asleep.

At the cold-hearth end of the table, Rimuru cleared his throat.

"She says," said Rimuru, "*looked, quiet.*"

Senku's chalk squeaked on the second word.

Nobody ate.



Fern stood up to read the ledger.

She read it in her clear, even voice, the voice she used for barrels, very fast, as if trying to get through it before anyone could say anything. Day thirty-nine. Arrival House opened at the eighth bell. Intake, none. Guests, two. Departures, none. Arrivals—

"Correction for the ledger," said Maomao, from the far end of the table.

Fern stopped.

The whole common room went quiet.

Maomao did not stand up. She sat in her place with her cup of tea and her notebook open beside it, and looked down the length of the long table at the four of them, with an expression of perfect calm.

"Last night," said Maomao, "I informed the house that a batch of chocolate served at dinner contained an aphrodisiac."

Fern's knuckles were white on the ledger.

"That was incorrect," said Maomao.

There was a pause.

"It contained my usual preparation," said Maomao, "at half the strength of the storm batch. One evening. Which has now worn off." She took a sip of tea. "It did not contain an aphrodisiac. It never contained an aphrodisiac. I have never in my life put an aphrodisiac in anything." She set her cup down. "I don't need to."

The silence was absolute.

Then Maomao opened her notebook, turned it round, and read aloud from it, in her flat clinic voice, so that every person in the common room could hear.

"*Observation, thirty-eighth, twenty-one thirty. Subjects informed of aphrodisiac. Subjects immediately separated. Subjects spent the night in a state of acute self-observation. No pharmacological cause.*" She turned a page. "*Conclusion. Subjects behaved exactly as they already wanted to. The only effect of the announcement was that, for one night, they were afraid it was the chocolate.*"

She closed the notebook.

"Debts settled," said Maomao. "Both."



It took the common room about four seconds.

Then Hakari fell off the bench.

Then Karane laughed so hard she had to hold onto Nano, and Nano, flat-faced, said "*Statistically devastating*," and Shizuka held up her book, on which she had already written in very large letters *SHE NEVER NEEDED TO*, and the fort came apart completely. Umaru was crying into her sash. Milim was on the floor. Seiko had her fan over her face and her shoulders were shaking. Senku had put his head down on the table and was making a long, high, wheezing sound, and Suika was patting his back. Down by the cold hearth, Rimuru had melted most of the way into slime shape out of sheer helplessness.

At the head of the table, Fern stood holding the ledger with an expression Mau would remember for the rest of his life.

She had spent the night on her side of a chalk line, awake, terrified of her own heartbeat. She had told Stark, in front of the entire house, that she checked his bracelet forty times a day. She had moved her chair four inches away from him in a panic, because she had believed for twelve hours that every warm thing she felt about him might be a drug.

And it had all been her.

Every bit of it. All along.

Stark, at the far end of the bench, was looking at her. His face had gone through relief and disappointment and something else, very quickly, and arrived somewhere Mau could not quite read. Then he got up, walked the length of the bench, and sat down in his usual place beside her, exactly where he always sat.

He did not say anything.

Fern looked down at him.

Then she sat down too, with the ledger, beside him, and moved her chair four inches *toward* him, and finished reading the day's entries in her clear, even, barrel-counting voice, as if nothing whatsoever had happened, with her ears scarlet to the tips.

"Arrivals," said Fern, "zero."

Nobody had ever heard the word said so firmly.



Frieren had not moved.

She was sitting on the bench beside Mau, three inches away, with her unbrushed hair, looking down the long table at Maomao. Her face was doing something very complicated.

Mau leaned over.

"Are you angry?" he said.

Frieren did not take her eyes off Maomao.

"I raised her dose," she said, slowly. "In the storm. I went into her medical store in the middle of the night and I raised it, without asking, because I wanted a week of you instead of an evening." She was speaking very quietly. "And then I lied to her face about a chocolate that didn't exist. With Fern."

"I know," said Mau. "You told me. Last night. Sort of."

"And she waited," said Frieren. "All this time. She waited until I'd forgotten. And then she got Jinshi to get the cocoa, and Senku to look the other way, and the *Ministry* to do the seating." She paused. "And she didn't even do anything to us. She just *said* something. And we did the rest ourselves."

Mau waited.

"That," said Frieren, "was very, very well done."

She stood up. She walked the length of the long table, past the collapsing fort and the wheezing Senku and the melted Rimuru, to the far end, where Maomao sat with her tea. She stopped in front of her.

The common room went quiet again, because nobody wanted to miss this.

Maomao looked up at her, serene.

"That was well done," said Frieren.

"I learned from someone," said Maomao, "who raised a dose."

They looked at each other for a long moment, an elf who was over a thousand years old and a pharmacist from a palace, across a cup of tea.

Then Frieren held out her hand.

Maomao looked at it. Then she shook it, once, briefly, the way you shake hands at the end of a long and honourable war.

"Never again," said Frieren.

"Until next time," said Maomao.

Frieren considered this.

"Until next time," she agreed, and went back to her seat.



That evening, the fort held an emergency codename session, behind the curtain, at Umaru's insistence.

Mau did not hear all of it. He heard enough.

"Item one," said Umaru's voice. "The matter of Fern's codename. In light of new evidence."

"No," said Fern's voice.

"Proposal," said Hakari. "AGENT HANDSPAN."

"*No.*"

"Proposal," said Karane, gleefully. "AGENT FORTY TIMES A DAY."

"*Absolutely not.*"

"Proposal," said Shizuka's book, which Coco read aloud: "*AGENT IT WAS ALL HER.*"

There was a sound like someone putting a ledger down very hard on a toll booth counter.

"The card," said Fern, "stays *pending*."

And it did.

But late that night, passing the kitchen, Mau saw a light under the door again. Fern was at the kitchen table, alone, in her nightgown, with a candle and a scrap of paper. This time she was not writing anything. She was holding the scrap and looking at it, and under her sleeve, at her wrist, something silver caught the candlelight.

She was smiling.

He went to bed without a sound.
