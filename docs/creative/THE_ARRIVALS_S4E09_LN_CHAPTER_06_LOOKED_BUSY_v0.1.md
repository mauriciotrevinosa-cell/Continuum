# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 21
## Chapter 25 — Looked. Busy.

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 21  
**Reading order:** 25  
**Continuity state:** Day 39 | Ciel adds 'busy' and won't explain | the house speculates; Rimuru keeps her secret | Frieren asks Ciel directly: not about him, 'for once' | Fern: 'Tomorrow is Day Forty' | at the hour: 'It is going well.'

On the morning of the thirty-ninth day, after the chocolate and the reckoning, Rimuru cleared his throat at the cold-hearth end of the table, and everyone turned.

"She says," said Rimuru, "*looked, quiet.*"

Senku's chalk squeaked.

"And she says," said Rimuru, and paused, and his face went slightly odd, "*busy.*"

There was a silence at the long table.

"Busy," said Fern.

"Busy," said Rimuru.

"Busy *how?*"

Rimuru listened.

"She says she's busy," he said. "That's all. She doesn't want to say with what."

Twenty spoons hovered over twenty bowls.

"Is it bad busy?" said Milim.

Rimuru listened again.

"She says there is no such thing as bad busy," he reported, "only busy."

"That's not an answer," said Milim.

"She says she is aware."



Nobody had ever heard Ciel refuse to explain something.

Ciel explained everything. That was what Ciel was. She had explained the seal and the guardrails and the hundred and forty-three patterns, and the Noise, and the leaks, and the shape of every crisis the house had been through since the autumn of the containment. She had explained them precisely, at length, in a voice that two people could hear, while those two people relayed it to everyone else. She had counted four hundred and thirty-seven deaths without knowing what she was counting, and when she found out, she had explained that too. Ciel did not keep things. It was one of the few things the whole house was sure of.

And now she was busy, and would not say with what.

By lunch, it had become the only topic of conversation in the Muslin.

"She's making something," said Senku, at the workshop door, with real scientific frustration. "Busy means work. Work means output. *What's* the output?"

"Maybe she's tired," said Bocchi, very quietly, from the music room doorway. "Maybe busy is what you say when you need people to leave you alone for a while." She went pink. "That's what I'd say."

"She's planning a surprise," said Umaru, with total certainty, from the toll booth. "Busy is a surprise word. Everyone knows that." She narrowed her eyes. "The Ministry should have been informed."

"Maybe she's sick," said Milim, who had been worrying about it since breakfast. "Can she get sick? Rimuru, can she get sick?"

"She can't get sick," said Rimuru, for the fourth time.

"How do you *know?*"

Rimuru opened his mouth. Then he closed it again. Because he did know, of course. He had known since story night. Ciel had told him, in the dark, in front of Wakana's plan, with Mau beside him: *thirty days, I have already started.* And then, the next morning, very quietly, only to him: *Do not tell them yet. I want it to be finished first. I want it to be good.*

So he did not tell them. He sat by the cold hearth and let the house speculate around him, and every time somebody asked, he said, "She's busy," and looked at the ceiling, which was a trick he had learned from Diablo.

Diablo, behind his chair with the teapot, looked at the ceiling too, in perfect solidarity.



Frieren asked directly.

She did it in the afternoon, in the room with the east window, sitting on the bed with her grimoire on her knees, while Mau mended his coat by the window.

"Ciel," said Frieren. To the air. To the room. Not to Mau or to Rimuru. To Ciel herself, as if Ciel were a person in the room who could hear her, which she was.

Mau looked up.

*She is speaking to me,* said Ciel, to one head. She sounded faintly startled.

"I know you can hear me," said Frieren. "You can hear everything he hears." She turned a page, not looking at anything. "What are you busy with?"

Ciel was quiet for a moment.

*Tell her,* said Ciel, *that I am busy.*

"She says she's busy," said Mau.

Frieren turned another page.

"Is it about him?"

Mau went still. He had not thought of that. He felt the old cold start in his chest. Something inside him. Something she had seen, in the seal, in the morning look, something that was going wrong, and she did not want to say—

*No,* said Ciel at once, and very firmly, the way you put a hand flat on a table. *Tell her no. Tell her it is not about him.* A pause, and then, with a strange note in it that might have been amusement: *Tell her that for once, it is not about him at all.*

Mau let his breath out.

"She says no," he said. "She says it's not about me." He paused. "She says, *for once.*"

Frieren looked up from her grimoire for the first time.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then a small, crooked smile came at one corner of her mouth.

"Good," she said, and went back to her book.



At dinner, Fern read the ledger. Day thirty-nine. Arrival House opened at the eighth bell. Intake, none. Arrivals, zero.

Then she looked up.

"Tomorrow is Day Forty," said Fern.

The long table went quiet.

Nobody had forgotten. Fern had written it in the ledger three weeks ago, on the nineteenth, after Hakari asked: *Coins: held. Until Day 40. Then the table.* It had been sitting there ever since, on its page, while the house had ugly days and snow and kotatsu and catapults and chocolate. It had been sitting there like a stone in a pocket. Everyone had felt it.

"After breakfast," said Fern. "All of us. Here."

She closed the ledger.

Nobody said anything. People looked at their bowls. Hakari's hand had gone to her pocket. Maki's had gone to hers. Across the table, Umaru took her coin out of her sash, where it had been since the morning, the nineteenth place she had put it, and looked at it, and put it back.

Mau put his hand in his coat pocket and felt his own coin there, small and warm.

*Everyone I love is already here.*

He thought about the table tomorrow. Thirty people, and more, each holding a small pale coin that could bring one person. Forty, fifty names, maybe. And the settlement with its one Arrival House and its empty intake table and its line in the snow that did not cover the road.

He did not think it was going to be simple.



That night, at the hour, before Frieren's foot found his shin, a voice came, quietly, to two heads.

*Looked,* said Ciel. *Quiet.*

And then, after a pause:

*Busy.*

And then, after a longer pause, very softly, so that Mau almost thought he had imagined it:

*It is going well.*

He lay in the dark and smiled at the ceiling.

Then Frieren's foot found his shin, cold as a stone, and he did not have to wait for the room to change, because he already knew it was not going to.
