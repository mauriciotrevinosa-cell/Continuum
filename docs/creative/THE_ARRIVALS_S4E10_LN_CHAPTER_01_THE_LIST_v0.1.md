Nijika: her sister

On the morning of the fortieth day, after breakfast, nobody got up from the long table.

Rimuru had said *looked, quiet, busy*. Senku's chalk had squeaked. The bowls had been cleared. And then, instead of the usual scraping of benches and scattering of people into the day, the whole house simply stayed where it was, and the room filled up around the table. The fort came out from behind its curtain and sat in a cluster at the north end. Rings One and Two, all of them, the way they had been at the council and on the night of rule five. Coins in pockets and sashes and apron pockets and sword cases. Fern at the head of the table with the ledger open to the page that said *Day 40*.

She did not read the day's entries. She turned back three weeks, to the nineteenth, and read the line aloud instead.

"*Coins: held. Until Day 40. Then the table.*"

She looked up.

"It's Day Forty," said Fern. "This is the table."



She had an agenda. Of course she did. It was written on a sheet of paper laid flat beside the ledger, in her small square hand, and it had three items.

"One," said Fern. "What we can hold. Two. Who we bring first. Three." She paused. "Everything else."

Nobody laughed. It was not a joke.

"What we can hold," said Fern, and turned the page round so that the table could see the numbers.

She had done the work. She always did the work. Arrival House: two terms rooms, one intake table, beds for eleven guests at most, and staff who had been idle for a month. The Muslin: every room spoken for, some twice, the fort already at thirty with six cushions in the annex. The real house: drawn, not built. The stores: enough flour and dried food for the winter for the people who were here now, and not much more. The roads: three weeks without a merchant from the west. The southern merchant still came, slowly, but he said the river towns were afraid. And the line.

"Anko," said Fern.

Anko stood up with her notebook.

"Night of the thirty-second," said Anko. "Tracks in the north field. Something big, from the hills. It walked straight at us and turned forty paces from the last cottage. It walked into the Goddess's protection." She turned a page. "The protection holds. But it covers the settlement. It does not cover the road. Not to the bridge. Not to the river. Not to the old inn." She closed the notebook. "Every person we bring here comes in by the road. And every one of them will be ours to feed."

She sat down.

There was a long silence at the long table.

"So," said Fern. "We can't bring everyone."

She looked down the table.

"I counted," she said. "This morning. Everyone in this room who has a coin." A pause. "If every one of us spent it today, we'd have more than forty new people in the settlement by tomorrow night. Strangers to everyone but one person each. With no beds, no rooms, no winter stores, coming in down a road that something from the hills is walking across." She closed the agenda. "We can't hold that. Not this winter. Not at once."

Nobody argued.

"So we go one at a time," said Rimuru, from the cold hearth. "Slowly. Each person gets time to arrive. Guest, then a room, then a place. Like everyone here did." He looked round the table. "Like Mau's first month at the old inn."

"One at a time," Fern agreed, and wrote it. "Then item two. Who first."



It went round the table.

Nobody planned it that way. Fern did not call on anyone. It simply happened, the way it had happened on the night of rule five, one person and then the next, each saying the thing they had been carrying.

Yuta went first. He had his sword case on the bench beside him. He did not open it.

"There's someone," he said. "From my world. My teacher." He looked at the table, not at anyone. "He was the strongest person who ever lived, where I'm from. He stood in front of everyone, alone, every time, and nobody ever stopped him, because nobody could. And it killed him." He paused. "I want to bring him here. Where people stop people."

Nobody said anything.

"He'd be useful," said Yuta, and almost smiled. "That's the strategic argument. He'd be the best teacher this house ever had. Mau has a sealed Infinity that leaks when he's grieving, and the only person in any world who knows how to control Infinity is him." He turned to look at Mau, finally. "But that's not why."

"I know," said Mau.

"And there's a doctor," said Yuta, to the table. "His friend. From school. A real one. She can heal other people, not just herself. That almost nobody can do." He looked down the table at Maomao. "You've been doing the whole settlement alone, with Kusuri. Through Last Defense. Through all of it."

Maomao did not say anything. But she did not say *no*, either.



Umaru had been listening at the north end, in her sash, with her coin in her hand. It was the twentieth place she had put it.

She stood up.

"The doctor," she said. "Mine. For the doctor."

The table went still.

"I'll give it," said Umaru, very fast, with her chin up and her eyes very bright. "To Yuta, or whoever, for the doctor. So Maomao isn't alone. It's a good use. It's the best use. It's strategic." She held the coin out, across the table, toward nobody in particular. "Take it. The President is authorized to release it in a crisis."

Nobody moved.

Then, from the far end of the table, from the armchair, there was a sharp *thwack*.

Seiko had rolled up a sheet of paper from Fern's agenda. Mau did not see where she had got it from. She had leaned out of her armchair, and reached the length of the bench, and brought the rolled paper down smartly on the top of Umaru's hooded head, once, the way you would swat a fly.

Umaru froze, with the coin still held out.

"You did well," said Seiko. "Now calm down."

Umaru stared at her.

"Put that away," said Seiko. "You've moved it twenty times this month. You think nobody's seen? You're not giving that away to the first good cause that walks past because you can't bear to decide what it's really for." She put the rolled paper across her knees. "Keep it. You'll know."

Umaru's chin had started to wobble.

"Then who—"

"Mine," said Seiko.

The table turned to look at her.

"When this teacher of yours comes," said Seiko, to Yuta, flatly, "he'll have mine. For his doctor. Transfers are allowed, the Goddess said so." She snapped her fan open. "I'll give it to him myself. On the day."

Umaru came round the end of the table in two steps and put her arms round Seiko in her armchair, and held on.

"You don't want to bring anyone?" said Umaru, muffled, into Seiko's shoulder.

Seiko looked down at the top of the hood.

Then she looked past it, along the length of the long table, to where Momo was sitting with her mouth slightly open.

"I already have my pain-in-the-neck granddaughter," said Seiko. "She was the only thing I cared about in that world." She looked away. "Anything else from there can stay there."

Momo stood up so fast her bench rocked.

She came down the table with her fist already clenched, the way she came at Seiko a dozen times a day, *pain-in-the-neck* still ringing in the air, for a smack on the arm, the way they always did. She got halfway. Then she heard the rest of it.

She stopped.

Then she walked the rest of the way, and put her arms round both of them, Seiko and Umaru together, and did not say anything at all.

Seiko sat in the middle of it with her fan open in front of her face and did not say anything either. But her free hand came up, after a moment, and rested on Momo's back.



It went on round the table.

Rem said her sister's name again, in front of everyone this time. "Ram." She did not say anything else. She did not need to.

Coco said she was not ready. Not because of the roads. Because of what she was reading. "I need to know first," she said. "Whether I can help her. When she comes. I don't want to bring her here as she is and then just *look* at her." Qifrey, beside her, did not say anything. He had her coin in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left side.

Maki took her hand out of her pocket, with the folded paper in it, and looked at it for a long moment, and put it back. "Written," she said. "Not yet." Yuta put his hand over hers on the bench.

Kaneki said nothing. When it came round to him he only shook his head, once. Nazuna, across the table, looked at her plate very hard. Anko did not look at anything.

Senku said, when it came to him, "Ten billion percent undecided," and looked at the ceiling. Suika, beside him, did not say anything, but her new glasses caught the light as she turned to look at him.

Mikasa said, quietly, "Armin." She did not say anything else. Beside her, Eren had gone very still.

"Armin," said Eren. "Yeah." He looked at the table. "He'd be good. He'd be better than me at all of this. The talking. The deciding." A pause. "He'd fix the roads in a month."

Fern wrote it down.

"Not yet," said Fern, gently. "Not this winter. Not with the road like it is." She looked at Mikasa and then at Eren. "It's on the list. It stays on the list."

Mikasa nodded. Eren did not say anything. But he did not stand up and go to the window, which was what Eren did when he had decided something was over.

It came round to Mau.

He put his hand in his coat pocket and felt the coin there.

"Mine doesn't have a name," he said. "Everyone I'd bring is already here."

"That's cheating," said Umaru, from inside Seiko's shoulder, wetly.

"I know," said Mau. "The cooper said that too."



And then it came round to Nijika.

She had been sitting beside Bocchi the whole time, very still, with her hand in her jacket pocket. Everyone had forgotten she was there, almost. Nijika had a way of being so steady that you forgot she was a person who might want something.

She took her hand out of her pocket. There was a coin in it, with a guitar pick and a hair tie.

"Me," said Nijika.

The table went quiet.

"First," said Nijika. "I'll go first." She was not looking at anyone. She was looking at the coin. "My sister. She ran a live house, in a basement, in another world. She raised me. When our mum—" She stopped. "She raised me. She'd hate it here. She'd hate the cold and the snow and the fort and all of you. She'd stand at the back with her arms folded and pretend she wasn't listening." She closed her hand round the coin. "I know about the road. I know about the line. I know it's the worst time. I know Fern says one at a time." She looked up, at last, down the long table. "I'm the one. First. I decided on the first night, on the bridge. I haven't said it because—"

She did not finish. She did not look at Bocchi.

Bocchi, beside her, had gone completely white.

Mau saw it happen. He saw her look at the coin in Nijika's hand, and then at her own guitar case, under the bench, with her own coin in the little compartment under the neck. Bocchi, who had a mother and a father and a little sister and a dog in another world, and had said that if she took one of them she did not know what she would be leaving the others. Bocchi, who had closed her case on the bridge with nothing in her hands.

Nobody said anything. Nobody knew what to say.

Then Kita, on Bocchi's other side, reached across Bocchi without a word and put her hand on top of Nijika's, on top of the closed fist with the coin in it.

And after a long moment, Bocchi did too.

She put her small cold hand on top of Kita's, on top of Nijika's. She did not say anything. She was shaking. But she did it.

Ryo, on the end of the bench, who could not be relied upon for anything, reached over and put her hand on top of the pile, and said, "She'll hate us," with deep satisfaction.

"She'll *love* you," said Nijika, and her voice broke completely. "She'll pretend she doesn't. For about a year."

Fern was writing.

She wrote it on a clean page of the ledger, under a new heading in capitals, which she had not used before. Then she turned the ledger round, so that the whole table could see.

*THE LIST.*

*1. Nijika: her sister. First.*

*2. When we're ready.*



They did not decide anything else that day. Fern would not let them. One at a time, she said. One, and then time, and then the next. The list could grow. But it would only ever grow one line at a time.

The table broke up in the early afternoon. People went back to the day. The fort went back behind its curtain. Seiko's armchair was carried back to its place, with Umaru still holding onto one arm of it.

Nijika sat on the bench with her coin in her hand for a long time after everyone had gone. Kessoku sat with her. Nobody played anything.

And Mau, crossing the empty common room with an armful of firewood, stopped at the board, where Fern had copied the list in chalk under the four rings and Senku's QUIET tally.

*THE LIST.*

*1. Nijika: her sister.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he went and put the firewood by the stove, and went out to the yard, where the ink tree stood in its ring of stakes with ten leaves, and did not know why his throat was tight.
