Umaru was in the toll booth on the evening of the forty-first, with the shutter down.

That was unusual. The toll booth's shutter was never down. The toll booth was the fort's front door and its customs house and its treasury and, on bad nights, its hospital. Rem served tea at its counter. Turbo Granny sat on its roof. The shutter had a sign painted on it, OPEN, in Umaru's own hand, with a hamster drawn beside it, and it had not been closed since the fort was a monument.

Tonight it was closed. A line of lamplight showed underneath it. From inside, every so often, there was a small sound, like someone sniffing very carefully so that nobody would hear.

Mau knocked on the shutter.

The sniffing stopped.

"The booth is closed," said Umaru's voice. "By order of the President."

"I know," said Mau. "I'm not a citizen. I can't come in anyway."

There was a pause.

"Non-citizens," said Umaru's voice, very carefully, "may sit outside the booth. If they want. On the step. It's not against the constitution."

Mau sat down on the step outside the toll booth, with his back against the closed shutter.



For a long time neither of them said anything.

The common room was dark around them. Everyone had gone to bed. The fort's curtain was tied back, and the cushions inside were empty, thirty-one of them plus six with NEXT in front. The Milim Beam stood in the dark. The hamster flag hung limp on its broom handle. On the board on the far wall, by the light of the one lamp that was always left burning over the scratches, Mau could just make out the chalk: *THE LIST. 1. Nijika: her sister.*

"I almost gave it away," said Umaru, through the shutter.

"I know. I was there."

"I almost gave it away because I couldn't—" She stopped. He heard the coin click against the counter. She was turning it over, he thought, the way Hakari did. "I've moved it twenty-one times," said Umaru. "Seiko was right. I've counted. Twenty-one. Under my pillow. On the windowsill. In the booth. In my sash. In my shoe, once, which was stupid. I keep moving it because if it's somewhere for too long, it starts to feel like it's decided something."

Mau waited.

"I have a brother," said Umaru.

He had known. Everyone had known, in the vague way the house knew things about each other's worlds. Umaru had a brother, a big brother, who had looked after her. She had mentioned him once or twice in the first year, at the old inn, in passing. Then she had stopped mentioning him, the way people here stopped mentioning things that hurt.

"Taihei," said Umaru. "He's older than me. A lot older. He looked after me. After our—" She stopped. "He looked after me. He worked all day, at an office, and then he came home and cooked, and cleaned, and did my laundry, and I lay on the floor in a blanket and played games and ate chips and yelled at him when the chips ran out." Her voice had gone very small. "I was terrible to him. Every day. And he never once told me to leave."

Mau did not say anything.

"I was a different person there," said Umaru. "Here, I'm—" She stopped. "Here I'm the President. I carried blankets in the evacuation. I counted them. Nobody told me to. I made the fort. I made a *constitution*." She was crying now, properly, he could hear it. "I'm not that person anymore. The one on the floor. And if he comes here, and I see him, I'm scared I'll be her again. The minute he walks in. I'll lie down on the floor and say *Onii-chan, chips*, and everything I did here will just—"

She did not finish.

"And if I don't bring him," said Umaru, "he's there. Alone. In that apartment. Cooking for nobody." The coin clicked on the counter. "And he'd have to leave everything. His job. His friends. His whole life. For me. *Again.* He already gave up so much for me. How can I ask him to give up a whole world?"



Mau sat with his back against the shutter for a long time.

He thought about the old inn, the year after the field, when Umaru had first started following him around. When she had first decided, without asking, that he was the person in the house who would look after her. She had never called him anything but Mau. But she had kept following him. Through all of it. The memory crisis, the Trial, the window. She had held a corner of his weight when he could not carry it. She had made him a citizen of nothing, a non-citizen allowed on the step.

"Umaru," he said.

"What."

"He's your family."

The coin stopped clicking.

"And I'm your family now," said Mau. "Here." He leaned his head back against the shutter. "So think about it. If he comes, you'll have two older brothers."

Silence from inside the booth.

"Two," said Mau. "One who knows you from before, and one who knows you from after. One who did your laundry, and one who watched you count blankets in an evacuation." He paused. "You won't be her again. You can't be. I'll be standing right there. I've seen the President. I'm not going to let you forget her."

Still silence.

"And besides," said Mau, "think how useful it'll be. When Frieren wants a date with me, you'll have someone else to bother."

There was a sound from inside the booth.

It took him a moment to understand it. It was Umaru, laughing. Wetly, helplessly, through her crying, a sound like a hiccup and a laugh at the same time.

"That's—" she said. "That's the stupidest—"

"It's very strategic."

"You're an *idiot*."

"I'm the idiot who accepted," said Mau. "It's on my sash."



The shutter went up.

Umaru was sitting on the floor of the toll booth, in her sash and her hood, with her face red and wet and her coin in both hands. She looked at him for a long moment, sitting on the step outside.

Then she nodded.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Two."

Mau did not say anything. He waited, because there was more. He could see it.

"But not yet," said Umaru. She was looking at the coin. "Not now. Not this winter. Not with the road like Anko said, and the tracks, and the line that doesn't go to the bridge." Her voice firmed up as she spoke. It was the President's voice now, the one that had counted blankets. "When he comes, I want it ready. I want a room. A real one. Not a cushion. Not a corner in the Muslin. In the real house, with a door, and a window, and somewhere for him to cook, because he'll want to cook, he always wants to cook." She looked up at Mau. "And safe. I want the place safe. I'm not bringing him here to be scared. He's been scared for me his whole life."

"Ready and safe," said Mau.

"Ready and safe," said Umaru. "Then."

She got up off the floor of the booth. She looked round it, at all the places she had put the coin over the past month. The counter. The shelf. The little drawer for the fort's crayons. Rem's cup on its hook.

Then she opened the drawer under the counter, the one where the fort kept its most important documents, the constitution and the application form for the cooper's daughter and the record of every codename session, and she put the coin inside, on top of the constitution, and closed the drawer.

She took a crayon and wrote on the front of the drawer, in large careful capitals:

*LATER.*

"Twenty-two," said Umaru. "That's the last one. It stays there."

"Until it's ready."

"Until it's ready and safe."

She came out of the booth and sat down on the step beside him, in the dark common room, under the one lamp over the scratches, and leaned her hooded head against his arm.

"Don't tell Frieren," she said, "about the date thing."

"I won't."

"She'll make me sit on the roof with Turbo Granny."

"I won't tell her."

They sat there for a while.

From the dark at the end of the family corridor, where nobody could see, a voice that had been listening for some time said very quietly, "I heard that," and then there was the sound of a door closing, very gently, on a room with an east window.

Umaru put her face in her hands.

Mau laughed until he had to hold onto the toll booth.
