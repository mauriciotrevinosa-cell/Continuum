## Chapter 28 — What We Make Together

It started with Rimuru, on the night of the List, in the kitchen, after Mau had gone to bed.

He did not call a meeting. He simply sat down at the kitchen table, where Kaneki was making his careful coffee and Nazuna was eating leftovers and Anko was writing, and said, to nobody in particular, "He said *mine doesn't have a name*."

Nobody needed to ask who.

"Everyone at that table," said Rimuru, "is holding a coin that can bring someone they love here. Forty of us. More." He looked at the dark window. "He bought every one of them. Four hundred and thirty-seven times. And he sat there today and said his doesn't have a name, because everyone he'd want is already here." He was quiet for a moment. "He bought us the whole world, and he kept nothing."

Kaneki put down his coffee.

"So we give him something," said Nazuna, through a cold potato.

"Give him what?" said Anko. "He doesn't want anything. He never asks for anything. Six evenings of drawing the real house, and the only thing he drew was a room for someone else."

Rimuru was looking at the window, at the dark yard, at the ink tree.

"His hoodie," he said slowly.

They looked at him.

"When he woke up. In the clearing, with Frieren. He was wearing a hoodie. Black and orange. And a watch. And there was a red shirt in his bag, with a horse on it." Rimuru's face had gone thoughtful. "He doesn't remember where they came from. He doesn't remember anything from before. But they're from *my* world. I'd know them anywhere." He turned back to the table. "They're from a sport. A racing sport. Cars. The fastest cars in the world. The hoodie's one team. The shirt's another. Two of the most famous teams that ever existed."

"Cars," said Nazuna.

"Cars," said Rimuru. He was starting to smile. "And I remember what they looked like."



By the next morning, half the house knew. By the next afternoon, all of it did, except Mau.

That was the first problem. Keeping it from Mau. Mau lived in the middle of the house. He walked through every room in it every day, and he noticed things, and the Noise noticed things he did not.

Frieren solved it.

"I'll keep him busy," she said, at a whispered meeting behind the fort's curtain, which Rimuru had been specially permitted to enter beyond his bowl, for reasons of national importance.

"For how long?" said Umaru.

"As long as you need."

"It might be three days."

Frieren considered this. "I can keep him busy for three days," she said, with total confidence, and the fort looked at her, and nobody asked how, and nobody needed to.

She took him to the river the next morning, to look at the ice, inside the line where the bank was safe, and kept him there all day with a grimoire and a basket of Momo's food and a story about a frozen lake she had once crossed with Himmel that took eleven hours to tell. The day after, she announced that she had a cold, which she did not, and needed looking after, which she did not, and lay in bed in the room with the east window with her feet against his shins and made him read to her from all six of the old grimoires, in order, out loud. He did not suspect a thing. He thought it was the best two days he had had all winter.



The workshop became a secret.

Rimuru drew. That was the beginning of everything. He sat at the big bench under the lamp with a stack of Senku's best paper and a pencil, and closed his eyes, and remembered.

"Ciel," he said, quietly, at the start. "I know you're busy."

*I am busy,* said Ciel.

"Could you help me remember? Just the shapes. I saw them a thousand times on television when I was a kid. I just can't get them clear."

There was a pause.

*I am busy,* said Ciel. *I will make time.*

And then, for an hour, Rimuru drew with his eyes half closed, and the lines came out of his pencil with a precision he had never had in either life, every curve and angle exactly where it had been on a screen in a small apartment in another world, a lifetime ago. Senku stood at his shoulder and watched with an expression of open envy.

"Two of them," said Rimuru, when he had finished. He laid the drawings side by side on the bench. "This one." He touched the first. A long, low, flat car, red and white, with an open cockpit and wide wheels at each corner and a wing at the back. "That's his hoodie's team. Not the year of his hoodie. Way before. The hoodie's newer. Orange." He tapped the drawing. "But this one is the best car that team ever built. Some people say the best car anybody ever built. It won fifteen races out of sixteen in one year. The driver was—" He stopped. "The driver was a legend. In my country especially. Everybody knew his name. Even people who didn't care about cars."

"And this one," said Yuta, beside him, who had recognized it at once and gone quiet.

Rimuru touched the second drawing. Sharper. Lower. All red.

"His shirt," said Rimuru. "The horse. That team. This is the year the other legend won everything. Thirteen out of eighteen." He looked at the two drawings for a long moment. "I don't know if he'll remember. Probably not. But his hands might."



Everybody made a piece.

That was the rule. Nobody said it, but it was the rule. Wakana carved the bodies, two of them, out of a block of pale, fine-grained wood he had been saving for something important, with a set of knives so small and sharp that Senku asked to borrow them and was refused. He worked from Rimuru's drawings, turning the block in his hands, taking away a sliver at a time, the way he had once carved the faces of dolls in another world. It took him a day and a night.

Senku did the wheels. He turned them on his little lathe from hardwood, eight of them, and blackened the tyres with soot and oil so that they looked like rubber, and made axles of thin brass rod so that the wheels would actually spin. "They'll roll," he said, with enormous satisfaction. "On a flat surface. In a straight line. Ten billion percent."

Marin did the paint.

Marin was the only person in the house who could have done it, and everyone knew it. She mixed the reds herself from madder root and something of Kusuri's that she would not name, until she had a red that made Rimuru stop in the workshop doorway and say *yes, that one*, in a voice that was not quite steady. The white she made from chalk and egg. She painted with a brush made from three hairs, under the big lamp, with her tongue between her teeth, for hours, and did not let anyone talk to her.

The fort did the small things. Coco drew the numbers on the sides, with a fine pen, in water-ink, because it was not worth real ink, but she drew them with the same care as a glyph. Umaru supervised. Bocchi sanded the wings until they were smooth as glass, because sanding was quiet work and you could do it in a corner. Milim insisted on helping and was given the job of holding the finished bodies very, very gently while the paint dried, and did it for three hours without moving, with an expression of such ferocious concentration that nobody dared speak to her.

Diablo polished. Of course he did. When everything was painted and dry, he took both cars, and a soft cloth, and polished them, and when he gave them back they shone the way only things Diablo had polished could shine.

Kaneki made the tiny steering wheels. Nobody knew he could carve. He had not known either.

Eren made the stands. Two small plain wooden blocks for the cars to sit on, each with a little groove so they would not roll off a windowsill. He made them without being asked, and left them on the workshop bench without saying anything, and went out, and Mikasa saw them, and looked at the door he had gone out of for a long time.

Yuta, at the very end, when everything else was done, sat down with the red and white car in his hands and a fine brush and a tiny pot of yellow paint, and painted the helmet in the cockpit. Yellow, with a green stripe and a blue one. Very carefully.

"That's his," said Yuta, when Rimuru looked at him. "The driver's. Everyone in Japan knew that helmet." He put the brush down. "My dad had a poster."



On the last night, when everything was finished, they turned the two cars over on the workshop bench, under the lamp.

The undersides were bare wood, smooth and pale.

Rimuru took a pencil and wrote his name on the underside of the red and white car. Then he passed the pencil to Yuta, who wrote his. Then Senku. Then Wakana. Then Marin. Then, one by one, everyone who had come to the workshop in three days to make a piece, and everyone who had not, because nobody was going to be left off: the fort, all thirty of them, and Rings One and Two, and Seiko, who wrote hers with a flourish, and Turbo Granny, who could not write, and dictated an insult instead, which Coco wrote down. The names went on both cars, round and round the undersides, smaller and smaller, until there was no bare wood left.

At the very end, Rimuru took the pencil back. In the one small space that was left, under the axle of the red and white car, he wrote a single letter.

*C.*

*I am busy,* said Ciel, very quietly, to one head.

"I know," said Rimuru.

*Thank you,* said Ciel. *For putting me on it.*

He turned the cars back over. They sat on the bench under the lamp, on Eren's little stands, red and white and red, shining with Diablo's polish, with Yuta's yellow helmet in the cockpit and Coco's numbers on the sides. The whole workshop stood around them, crowded in, in the middle of the night, and nobody said anything.

"Tomorrow," said Rimuru.

Down the corridor, in the room with the east window, Frieren had a cold she did not have, and was making Mau read to her from the sixth grimoire, out loud, for the third time.
