On the morning of the forty-fifth day, Frieren's cold was gone.

It vanished overnight, completely, the way colds do not. She got up before the hour, which she never did, and was dressed and sitting on the end of the bed with her boots on when Mau woke, and said, "Breakfast," in a voice that left no room for anything else.

"You're better," said Mau.

"I'm better."

"That's fast."

"Elves heal quickly," said Frieren, who had once spent three days in bed with a sniffle and made him bring her soup on a tray, and stood up, and held the door open for him.

He went down the family corridor in his socks, still half asleep.

The common room was full.

Not breakfast-full. Full the way it had been full on the night of the List, and the night of rule five: the whole house, every bench, every corner, the fort out from behind its curtain, Seiko in her armchair, Rimuru by the cold hearth, all of them, already there, already sitting, and none of them eating. They were all looking at him.

On the long table, in the middle, where the bread usually went, there was something under a cloth.

Mau stopped in the doorway.

"What," he said.

Nobody answered.

Rimuru cleared his throat at the cold-hearth end of the table.

"She says," said Rimuru, "*looked, quiet, busy.*" And then, after a pause, with a small odd smile: "And she says *sit down*."

Senku's chalk squeaked.

Mau sat down.



Rimuru got up, and came round the table, and stood beside the thing under the cloth.

He did not make a speech. Rimuru did not make speeches. He stood there for a moment with his hand on the edge of the cloth, looking at Mau, and Mau saw that he did not quite know how to start.

"At the table," said Rimuru. "On the fortieth. You said yours didn't have a name."

Mau did not say anything.

"Forty of us," said Rimuru. "More. Every one of us with a coin in our pocket that can bring someone we love here. You bought them. All of them." His voice was very level. "Four hundred and thirty-seven times. And then you sat there and said everyone you'd want is already here. And you meant it, and it's true, and it's the most you thing anyone's ever said." He paused. "And it made all of us want to hit you."

Somewhere at the north end of the table, Umaru made a small agreeing noise.

"You bought us something impossible," said Rimuru. "So we made you something." He looked down at the cloth. "You might not even remember it."

He took the cloth off.



They sat on the long table in the morning light from the windows, on two small plain wooden stands, side by side.

Two cars.

Mau looked at them.

They were not like anything in this world. They were long and low and flat, with wide black wheels at each corner and wings front and back, and a small open space in the middle where a person would sit. One was red and white. One was all red. They shone, the way things shone when Diablo had polished them, so that the light from the window ran along their sides like water. There were numbers on the sides, small and careful. In the open cockpit of the red and white one, there was a tiny painted helmet, yellow, with a green stripe and a blue one.

He did not know what they were.

He knew what they were.

He did not know how he knew. It came up from somewhere below remembering, the way Frieren's face had come up on the first morning of his life, in the clearing, before he knew his own name. A shape that his body recognized before his mind did. Long and low and flat and fast.

"I don't know how much you remember," said Rimuru quietly. "From before. Probably nothing. But I know what you were wearing, the morning you woke up." He touched the red and white car, very lightly, with one finger. "This one is one of the cars of your hoodie's team. Your hoodie's newer. Orange. This is a lot older." His voice changed slightly. "But this is the best one that team ever made. Maybe the best one anybody ever made. It won fifteen races out of sixteen, in one year. And the man who drove it—" He stopped. Yuta, on the bench under the window, had looked down at his hands. "The man who drove it was a legend. Everyone knew his helmet."

Then he touched the red one.

"And this one," said Rimuru, "is your other shirt."

Mau put out his hand.

He did not decide to. His hand went out by itself and picked up the red and white car, very carefully, the way you pick up something you are afraid of breaking. It was lighter than it looked. The wood was smooth and warm. He turned it over in his hands. He turned one of the front wheels with his thumb, and it spun, smoothly, on its little brass axle, with a tiny whispering sound, and kept spinning after he took his thumb away.

He watched it spin.

Something in his chest had gone very tight and very warm at the same time.

"I don't remember," he said.

His voice came out strange.

"I don't remember any of it," said Mau. "I don't know who drove it. I don't know where it raced. I don't know why I had the hoodie. I don't—" He stopped. He was holding the car in both hands. "But I know it's fast," he said. "I don't know how I know. I just know. It's the fastest thing. It's the fastest thing there is."

Rimuru did not say anything.

"Your hands knew," said Yuta, from the bench, very quietly. "Rimuru said they might."



He set the red and white car down very carefully on its stand.

He picked up the red one. It was sharper, somehow. Lower. Meaner. He held it up to the light from the window and looked at it, and thought of the red shirt folded at the bottom of the backpack in the room with the east window. The one with the horse. The one Frieren had worn on a laundry day in the first year, because all her own clothes were wet, and had refused to give back for a week.

"How did you—" he said. "Who made them?"

"Everyone," said Rimuru.

Mau looked up.

"Turn them over," said Rimuru.

He turned the red one over.

The underside was covered in names.

Pencil. Every inch of the bare wood, round and round, in a spiral, in thirty different hands. Large and small. Neat and terrible. He saw *Wakana*, carved more than written. He saw *Marin*, with a heart. He saw *Senku*, in small square capitals, and *Suika*, very large. He saw *Umaru (PRESIDENT)* and *Agott (under protest)*. He saw *Bocchi*, so small he had to tilt the car to read it. He saw *Milim*, pressed so hard into the wood it had gone through the varnish. He saw *Eren*, plain, and *Mikasa* beside it. He saw *Kaneki*. He saw *Diablo*, in beautiful flowing script. He saw *Seiko*, with a flourish, and next to it, in Coco's careful hand, a sentence that began *Turbo Granny says* and then became extremely rude.

He turned over the red and white one.

More names. Everyone. Every single person in the house.

And at the very end, in the one small space under the axle, where there had been no room left for anyone else, a single letter.

*C.*

*I am busy,* said Ciel, to two heads, very softly. *But I made time.*



He did not cry, exactly. Not the way he had cried in the plaza on the eleventh morning. He sat at the long table with the two cars in front of him on their little stands, and the whole house around him, all of them, every name on the undersides, and he put his face in his hands for a moment and breathed, and when he took his hands away his eyes were wet and he was smiling so hard it hurt.

"You bought us something impossible," said Rimuru again, quietly. "So we made you something you might not even remember."

"I'll remember this," said Mau.

"Yeah," said Rimuru. "That was kind of the idea."



He carried them to the room with the east window himself, after breakfast, one in each hand, very carefully, down the family corridor, with Frieren walking beside him holding Eren's two little wooden stands.

The windowsill was not large. Ori's stone was on it, the small grey one from the tidepool, which had gone away and come back. There was room for the stands on either side of it, just.

He put them down. The red and white car on the left, toward the morning. The red one on the right. Ori's stone in the middle, between them.

Frieren stood beside him and looked at the windowsill.

"It's full now," she said.

"Yeah."

She looked at the two cars for a long moment, and at the stone, and at the snow outside the window, and the yard, and the ink tree in its ring of stakes with its ten leaves.

"The red one," she said. "That's the shirt. The one I wore."

"You remember?"

"You wouldn't give it back," said Frieren. "For a week."

"*You* wouldn't give it back."

"That's what I said."

He laughed. She looked at him sideways, and the small crooked smile came at the corner of her mouth.

Then she reached out, very carefully, with one finger, and turned one of the front wheels of the red and white car, and watched it spin, with its tiny whispering sound, on its little brass axle, in the morning light.

It kept spinning for a long time after she took her finger away.
