On the forty-sixth day, Diablo walked to the forest.

He went alone, on foot, in the snow, along the road north and then east along the edge of the fields where the tracks had gone, and then into the trees. He did not hurry. He walked the way he always walked when he was carrying something for Rimuru, one step behind and to the side of nobody, with great ceremony, as if an invisible master were walking ahead of him.

Rimuru had asked him the night before. Mau had been sitting beside Rimuru at the cold hearth when he asked.

"The witches," Mau had said. "They told Rem they were tired of coming. In the ten days, when I was at the falls. *Every second week. This room. This door. This chair. Back to the forest. And again.*" He had looked at the fire. "They said they wanted to talk about the contract. When I was myself again."

"Are you?" said Rimuru.

Mau had thought about it honestly.

"Mostly," he said. "Enough." He looked at Diablo. "Will you go? Not to bring them. To ask."

"Ask what?"

"Where they want to talk," said Mau. "Here, or there. They've come to me every second week for a year. Through the snow, through the window, through the night the seams went dark. I walked to them once." He paused. "It should be their choice."

Diablo had bowed.

So now he walked.



The cabin was where it had been. A small dark wooden house in a clearing deep in the old forest, where the trees were huge and grew far apart and the ground between them was soft with a hundred years of needles under the snow. Smoke came from its chimney. A path had been swept to its door, very neatly, by someone who did not like an untidy path.

Diablo stopped at the edge of the clearing.

He had never been here. Mau had, once, with Fern and Stark, on the day he walked to the cabin for the spell with Frieren's birthday in it, and he had come home and described it at the long table: a small dark house in the old trees, and a door at the back of its front room that opened onto a corridor of books going on much further than a cabin could. Stark had needed to sit down. Diablo had listened to the description very carefully, the way he listened to everything about places where old things lived, and had concluded that whatever kept those books was older than him, perhaps. Older than most things.

He walked up the swept path, and stopped at the door, and knocked.

Three times. Courteously. The knock of a servant at a door that is not his master's.

For a long moment, nothing.

Then the door opened.



It was the first witch.

She stood in the doorway with her hands folded in front of her, composed, precise, as she was at every session, looking at him with mild interest. Behind her the cabin went back much further than a cabin should, a long dim corridor lined floor to ceiling with books, lit by lamps that did not seem to need oil.

"The one who walks behind the slime," said the first witch.

"Diablo," said Diablo, and bowed.

"We know your name." She looked past him, at the empty path, at the trees. "You are alone."

"I am."

"He did not come."

"He sent me," said Diablo, "to ask a question. Before anything."

The first witch's expression did not change. But something behind her, in the corridor of books, did. Diablo felt it: a stirring, a shifting of attention, the way a room changes when several people who were not listening suddenly are.

"Ask," said the first witch.

Diablo straightened. He delivered it exactly as he had been given it, word for word, in his most formal voice, the voice he used for ambassadors.

"Mau is better," said Diablo. "Not entirely. Enough. He has heard that you are tired of coming, and that you wish to speak about the contract. He would like to speak about it too." He paused. "Before anything, he wishes to know: would you rather speak in the settlement? Or should he come to you?"

The first witch did not answer at once.

She stood in the doorway and looked at him for a long time. Then she turned her head, very slightly, and looked back over her shoulder into the cabin, into the long dim corridor of books.



Diablo saw them then. For the first time. All of them.

There had always been two at the sessions: the first witch, who asked the questions, and the other, who never spoke. Rem had told the house that there were more, in the forest. Nobody had seen them. Nobody had asked.

They were in the corridor of books, at various distances, the way people sit in a large house in the evening when each of them is doing something different.

There was a woman in black, with hair as white as milk, sitting at a reading desk under a lamp with a book open in front of her. She had stopped reading. She had one finger on the page, keeping her place, and she was looking toward the door with an expression of sharp, total, almost hungry attention, the expression of a person who has heard a question she did not expect and wants very much to know the answer.

There was someone lying full length on a sofa at the far end, under a blanket, apparently asleep, who had nevertheless opened one eye.

There was a woman with bandages wound round her knuckles, sitting very upright on a hard chair, with her fists on her knees.

There was someone half hidden behind a bookcase, peering round the edge of it, who pulled back out of sight the moment Diablo's eyes found her.

There was a woman at the corridor's kitchen end, sitting cross-legged on the floor beside a basket of something, eating it steadily, without stopping, even now. She was looking at the door too, and chewing.

And there was the other one. The quiet one.

She was standing closest to the door, just behind the first witch's shoulder, half in shadow. She was the one who had never spoken at the sessions, except once. *What is that?*, in the common room of the Muslin, about the fort. She had taken her ONE DAY PASS from Umaru's hand very quickly, as if she were afraid it would be taken back, and had folded it once, very carefully, and put it somewhere inside her robe.

She was looking at Diablo now. Not at the first witch. At him. And through him, Diablo thought, at the road behind him, and the fields, and the settlement beyond the fields, with its lit windows and its fort and its flag on a broom handle.

The first witch looked at her.

Not at the woman in black, who kept the books. Not at any of the others. At the quiet one.

Diablo understood something then that he had not understood before, in a year of sessions. The first witch asked the questions. But it was the quiet one the others looked to.

The quiet one did not say anything for a long moment.

Then she said one word. Her voice was very soft, and slightly hoarse, the voice of someone who does not use it much.

"There."

The first witch turned back to Diablo.

"We will come," she said. "To the settlement."

She paused.

"All of us."



Diablo walked back along the edge of the fields in the late afternoon with the news.

He did not hurry. But he walked a little faster than he had walked out, and somewhere around the place where the tracks had turned, forty paces from the last cottage, he realized that he was smiling, and that he did not quite know why.

At the Muslin, Mau was waiting on the front step with a cup of tea that had gone cold, which meant he had been waiting a long time.

He stood up when he saw Diablo come round the corner of the yard.

"Well?"

Diablo stopped in front of him, and bowed.

"Here," said Diablo. "Tomorrow, at the agreed bell." He straightened. "All seven."

Mau looked at him.

"Seven," he said.

"Seven," said Diablo. "There have always been seven. We have only ever been shown two." He paused. "The quiet one chose, Mau-sama. The others did as she chose."

Mau stood on the step for a long moment, with his cold tea.

Then he went inside, and found Fern, and told her that tomorrow there would be seven more at the long table, and Fern put down her pen and looked at him and said, "Seven," and went to tell Momo, and Momo dropped a pot, quietly, so that it went *tink*.
