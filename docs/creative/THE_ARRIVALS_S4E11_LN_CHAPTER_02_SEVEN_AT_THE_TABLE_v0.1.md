They came at the agreed bell.

Not a moment early, not a moment late. The bell rang in the tower over Arrival House, and at the last stroke there was a knock at the front door of the Muslin, three times, precisely spaced. Fern, who had been standing beside the door for a quarter of an hour with the ledger under her arm, opened it.

Seven women stood on the step in the snow.

The house had gathered in the common room behind her. All of it. Rings One and Two, at the long table and around it. The fort, in a cluster at the north end, very quiet for once. Seiko in her armchair. Rimuru by the cold hearth with Diablo behind him. Mau beside Rimuru, because there were two faces, and this was a thing the faces did together. Frieren a little behind Mau, near enough to touch.

The seven came in out of the snow, one after another, and stood in a line inside the door.

Mau had seen two of them every second week for a year. He had seen a third once, in a corridor of books. He had never seen the others.

The first witch, composed, precise, with her hands folded. The quiet one, just behind her shoulder. The woman in black with the milk-white hair, who kept the books, who looked round the common room with sharp, hungry interest, at the board and the four rings and Senku's QUIET tally and Wakana's plan pinned to the wall, as if she were reading all of it at once. The one with bandaged knuckles, very upright, who stood as though she expected to have to defend herself, and was trying very hard not to look as if she expected it. The one who had hidden behind a bookcase, who now stood half behind the woman with the bandages, and kept her eyes on the floor. And the one who had been eating, who was not eating now, but who looked at the long table, at the bread and the soup Momo had put out, with an expression Mau knew. He had seen it on the faces of refuge families in the first week after Last Defense.

Then the one who had been lying on the sofa under a blanket came in last, very slowly, as if every step were a negotiation, found the nearest wall, and leaned against it with her eyes closed.

"Seven," said Umaru, very quietly, from the fort.

Nobody had told the fort to be quiet. It simply was.

Umaru came out from behind the curtain.

She was in her hood and her sash, with the Guardian a step behind her. She had seven cards in her hand. She went down the line of witches, one by one, and held one out to each.

*ONE DAY PASS.*

"Ring Three can't live in the fort," said Umaru. "That's the rule. But anyone can visit, with a pass, if the President says so." She stopped in front of the quiet one. "The President says so."

The quiet one looked at the card in Umaru's hand.

Then she reached inside her robe and took out another card. Old. Folded once, very carefully, along a crease that had been opened and closed many times. ONE DAY PASS. *Valid: today.* In Umaru's crayon, from the day before Frieren's birthday.

She held it out to Umaru.

Umaru looked at the old card. She looked at the crease, worn soft. She looked up at the quiet one's face.

Then she took a crayon from her sash, and took the old card, and on the line that said *Valid: today*, she crossed out *today* and wrote, in large careful capitals, *ALWAYS*.

She gave it back.

The quiet one held it in both hands and did not say anything at all.



They sat at the long table. All seven. It took a while. The one from the sofa had to be helped, which the one with bandaged knuckles did, briskly and without comment. The one who hid sat between the woman with the bandages and the wall. The one who had been eating sat next to the bread, and Momo, without a word, put a bowl of soup in front of her before anyone else, and she looked at it, and then at Momo, and then she ate it, steadily, without stopping, and Momo put down another.

Fern sat at the head of the table with the ledger open.

"The contract," said Fern.

The first witch inclined her head.

"Every second week," she said. "For a year. The old inn. Upstairs, the second door. The chair that wobbles. An hour of questions about his seams. In return, we watch them, from the forest, and tell him when they flare." She folded her hands on the table. "We are tired of coming."

"I know," said Mau. "Rem told me."

"We are tired," said the first witch, "of the room, and the door, and the chair, and going back to the forest, and coming again." She looked round the common room. "We stood in your yard for two days, on the night your seams went dark. We stood under your window while you healed. We came here once, on a day of no questions, and ate at this table." She paused. "Then we went back to the forest. And the forest was very quiet."

Nobody said anything.

"So," said Mau. "What do you want?"



The woman in black spoke first.

She had not taken her eyes off the plan of the real house on the wall since she sat down. Now she turned them on Mau, with that sharp, total attention.

"I keep every book," she said. "Everything I have ever read. I do not lend. I do not carry. I do not send by anyone." She tilted her head. "So if your settlement is to have my books, my books must be in your settlement." She looked back at the plan. "There is a room on that wall with nothing in it. At the end of a corridor. Past the one who measures and the bridge the small president retired."

Mau went very still.

"That's—" he said. "That room doesn't have a name. It's for whoever—"

"It is not large enough," said the woman in black. "Nothing you have drawn is large enough." A small smile, very faint, at one corner of her mouth. "That has never stopped me before. A room only has to have a door. What is behind the door is my concern."

Across the table, Coco had gone white, then pink. She was looking at the woman in black the way a person looks at a door they have been searching for in the dark.

"You'd give us your library," said Fern slowly.

"I would *keep* my library," said the woman in black. "In your house. Where anyone who wished could read it." She looked down the table, at Coco, very briefly, and back. "Anyone."

Fern wrote.



The one who had been eating spoke next. She had finished her third bowl of soup. She put the spoon down very neatly, and looked not at Mau, or Fern, or Rimuru, but at the long table itself: at the bread, the soup, the bowls, the people.

"Your stores are thin," she said.

Fern's pen stopped.

"I walked past them, coming in," said the one who had been eating. "The barn by the kitchen. You have enough for the winter. For the people who are here. Not for more." Her voice was soft and very matter-of-fact. "Your road is closing. The merchant from the west has not come in three weeks. The one from the south comes slower every time. Something big walked across your north field and could not get in, and when the road is all the way closed, nothing will get in, and nothing will get out." She looked at the bread. "I do not like hunger," she said. "I have never liked hunger. I made a great many things once, in another world, so that nobody would be hungry, and most of them were a mistake." She looked up at Fern. "I would not make that mistake here. I would only grow things. Fields. Stores. The barn. Whatever your soil will give, I would make it give more."

Senku, on the floor by the cold hearth, had sat up very straight.

"Agriculture," said Fern.

"If that is the word."

"You want to run the settlement's food."

"I want nobody at this table to be hungry," said the one who had been eating. "Ever. That is all I have ever wanted." She picked up a piece of bread, and looked at it, and put it down again. "If that is called running it, then yes."

Fern looked at Senku. Senku looked back at her with the expression of a man who has just been handed a gift he had been trying to build for a year.

"Ten billion percent yes," said Senku.

Fern wrote it down.



Then the first witch spoke again.

"The rest of us," she said, "want something smaller."

She looked down the long table, at the four who had not spoken: the one with the bandages, the one who hid, the one leaning on the table with her eyes half closed, and the quiet one, holding her ALWAYS card in both hands.

"We want to live here," said the first witch. "Not in the fort. We know the rule. Near. In the settlement, or the house, when the house is built. A room. A door." She paused. "And we want to share the days."

Nobody spoke.

"We have watched this house for a year," said the first witch. "From the forest. From under the window. From your yard." Her voice did not change, but something underneath it did. "The way it eats together. The way it argues. The way it carries one person when that person cannot walk, and then argues about who carried him. The way it says *looked, quiet* in the mornings." She looked at the quiet one beside her. "One of us has watched it more closely than the rest. One of us has wanted it more than the rest."

The quiet one did not look up.

"Not the questions," said the first witch. "We will keep the questions. We are what we are. But not every second week, in a room, in a chair. Once a week. Here. For an hour." She paused. "At a table. With tea. All seven of us." She looked at Mau. "Not an interrogation."

Mau looked at the seven of them, at the long table, among the bread and the soup and the house.

"Not an interrogation," he agreed. "A conversation."

The first witch inclined her head.

"Because," said Mau, "if you're going to live here, and eat here, and run the barn, and keep your books at the end of my corridor, then you're going to be family." He looked at the quiet one. "And family doesn't interrogate each other at the table. Family just asks."

The quiet one looked up.



Fern wrote the new contract in the ledger, on a clean page.

She read it aloud when it was done, in her clear, even voice, so that everyone in the common room could hear.

*Ring Three. The seven.*

*One: Weekly, one hour, at the long table. Tea. All seven. A conversation.*

*Two: The keeper of books keeps her books at the end of the upstairs corridor, in the room with no name, when the house is built. Anyone may read them.*

*Three: The one who does not like hunger runs the fields, the stores and the barn, with Senku and Fern.*

*Four: The seven may live in the settlement, and in the house when it is built. Not in the fort. Visits by pass.*

*Five: No spells. No curses. Not on anyone in the settlement. Not ever.*

She looked up.

"Five," said the first witch. "Agreed. We do not need them here." She paused. "We have never once needed them with him."

"Signed," said Fern, and turned the ledger round.

Mau signed. Rimuru signed. Two faces, as they had signed the petition-vetting agreement in the ten days, as they had been named at the council.

Then the ledger went down the table to the seven.

The first witch looked at the line for signatures for a long time.

"We do not sign with names," she said. "Not yet."

"Then how—"

The first witch took the pen. Beside her own line, in a precise and unfamiliar hand, she did not write a name. She wrote a single word.

Then she passed the pen to the woman in black, who wrote a word beside hers. Then to the one who had been eating. Then down the line, one by one, to the one with bandaged knuckles, and the one who hid, who wrote hers so small it could barely be read, and the one from the sofa, who had to be woken up to do it, and did it lying with her cheek on the table.

The quiet one signed last.

When the ledger came back to Fern, she turned it round and looked at it, and her face changed, slightly, and she did not read it aloud.

Mau leaned over and read it upside down.

Seven words. Seven signatures. Not names.

*Pride. Greed. Gluttony. Wrath. Lust. Sloth.*

And the last, in a soft unsteady hand, beside a line that had been pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through the page:

*Envy.*
