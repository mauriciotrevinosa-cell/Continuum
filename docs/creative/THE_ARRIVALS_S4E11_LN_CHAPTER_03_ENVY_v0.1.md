After the contract, nobody quite knew what to do, so the house did what it always did, which was eat.

Momo brought out more bread. Seiko had her armchair turned round so that she could look at the seven women at her long table with the expression of one old thing assessing several others. The fort, which had been silent for an hour, came apart all at once into noise. Hakari tried to teach the string game to the one who had hidden behind the bookcase, who turned out to be very good at it and to blush every time she won. Umaru presented the one with bandaged knuckles with a codename application form, and the one with bandaged knuckles read it with great seriousness and asked whether violence was permitted under the constitution, and was told *gently*, and seemed satisfied. The one from the sofa had found the kotatsu in the big room within ten minutes, by some instinct nobody understood, and was asleep under it with only her head showing. Nano stood over her with a schedule and a look of deep professional respect.

The woman in black had found Coco.

Mau saw it from across the common room. Coco had gone up to her at the long table, with the heavy book about crystalline glyph structures held against her chest, and stood in front of her, and said something he could not hear. The woman in black looked down at the book. Then up at Coco. Then she held out her hand for it, and Coco gave it to her, and she opened it to a page near the back, the page Coco had been reading for a month, and looked at it for a long time.

Then she said something. Coco's face changed.

Mau found out later what it had been. Qifrey told him, at the door of the atelier, with his hands folded and his face carefully unreadable.

"She said, *this is the binding. You are looking for the undoing.*" Qifrey paused. "And then she said, *I have a book on undoing. Come to the cabin. Bring someone who is not afraid of corridors.*"

"And Coco?"

"Coco said yes before she finished the sentence." Qifrey looked across the common room at his student, who was standing at the long table beside the woman in black with her book held against her chest again and her face lit up like a lamp. "She asked me if I was afraid of corridors."

"Are you?"

"Terrified," said Qifrey. "I said no."



And the quiet one followed Mau.

He did not notice at first. He was moving round the common room the way he moved through a crowd in the house, a word here, a hand on a shoulder there, and somewhere around the third person he turned and she was there. Just behind him. Half a step. Not touching. Holding her ALWAYS card in both hands, and looking at him.

He turned to the next person, and she was there.

He went to the kitchen for more tea, and when he came back she was standing just inside the kitchen door, waiting, and fell in half a step behind him again as he went past.

Across the common room, Frieren had stopped talking to Fern.

She was watching.

Mau saw her watching. He saw her face do something very still and very careful. And then he saw her decide something, and stay where she was, and not come across the room, and he understood that she was going to let this happen. Whatever it was. Because it was not hers to stop.

He stopped by the cold hearth, where nobody was, and turned round.

The quiet one stopped too. Half a step away.

"Hello," said Mau.

She looked at him.

Up close, she was younger than he had thought, or older. It was hard to tell. Her eyes were very dark and very steady, and there was something in them that he had seen once before, under the window of the room with the east window, on the day of no questions, when she had looked back at the Muslin from the edge of the yard for a long time with her card in her hand. He had not been able to name it then.

He could name it now.

It was how Umaru had looked at the six cushions in the annex. How the cooper had looked at the fort's flag. How Kessoku looked at a stage. It was wanting. Plain wanting, the way you want a thing you are sure you will not be given.

"You followed me," said Mau.

"Yes," said the quiet one.

It was the most she had ever said to him.

"Why?"

She did not answer at once. She looked down at the card in her hands, ALWAYS, in Umaru's crayon. Then she looked up at him again, and took one small step forward, the half step that closed the distance, and put one hand on his sleeve. Very lightly. The way you would touch something in a shop that you knew you could not afford.

"I love you," she said.



The common room went quiet. Not all of it. Only the part that was close enough to hear, which was more of it than Mau would have liked.

He did not move.

She said it the way you say a line you have said many times before, to someone else, in another place. Practised. Worn. Not false, exactly. But the words had been shaped for another person, and they did not fit him, and she knew they did not fit him, and she was saying them anyway because they were the only shape she had.

"I love you," said the quiet one again, softer, holding his sleeve.

Mau looked at her hand on his sleeve.

He thought about what the first witch had said at the table. *One of us has watched it more closely than the rest. One of us has wanted it more than the rest.* He thought about a card folded once along a crease opened and closed so many times it had gone soft. He thought about the day of no questions, when she had eaten Momo's lunch with something that was almost hunger, and sat closer to the stage than anyone, and listened to every note.

She did not love him. Not the way the words meant. She had watched this house for a year, from the forest and the yard and the window, and she had seen what it gave. To him, and from him, to everyone. The way he carried people and the way they carried him. And she wanted it. She wanted it so badly that she had reached for the only words she knew for wanting something, the words she had used once before, in another world, for someone else.

"You don't," said Mau gently.

The quiet one's hand tightened on his sleeve.

"Love me," said Mau. "Not like that. I don't think that's what it is." He did not take her hand off his sleeve. "I think you watched this house for a year. And you saw what everyone in it gives everyone else. And you wanted it. And you thought the only way to get it was to say that." He paused. "To me. Because I'm the one in the middle."

She did not say anything. Her eyes had gone very bright.

"You don't have to," said Mau. "That's the thing. You don't have to say that to get it."

She looked at him.

"I can give you a family's love," said Mau. "That's what this is. That's what all of it is. Everyone in this room." He looked round the common room: at the fort, and the long table, and Seiko in her armchair, and Hakari teaching the string game, and the one from the sofa asleep under a kotatsu, and Frieren across the room, watching, very still. "You signed the contract. You're going to live here. Eat here. Sit at that table every week and ask me questions." He looked back at her. "That's not a thing you have to win. You already have it."

The quiet one was very still.

"And anyway," said Mau.

He did not know where it came from. It came out of him the way things came out of him on the roof now, lightly, without warning.

"The whole world already thinks we're monsters," said Mau. "For what we do. For what we are. For him." He nodded at Rimuru by the cold hearth. "For me. There's a rumour wall in this house with seven different names for us on it, and most of them are *monster*." He shrugged. "So everyone out there already envies us. Don't they?" He smiled at her. "They just don't know it yet. You were early."

There was a silence.

Then the quiet one laughed.

It came out of her all at once, in a startled, hoarse little burst, as if it had been shaken loose from somewhere very deep and had not been used in a long time and did not quite know how to work. She put her free hand over her mouth. Her shoulders shook. Her eyes, very dark and very steady, filled and spilled over, and she was still laughing, and she did not let go of his sleeve.

Across the common room, the first witch had turned round in her chair at the long table and was staring.

Nobody in the house had ever heard her laugh. Mau thought, looking at the first witch's face, that nobody in the cabin had either.



When she had stopped, she did not let go of his sleeve. She held it a moment longer. Then she let it go, deliberately, finger by finger, the way you put down something you have decided you do not need to hold onto, because it is not going anywhere.

"Satella," she said.

Mau looked at her.

"My name," said the quiet one. "It is Satella." She looked down at the card. "Nobody here has asked. Nobody has said it in a very long time." She held the card against her chest. "I wanted you to be the first."

"Satella," said Mau.

She closed her eyes for a moment, as if she were listening to how it sounded in this room.

Then she turned and went back to the long table, and sat down beside the first witch, and the first witch looked at her for a long time without saying anything. Then, very slightly, so slightly that only someone watching closely would have seen it, the first witch put her hand over Satella's on the table.



After that, the names came.

Not all of them.

The woman in black gave hers to Coco, across the crystal book, as casually as if it had never been a secret: "Echidna." Coco wrote it down at once, on the inside cover of her private notebook, under the lattice she had drawn eighty-one times in water.

The one who did not like hunger gave hers to Senku and Fern, at the end of the table, when they asked her what to call the person who would run the barn. "Daphne," she said, and took another piece of bread.

The others did not give theirs.

When Umaru, at the north end, demanded the rest for the fort's records, the first witch turned in her chair and looked at the small president in her hood and her sash for a long moment.

"At the tea," said the first witch. "One each week. Mine last." The faint pleased smile came, the one she gave when Mau drew a line and held it. "It is more interesting that way."

Umaru considered this with great seriousness. Then she made a note on her clipboard.

"The fort accepts," she said, "the terms."



The witches went home at dusk, back across the plaza and along the edge of the fields toward the forest, with Diablo walking one step behind and to the side of them, with great ceremony. At the edge of the yard, Satella stopped and turned and looked back at the Muslin for a long time, at the lit windows, at the fort's flag on its broom handle, at the long table where people were still eating, the way she had looked back once before.

This time she did not put her card away. She held it up, once, so that anyone at the window could see it. ALWAYS. Then she turned and followed the others into the dark.

Frieren came and stood beside Mau at the window.

"She's going to follow you around," said Frieren, "for a year."

"Probably."

"I'm going to have to get used to it."

"Are you angry?"

Frieren was quiet for a while, watching the dark where the witches had gone.

"No," she said at last. "She wants what I wanted. Before." She did not look at him. "To be given what you give everyone. I used to watch you give it to the whole house, at the old inn, and think, *I want to be the only one he gives that to*." A pause. "And then I understood that it isn't less, when you give it to everyone. It's more." She leaned her shoulder against his. "She'll understand too. It took me a long time. She has a long time."

He did not say anything. He put his arm round her, and they stood at the window and watched the dark.



That night, at the hour, before dawn, a voice came to two heads.

*Looked,* said Ciel. *Quiet.*

A pause.

*Busy,* said Ciel. *Eighteen days.*

Mau lay in the dark and smiled at the ceiling.

Frieren's foot found his shin, cold as a stone. *One. Still here.*

And then, after that, there was another voice.

It did not come to two heads. It did not come to anyone's head. It came to the whole settlement at once, the way it had come on the evening of the tenth day, when every stayer had found a coin in their palm: not loud, not soft, from no direction anyone could point to, to everyone, everywhere, in every bed and every corridor, in the Muslin and Arrival House and the refuge cottages and the old inn hours away, and a cabin in the forest that was bigger inside than out.

Three words.

*The world is evolving.*

Then nothing.

Mau lay very still in the dark.

Beside him, Frieren had gone rigid. Down the family corridor, he heard a door open, and then another. Somewhere in the house, someone was getting up. In the loft above the closet, Rimuru had changed shape without meaning to and was sitting up in the dark, very still, listening.

*Ciel,* said Rimuru, silently. *Did you—*

*I heard it,* said Ciel. *Everyone heard it.*

*What does it mean?*

There was a long pause. Longer than any Ciel had ever left.

*I do not know,* said Ciel. *I looked.* Another pause. *Quiet. Everything is quiet.*

Outside the east window, the yard lay white and still under the stars. The ink tree stood in its ring of stakes with its ten leaves. Beyond it, beyond the plaza and the last cottages, beyond the line in the snow that nobody could see and something from the hills had walked into and turned away from, the road ran north into the dark, pale and empty, toward a world that was, apparently, changing.

Nobody went back to sleep for a long time.

But in the morning, at breakfast, Rimuru cleared his throat at the cold-hearth end of the long table, and everyone turned.

"She says," said Rimuru, "*looked, quiet.*"

Senku's chalk squeaked on the second word.

And, after a moment, because there was nothing else to do and because they were a family before anything, everyone ate.
