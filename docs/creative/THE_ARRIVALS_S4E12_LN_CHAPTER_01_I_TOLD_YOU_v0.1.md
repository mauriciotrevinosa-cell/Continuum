For two days after the voice, the house was very careful.

Nobody said so. But on the forty-eighth day and the forty-ninth, people did not go far. The Searchers kept to the short routes. Anko walked the edge three times a night instead of once and came back each time with nothing in her notebook. Mikasa stood at the north end of the plaza for an hour in the afternoon, looking at the road, until Eren came and stood beside her and they looked at it together. Himmel watched the hills. At breakfast, when Rimuru said *looked, quiet*, the whole table let out a breath it did not know it had been holding.

*The world is evolving.*

Nothing happened. Nothing walked across the north field. No merchant came, but no merchant had come from the west in a month. The snow lay on the fields. The ink tree stood in its ring of stakes with its ten leaves. Ciel said *busy* in the mornings and *seventeen days*, and then *sixteen*.

On the morning of the fiftieth day, the deer came.



Mau was in the yard splitting wood with Yuta.

It was the kind of morning that had become ordinary: cold and bright, the snow hard underfoot, their breath going up white. Yuta set a round on the block and Mau split it, and Yuta set another. They did not talk much. They did not need to. It was the most restful thing either of them did all week.

Then Okarun came round the corner of the Muslin at a speed that was technically within Fern's limit, and stopped, and said, "There are deer in the north field."

Mau lowered the axe.

"Deer," said Yuta.

"A *lot* of deer," said Okarun. "The north watch counted forty. Then they stopped counting." He looked faintly wild. "They're just standing there. At the line. Where the tracks turned. All of them. Looking at us."

Yuta looked at Mau.

Mau did not look at Yuta.

He put down the axe and walked to the corner of the yard, where you could see past the washing court and the last cottages to the white sweep of the north field. Yuta came after him, not hurrying.

There were deer in the north field.

Okarun had not exaggerated. There were more than forty. There might have been a hundred. They stood in the snow in a long loose crowd, brown against the white, at the place where the tracks of the huge thing from the hills had come down and turned. Some of them were grazing at the snow, pawing through it for whatever was underneath. Most of them were simply standing, quite still, with their heads up and their ears forward.

Looking at the settlement.

"Huh," said Yuta.

Then, as Mau watched, the deer began to move.

Not away. In. Through the line in the snow that the huge thing had walked into and turned back from, without slowing, without noticing, the way you walk through a doorway. They came down the north field in a long unhurried procession, past the last cottage, past the slope behind the washing court where the trays were stacked, and into the settlement.

The protection, Mau thought, distantly, was for things that meant harm. Deer did not mean anything. Deer were deer.

The refuge children came out of the cottages to look. Then the washing court came out, with wet hands. Then half the house, from the Muslin, in the doors and the windows. The deer came through the middle of all of them, down the plaza road, calmly, as if they had an appointment, and the crowd parted for them and closed behind them and followed.

And in the middle of the deer, walking among them as if she were one of them, was a girl.

She was a teenage girl in a school uniform. Navy blue, with a pleated skirt and a red ribbon at the collar, the uniform of some school in some world where there were schools. She had a paper bag in one hand. She was eating something out of it, steadily, with total contentment, and every few steps she held something out to the nearest deer, and the deer ate it out of her hand.

She had antlers.

Not a headband. Not a hat. Antlers. Two great branching antlers, the colour of old wood, growing out of her brown hair on either side of her head as naturally as ears, and swaying slightly as she walked.

Mau stood at the corner of the yard and stared at her.

He could feel Yuta beside him.

He could feel Yuta, specifically, turning his head and looking at him. Looking at his face. Looking at him looking at the girl with the antlers in the middle of a hundred deer coming down the plaza road on a winter morning, and very clearly understanding exactly what Mau was thinking, because Yuta had known him for a long time.

There was a long silence.

"I told you," said Mau, "they were up to something."



He had told him.

In the first month at the old inn, on the north trail, Mau had seen a deer. Just one. It had stood among the trees and looked at him for a long time, in a way that animals did not usually look at people, and then it had gone. He had come back to the inn and told everyone about it. Then he had seen it again, a week later, in the same place. And again. And he had told everyone about it again, every time, at length, over dinner, for most of the first month. *It was the same one. I'm sure it was the same one. It looked at me like it knew something.*

And Yuta had told him to stop.

Every time. Patiently, then less patiently, then with the flat exhausted voice of a man who has heard about a deer forty times. *It's a deer, Mau. Deer look at things. That's what deer do. They're not up to anything. Please stop talking about the deer.*

Frieren had said, once, *maybe it followed you home.* That had made it worse.

That had been more than two years ago.

Now there were a hundred deer in the plaza, and a girl with antlers eating something out of a paper bag in the middle of them.

Mau turned his head and looked at Yuta.

Yuta was looking at the deer.

He opened his mouth. Mau watched him do it. He watched Yuta open his mouth to say it, *it's a deer, Mau, deer look at things*, the way he had said it forty times at the old inn. Yuta stood there with his mouth open, looking at a hundred deer and a girl with antlers.

And for the first time in the whole history of their friendship, Yuta said nothing at all.

He closed his mouth.

He looked at the deer a moment longer. Then he turned round, walked back to the chopping block, picked up the next round of wood, set it on the block, and stood waiting, with his back to the plaza, for Mau to come and split it.

Mau laughed so hard he had to sit down in the snow.



The girl with the antlers had stopped in the middle of the plaza.

The deer stopped with her, all of them, in a great loose ring, as if she had told them to, although she had not said anything. The crowd stopped too, at a respectful distance. Mikasa had arrived at the edge of it with her hand not quite on her sword. Anko was writing very fast. Somewhere in the Muslin, Umaru's voice could be heard demanding to know whether deer required a pass.

The girl finished what was in her mouth. She looked round the plaza, at the crowd, at the Muslin, at the fort's flag on its broom handle in the window, with an expression of mild, pleasant interest, the expression of someone who has wandered into a new town and found it agreeable.

Then her eyes found Mau, sitting in the snow at the corner of the yard, still laughing.

She tilted her head. The antlers tilted with it.

She walked across the plaza toward him, unhurried, with the paper bag in her hand. The deer parted for her and closed behind. She stopped in front of him and looked down at him, sitting in the snow, with great seriousness.

"Excuse me," she said. "Are you the human who talks to deer?"

Mau stopped laughing.

"The deer say," said the girl, "that a long time ago, on a trail in the north, a human used to come every day and talk to them. For a whole month. About nothing. About how cold it was, and whether the soup would be good, and about an elf." She considered him. "They said he was very nice, and very strange, and talked too much."

From the chopping block, without turning round, Yuta made a small sound.

"They said," the girl went on, "that he lived here now. So I came to see." She held out the paper bag. "Would you like a deer cracker? They're very good. They're for deer. But I eat them too."

Mau looked up at her, with snow in his hair.

He took a deer cracker.

"I'm Noko," said the girl with the antlers. "Most people call me Nokotan." She looked round the plaza again, at the hundred deer and the staring crowd and Mikasa's hand not quite on her sword, and smiled, very pleasantly. "I think I'm lost."
