Nokotan did not know how she had got here.

She explained this at the long table in the Muslin, an hour later, with a bowl of Momo's soup in front of her and her antlers very carefully angled so as not to knock the lamp, which she had already knocked twice. The whole house had crowded into the common room to listen. The deer had stayed in the plaza. Most of them were lying down in the snow, chewing. A few had found the hay bales at the bottom of the slope. One had got into the washing court and was being shouted at.

"I was walking home from school," said Nokotan, "and I fell in a hole."

"A hole," said Fern, who had the ledger open.

"A hole," Nokotan agreed. "It was shaped like a deer."

There was a pause.

"The hole," said Fern carefully, "was shaped like a deer."

"It was a very good hole," said Nokotan. "I fell for a long time. Then I was in a forest, in the snow, and there were deer." She ate a spoonful of soup with great satisfaction. "So I stayed with the deer. They were very welcoming. We ate grass and bark and things. I had my crackers." She held up the paper bag, which did not seem to be getting any emptier. "And they told me about the human."

Everyone looked at Mau.

Mau looked at his soup.



"How long ago?" said Rimuru, from the cold hearth.

Nokotan thought about it.

"A month?" she said. "Maybe. I wasn't counting. Deer don't count." She tilted her head, and the antlers tilted, and the lamp wobbled, and Ori caught it. "Then the deer said the human lived here, and we should come and see. So we came." She looked round the common room with pleasant interest. "This is a nice house. It's very loud."

"It's not usually this loud," said Fern.

"It's always this loud," said six people.

"You came in through the line," said Anko. She had her notebook open. "The thing in the north field. Something huge walked into it last month and turned back." She looked at Nokotan closely. "You walked straight through."

Nokotan looked blank.

"What line?"

Anko looked at her for a long moment. Then she wrote something in her notebook and underlined it.



It became clear quite quickly that Nokotan did not work the way things worked.

It started with the crackers. The paper bag did not empty. She ate from it continuously, and fed the deer from it continuously, and at one point gave a cracker to every child from the refuge families who had crowded in at the door, which was eleven children, and the bag was exactly as full afterward as before. Senku asked to look inside it. She handed it to him. He looked inside. It was a perfectly ordinary paper bag with about six crackers in it.

He handed it back. She reached in and took out a cracker. He took the bag again and looked inside. Six crackers.

"That's not possible," said Senku.

"They're very good crackers," said Nokotan.

Then it was Milim.

Milim had been watching the girl with the antlers since she walked into the plaza with an expression of narrowing suspicion, the expression of a dragon who has detected a rival. Halfway through the soup she stood up and said, "Race me."

Nokotan looked up.

"Why?"

"Because," said Milim. She did not elaborate. She was pointing at the antlers.

"Okay," said Nokotan pleasantly, and stood up, and walked to the door, and went out.

Milim went out after her. So did most of the house.

They raced the length of the plaza and back. Milim was, by a very large margin, the fastest thing anyone in the settlement had ever seen except for her own Milim Beam. She went down the plaza road in a white blur that threw up a wave of snow on either side, holding back so hard that Umaru's *gently* could be heard echoing faintly off the walls of Arrival House.

Nokotan walked.

She did not run. She walked, at an ordinary unhurried pace, eating a cracker, down the plaza road. And she arrived at the far end at exactly the same moment as Milim, and turned round, and walked back, and arrived at the start at exactly the same moment as Milim again. She was not out of breath. Her antlers had a little snow on them.

Milim stood at the start line staring at her.

"How," said Milim.

"I don't know," said Nokotan. "I just do." She held out the bag. "Cracker?"

Milim looked at her for a very long time.

Then she took a cracker.

"This isn't over," she said, with her mouth full.

"Okay," said Nokotan.



By the afternoon, Karane had lost her mind.

It was nothing in particular that did it. It was everything, in sequence. It was the bag that did not empty. It was the race. It was Nokotan standing in the middle of the common room and turning round too fast, so that her antlers swept every cup off the long table, and then turning round again and every cup being back on the table, upright and full, without anyone having touched them. Diablo, behind Rimuru's chair, watched that one with something like professional envy. It was Nokotan sitting down on the bench and the bench being, for a moment, a different shape. It was the moment when one of her antlers caught on the fort's curtain and she simply took the antler off, untangled it, and put it back on, with a small click, like a hat.

"*NO,*" said Karane.

Everyone turned.

Karane was on her feet. She was pointing at Nokotan's head with a shaking finger, red in the face, the way she went red when she was about to say something she would regret.

"You can't just *take them off!*" said Karane. "They're *antlers!* They're attached to your *skull!* There's *bone!* That's not how *anything works!*"

Nokotan touched one of her antlers thoughtfully.

"They come off," she said, "when they're tangled."

"*They do NOT come off!*"

"They do," said Nokotan, and took one off, and held it out to Karane to demonstrate, and put it back on. *Click.* "See?"

Karane made a sound that nobody in the house had ever heard her make, a sort of high strangled squeak, and sat down very hard on the bench. Nano patted her shoulder. Shizuka held up her book, on which she had written, in very large letters, *I LIKE HER*.

"I need to lie down," said Karane, faintly.

"There's a kotatsu," said Hakari.

"I need to lie down under a kotatsu forever."



Rimuru had been very quiet through all of it.

Mau, at the end of the table, could see why. He could hear it. Ciel had been talking since the soup. Not to the house. To two heads. Low and fast and continuous, in a tone Mau had never once heard from her, the tone of a very precise instrument being asked to measure something that would not hold still.

*She has no seams,* said Ciel. *Not like you. Not like any of them. Every person in this room carries the system they came from, mana or cursed energy or magicules or something, in a structure I can read. She has no structure. There is nothing to read.* A pause. *The bag is a paper bag. It contains six crackers. It also contains an unlimited number of crackers. Both of these are true. I have checked eleven times.*

*Is she dangerous?* said Rimuru, silently.

*I do not think she is anything,* said Ciel. *I think she simply is.* Another pause, longer. *I asked Construction to look. It was curious. It looked.*

Mau went very still.

*It is not a pattern,* said Ciel. *It does not follow rules that can be learned. It follows rules that change to suit whatever is funniest at the time.* There was something in her voice now that might, in anyone else, have been called despair. *Construction tried to analyze it. It came back with more questions than it went in with. I have instructed it to stop.*

"Ciel," said Rimuru out loud, very carefully, because the whole table had noticed him go quiet and was now looking at him. "What *is* she?"

There was a pause.

*I believe,* said Ciel, *that the word in your world is* cartoon.

Rimuru put his face in his hands.

"What did she say?" said Fern.

"She says," said Rimuru, through his hands, "that she doesn't know."

"She knows," said Mau.

"She *knows*," Rimuru admitted. "She just doesn't want to say it, because it's stupid."

*It is extremely stupid,* said Ciel. *It is the stupidest thing I have ever observed. I have observed a great many things.* A pause. *Tell Mau that whatever he is thinking of trying, he is not to try it.*

"She says," said Rimuru, lifting his head and looking down the table at Mau, "that whatever you're thinking of trying, you're not to try it."

Mau, who had been thinking, for just a moment, about how useful it would be to have a bag that never emptied, put his hands flat on the table.

"I wasn't going to," he said.

*He was going to,* said Ciel.

"I *wasn't*—"

Across the table, Nokotan had taken off one antler again and was using its tip to scratch an itch behind her ear. She put it back on. *Click.* Then she looked up and saw the whole table staring at her.

"What?" she said pleasantly, and held out the bag. "Cracker?"
