Jinshi registered her himself.

He did it at the intake table at Arrival House at the eighth bell on the fifty-first morning, in his beautiful courtly hand, in the guest register that had not had a new name in it since the window. He had cleared the whole desk for it. He had sharpened three pens. When Nokotan came in through the door with her paper bag and her antlers, ducking slightly under the lintel, followed by four deer who had decided to come in too, Jinshi rose from his chair and bowed as if she were a visiting dignitary.

"Welcome to Arrival House," said Jinshi.

The deer looked at him. Nokotan looked at him.

"Thank you," she said. "Is it all right if they come in?"

Jinshi looked at the four deer standing in the hall of Arrival House, on the clean floor, between the empty waiting benches, chewing.

"Of course," said Jinshi.

He sat down and opened the register.

*Name:* he wrote, and looked up.

"Noko Shikanoko," said Nokotan.

*Origin:*

"A hole," said Nokotan. "Shaped like a deer."

Jinshi wrote, without hesitation and in his most beautiful hand: *A hole, shaped like a deer.*

*Companions:*

Nokotan looked over her shoulder, out of the open door, at the plaza, where about a hundred deer were lying in the snow.

"Approximately one hundred deer," said Jinshi, writing it.

"Approximately," Nokotan agreed.

*Intended length of stay:*

She thought about it.

"I don't know," she said. "Until I have to go home."

Jinshi wrote that down too. Then he looked at the page for a long moment. Then he turned the register round and looked at it as a whole, the first entry on a new page in a month, and something in his face eased that had been tight since the twelfth day.

"Guest," he said quietly. "One."

At the long table that night, Fern read the ledger, and when she came to *Guests*, she paused, and looked at the page, and read it exactly as it had been written.

"Guests," said Fern. "One." A pause. "Deer."

Nobody corrected her.



She stayed a week.

She slept at Arrival House, in the first guest room, the one that had been empty since the window, with the window open, because the deer liked to look in at night. In the mornings she came to the Muslin for breakfast. She arrived exactly when Rimuru was clearing his throat at the cold-hearth end of the table, every single morning, as if she had been listening at the door for it, and said *looked, quiet* along with everyone else without knowing what it meant, and then sat down and ate three bowls of porridge and a great many crackers.

The deer stayed too. They lay in the plaza and the yard and the hay at the bottom of the slope. They went where they liked. They did not seem to need feeding, although they accepted feeding enthusiastically from everyone. They were very gentle, and very curious, and completely unafraid. Within two days, the refuge children were riding them.

Fern made a rule about this. Fern made a rule about most things. The rule was that children could ride deer only at the speed of a walking deer, which was a rule so specific that Okarun, reading it on the board, looked personally attacked.

Umaru made the deer a pass.

It was one pass, a very large one, on the back of an old map, in crayon. It said *ONE DAY PASS (DEER)* at the top, and *VALID: ALL DEER, THIS WEEK*, and had the Ministry's seal at the bottom. She nailed it to the toll booth. The deer did not read it. But for the whole week, any deer that wandered into the fort was allowed to stay, and several did, and lay among the cushions, and were fed, and Agott said that this was a grotesque abuse of the constitution, and then fed one of them a cracker when she thought nobody was looking.



On the second day, Seiko made crackers.

She had been watching the paper bag since the plaza with the expression of an old woman who has seen something done badly and cannot bear it. On the morning of the fifty-second she had her armchair carried into the kitchen, and summoned Momo, and said: "Rice flour. Bran. A little sugar. No salt."

"Grandma," said Momo, "what are we making?"

"Deer crackers," said Seiko. "Proper ones. The kind they sold in Nara when I was a girl, for the deer in the park." She looked at the kitchen fire with something that was not quite nostalgia. "I fed the deer there. A long time ago. Before you were born. Before your mother was born."

Momo looked at her grandmother for a long moment.

"I didn't know that," she said.

"You never asked," said Seiko. "Flour."

They made them all morning. Round, flat, pale-brown discs, pressed thin and baked crisp, stamped in the middle with the end of a wooden spoon because Seiko said there should be a mark. When they were done, Momo carried a plate of them out into the plaza where Nokotan was sitting on a hay bale with three deer.

Nokotan looked at the plate.

She took one. She looked at it. She smelled it. She broke it in half, and listened to the snap, and ate one half, slowly.

Her eyes filled with tears.

"These are *real*," she said.

"Grandma made them," said Momo.

Nokotan stood up, holding the other half of the cracker, and walked straight across the plaza to the kitchen door, where Seiko's armchair stood in the doorway in the cold, and stopped in front of the old woman.

Then she bowed. A real bow, very deep, with her antlers swinging down nearly to the snow.

"Thank you," said Nokotan.

Seiko looked at her over her fan.

"Hm," said Seiko. "Good."

That was all. But Momo, behind the plate, saw her grandmother's face, and did not say anything about it for the rest of the day, and was very gentle with her at dinner.



On the third day, Frieren tried to find out what Nokotan was made of.

She did it the way Frieren did everything, with great patience and no explanation. She sat down beside Nokotan on a hay bale in the plaza, and watched her feed the deer for an hour, and then said: "Do you know any spells?"

"No," said Nokotan.

"Can you sense mana?"

"What's mana?"

Frieren looked at her for a long moment. Then, very quietly, she let her own mana suppression slip. Just a little, just for a moment. It was a thing she almost never did, because in her world it had been the most important secret she kept. Mau, splitting wood at the corner of the yard, felt it go out across the plaza like a door opening in a dark house.

The deer looked up.

Nokotan did not.

"You really can't feel it," said Frieren.

"Feel what?"

Frieren closed the door again. She sat on the hay bale beside the girl with the antlers and looked at her with an expression Mau had seen on her face only when she found a grimoire she could not read.

"You're empty," said Frieren slowly. "There's nothing there. No mana. No anything." She tilted her head. "But you're not empty. You're full of something. I don't know what it is."

"Crackers," suggested Nokotan.

Frieren considered this seriously.

"Maybe," she said.

She did not try again. But she sat with Nokotan on the hay bale for the rest of the afternoon, watching the deer, and when Mau came past with the wood she looked up at him and said, quite seriously, "I think I like her," in the voice of someone who had not expected to.



On the fourth day, at dusk, Mau saw the deer.

Not Nokotan's. *The* deer.

He was coming back from the washing court along the edge of the north field, with his basket on his hip and his shirts wrung the right way, and he looked up and there it was. Standing at the edge of the field, where the north trail would have been if there had been a north trail here. Apart from the others. A single deer, brown against the snow, with its head up.

Looking at him.

He stopped.

He did not know how he knew it was the same one. He had not known how he knew at the falls, either, in the ten days, on the morning he burned the pages. He simply did, the way he had known Frieren's face on the first morning of his life. It was the deer from the north trail at the old inn, in the first month. The one he had talked about until Yuta begged him to stop. The one that had stood by the coast road, and at the falls, and had looked at him every time with that same steady look, as if it knew something.

It was standing at the edge of the field, a little apart from Nokotan's hundred, not grazing, not moving.

Looking at him.

Nokotan was on the slope behind the washing court with the refuge children, watching them ride. Mau walked over to her without taking his eyes off the deer.

"Nokotan."

"Mm?"

"That one." He pointed. "At the edge of the field. On its own."

Nokotan looked.

She looked for a long time. Longer than she had looked at anything all week. The pleasant interest went out of her face, slowly, and something else came into it, something Mau could not name.

"That one isn't mine," she said.

"I know."

"It wasn't with us," said Nokotan. "In the forest. When I fell. I never saw it." She was still looking at it. "It's just here."

"Since when?"

Nokotan was quiet.

"Since before me," she said. "I think. Since before everyone." She turned and looked at him, and her face was very serious under the antlers. "The deer don't talk about that one. I asked. They just look at it." She looked back at the field. "Like that."

When Mau turned back to the field, the deer was gone.

He stood on the slope with his basket of shirts and looked at the empty place in the snow where it had been. He did not know what he felt.

Frieren found him there a little later, and stood beside him, and looked where he was looking.

"It was here," he said.

"The deer?"

"*The* deer."

Frieren was quiet for a while.

"Maybe," she said, "it followed you home."

He turned and looked at her.

She was not smiling. She was looking at the empty field with the same expression she had worn on the hay bale, looking at Nokotan. The expression she wore for a grimoire she could not read.

Then she took his basket from him, and carried it in, and did not say anything else about it.
