The first tea was on the fifty-fourth day, at the agreed bell, in the common room of the Muslin.

The house cleared out for it.

Nobody had been told to. Nobody had made a rule. But at the bell before the agreed bell, people began to drift out of the common room in ones and twos, the way water drains out of a basin. The fort pulled its curtain closed and went quiet behind it. Fern took the ledger to the kitchen. Senku took a book to the workshop. Rimuru, by the cold hearth, looked at Mau, and nodded once, and went up to his loft. Even Diablo, who had never willingly left a room Rimuru was not in, took the teapot and went to stand outside the front door.

Frieren went last.

She stood at the end of the family corridor with her grimoire under her arm and looked back at Mau, alone at the long table, with eight cups set out and Rem's best teapot in the middle.

"I'll be in the corridor," she said.

"You don't have to wait."

"I know," said Frieren, and went and sat on the floor of the family corridor with her back against the wall, where she could not see the common room, and opened her grimoire, and did not read it.



The seven came at the bell.

They came in the way they had come on the forty-seventh day, out of the snow, one after another. But this time they did not stand in a line inside the door. They went straight to the long table, as if they had been sitting at it all their lives, and sat down. The first witch at the end facing Mau, with her hands folded. Satella at Mau's left, very close, holding her ALWAYS card. Echidna in black at his right, looking round the empty common room at the board and the plan and Senku's QUIET tally with that sharp, hungry attention. Daphne beside the bread. Wrath, with her bandaged knuckles, very upright. Lust, half hidden behind Wrath's shoulder. And Sloth, who sat down on the bench, and then lay down on it, and closed her eyes.

Mau poured the tea.

He had thought, beforehand, that this would be strange. The sessions at the old inn had always had a shape: the room, the door, the wobbly chair, the questions in order, his seams first and then whatever else. It had been an hour of being looked at very closely by people who wanted to know how he worked. He had liked it, in a way he could not explain. But it had been an interrogation, however gentle. They both knew it.

This was a table, and tea, and seven women he had promised would be family.

He did not know what to say.

"So," said Mau.

The first witch looked at him across the table. The small pleased smile came.

"You may ask first," she said. "That is the rule now. It is a conversation."

Mau thought about it.

"The voice," he said. "Four nights ago. *The world is evolving.* You heard it?"

"Everyone heard it," said the first witch.

"Do you know what it means?"

The seven were quiet. Even Daphne stopped eating.

It was Echidna who answered. She had been looking at the board, at the four rings and the QUIET tally. Now she turned and looked at Mau, and her face was very still.

"No," said Echidna.

It was such a small word. But the way she said it made the back of Mau's neck go cold.

"I keep every book," said Echidna. "Everything I have ever read. I have read a great many things, in a great many places, for a very long time." She put one finger on the rim of her cup. "There is very little left in any world that I do not know." She looked down at the tea. "I do not know this."

Nobody said anything.

"It is the first thing in a very long time," said Echidna, "that I have not known." A pause. "I find that I am delighted." Another pause. "I also find that I am afraid. I had forgotten that those could be the same."

Satella, at Mau's left, moved very slightly closer to him on the bench.



They were twenty minutes into the hour when the door opened.

Not the front door. The side door, the one from the yard, which stuck in the cold. It opened with a bang and a scrape, and a gust of snow came in, and then a pair of antlers, ducking carefully under the lintel, and then Nokotan, with her paper bag, followed by a deer.

She stopped.

She looked at the long table: Mau, and the seven women in their strange robes, and the teapot, and the eight cups.

"Oh," said Nokotan. "Is this a party?"

The seven turned and looked at her.

It was, Mau thought later, the most completely the seven had ever looked at anything in his presence. Every one of them. Even Sloth, on the bench, had opened her eyes. Fourteen eyes, very old, very steady, fixed on a teenage girl in a school uniform with antlers and a paper bag, standing in a puddle of melting snow with a deer behind her.

Satella said, very softly, almost to herself, "What is *that*?"

It was the first thing she had ever said in the Muslin, months ago, on the day of no questions, about the fort. Mau did not think she knew she had said it again.

"It's Nokotan," said Mau. "She's a guest. She's staying at Arrival House for a week." He looked at Nokotan. "This is a tea. It's kind of private."

"Oh," said Nokotan. "Sorry." She did not leave. She looked at the bread on the table, and at Daphne beside the bread, who was looking back at her. "Can I have some bread?"

"I—" said Mau.

Daphne picked up a piece of bread and held it out.

Nokotan came over to the table, very politely, and took the bread, and then, because it was only fair, reached into her paper bag and took out a cracker and held it out to Daphne in return.

Daphne looked at the cracker.

She took it. She ate it. Her face did something very complicated.

"More," said Daphne.

Nokotan held out the bag.



Echidna stood up.

She came round the end of the table, slowly, and stopped in front of Nokotan, and looked at her. Up close. With the total, hungry attention she gave the books in her corridor, and Mau's seams, and anything in any world that she did not yet know.

She looked for a long time.

Nokotan looked back at her with pleasant interest, chewing.

"You have no seams," said Echidna.

"Okay," said Nokotan.

"You have no structure. You carry no system. There is nothing in you that can be read." Echidna tilted her head. "I have read the oldest things in a hundred worlds. I have read creatures that were nothing but hunger, and creatures that were nothing but time, and once, a god's handwriting." She leaned a little closer. "I cannot read you."

"I'm not very interesting," said Nokotan.

"You are the most interesting thing I have seen in four hundred years," said Echidna, "and you are not even *trying*." She straightened up. Her face was doing something Mau had never seen it do, which was look frustrated. "What *are* you?"

Nokotan thought about this seriously.

"A transfer student," she said. Then, after a moment: "And I like deer."

Echidna looked at her for a very long time.

Then she turned round, walked back to her place at the table, sat down, picked up her cup, and drank her tea in one swallow, with the air of a woman who has just been personally defeated by something she will spend the next century thinking about.

Across the table, the first witch was smiling. Not the small pleased smile. A larger one.

"She has never once," said the first witch to Mau, very quietly, "not known what something was."

"Twice in one day," said Mau.

"Twice in one day," the first witch agreed. "It is a good day."



On the bench, Sloth sat up.

It took her a long time. She did it in stages, the way she did everything, as if each movement had been negotiated separately. When she was upright, she looked at Nokotan, standing by the bread with Daphne and the deer and the paper bag, for a long moment, with heavy-lidded, very ancient eyes.

Then she looked at Mau.

"Sekhmet," she said.

Mau did not understand at first.

"My name," said the witch who had been lying on the bench. Her voice was low and slow and enormously tired, as if speaking were a great deal of effort and she had decided this was worth it. "Sekhmet." She looked at Nokotan again. "It seemed like the kind of day for it."

Then she lay back down on the bench and closed her eyes.

The first witch, at the end of the table, took a small notebook from her robe and made a mark in it.

"One," she said.



Nokotan left before the hour was over, with her deer, and half the bread, and a promise from Daphne to show her the barn, which nobody had asked Daphne to make. The side door stuck behind her. The snow came in and settled.

The rest of the tea was quiet. They talked about ordinary things. Mau asked Daphne about the barn, and Daphne told him, at length, about winter wheat. Wrath asked, stiffly, whether the clinic ever needed someone who could set a bone without magic, and Mau said he would ask Maomao, and Wrath nodded and did not say anything else, but her bandaged hands unclenched a little on her knees. Lust said nothing at all, but at one point, when Mau was talking about Kessoku's song in the snow, she looked out from behind Wrath's shoulder for a whole minute before she remembered to hide again.

Satella poured his tea, every time his cup was empty, before he noticed it was empty.

At the end of the hour, exactly at the end, the first witch closed her notebook and stood.

"Next week," she said.

"Next week," said Mau.

At the door, Echidna stopped, and turned, and looked back at the side door, where Nokotan had gone out, with an expression of deep, private, unresolved frustration.

"When she leaves," said Echidna, "tell me. I wish to know *how*."

"I don't think anyone's going to know how," said Mau.

"That," said Echidna, with great feeling, "is exactly what I am afraid of."

She went out into the snow.

In the family corridor, Frieren was still sitting on the floor with her back against the wall and her grimoire open on her knees, on the same page.

"How was it?" she said.

Mau sat down on the floor beside her.

"Echidna met Nokotan," he said.

Frieren closed her grimoire.

"Tell me everything," she said.
