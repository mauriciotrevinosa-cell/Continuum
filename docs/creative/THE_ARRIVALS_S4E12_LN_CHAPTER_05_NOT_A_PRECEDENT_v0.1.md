On the fifty-seventh morning, Nokotan came to breakfast, said *looked, quiet* along with everyone else, ate three bowls of porridge and a great many crackers, and then put down her spoon and said:

"I have to go home today."

The long table went quiet.

It was a different quiet from the others. Not the quiet of the List, or the night of rule five, or the voice. It was the quiet of a house that has got used to something in a week and has just been told it is ending. Across the table, the girl with the bean, who had been allowed in for breakfast because she had ridden a deer to the door and refused to get off, put down her own spoon and stared.

"Home," said Fern.

"Home," said Nokotan. "My world. I have school." She thought about it. "I've probably missed a lot of school."

"You can *do* that?"

It was Umaru who said it, but it was what everyone was thinking. Mau could see it go round the table, face to face. *You can do that.* The house had stood at a door for ten days, every one of them, and looked through it, and turned around. Some people had gone through, at sunset, at the bridge, and were gone. The rest had stayed, and the door had closed. Return had been a thing that happened once, for everyone, with a Goddess and a window and a price. It was not a thing that happened on an ordinary Tuesday after porridge.

"I think so," said Nokotan. She did not seem to find this remarkable. "Coming here was an accident. I fell in a hole. But I can go back. Once." She held up one finger, very seriously. "Only once. I can feel it. It's like a door I'm holding." She looked round the table. "If I don't go now, I don't think I'll be able to later."

"Can you take someone?" said Umaru. "With you? Just to see? And then come back?"

Nokotan looked at her for a long moment. Her face was kind.

"No," she said. "It's only big enough for me."

Umaru looked down at her porridge.



Fern stood up.

She did not have to. Nobody had asked her to. But she stood at the head of the long table, with the ledger open in front of her, and looked at the whole house, and Mau understood what she was going to say before she said it, because he had seen the same thing go round the table that she had.

"Listen," said Fern.

Everyone listened.

"What she can do," said Fern, "is hers." She looked at Nokotan, and then back at the table. "She fell in a hole that was shaped like a deer. She can walk through a line that turned back something from the hills. Her bag doesn't empty. Her antlers come off." Her voice was very level. "Nothing about her works the way anything else works. Ciel can't read her. Echidna can't read her. Construction tried, and got more questions than it went in with."

She paused.

"So nobody here is going to look at her going home and think there's a door," said Fern. "There isn't. Not for us. Not for anyone in this room. What we chose at the bridge, we chose. It cost what it cost." Her eyes went, very briefly, down the table to Mau, and away. "This doesn't change it. This is hers. It's not a precedent."

There was a silence.

Then Seiko, at the far end, snapped her fan shut.

"Well said," said Seiko.

Fern sat down, very pink, and wrote something in the ledger.



They walked her to the north field. All of them.

It was not planned. It was simply that when Nokotan got up from the table and went to the door with her paper bag, the whole house got up too, and followed her out, and the refuge children came out of the cottages, and the washing court came out with wet hands, and the deer came up from the plaza and the yard and the hay at the bottom of the slope, all hundred of them, and fell in around her. It became a procession. Down the plaza road, past the washing court, past the slope, past the last cottage, out into the north field, toward the place where the tracks had once turned.

Echidna was waiting there.

Nobody had told her it was today. But she was standing at the edge of the field in her black robe, in the snow, with her hands folded and her eyes fixed on Nokotan with the expression of a scholar who has been waiting a long time to watch something happen and is determined not to miss a single detail.

"I asked him to tell me," she said, when Mau looked at her. "He did not tell me. I came anyway."

"You felt it?"

"I felt a door," said Echidna. "Very small. Very silly. Opening." She did not look away from Nokotan. "I wish to see how."



Nokotan stopped in the middle of the north field, with the deer around her.

She turned round and looked back at all of them. The house, and the settlement, and the children, and the Muslin with its fort's flag on a broom handle, small in the distance. She looked at it all for a long moment with the same pleasant interest she had looked at everything with for a week.

"Thank you," she said. "For the soup. And the crackers." She looked at Seiko, in her armchair, which somebody had carried all the way out into the snow. "Especially the crackers."

Seiko inclined her head very slightly.

"If I ever come back," said Nokotan, "will you have some ready? Proper ones?"

"They'll be ready," said Seiko.

"Promise?"

"I don't make promises," said Seiko. "I make crackers." She opened her fan. "They'll be ready."

Nokotan smiled.

Then she reached up and took off her antlers.

Both of them. *Click. Click.* She held them in her hands for a moment, the great branching antlers, the colour of old wood. Then she turned them upside down.

Crackers fell out.

Not a few. A great many. A cascade of round pale crackers poured out of the hollow bases of the antlers, far more than could possibly have fitted inside them, and kept pouring, and piled up in the snow at her feet. A drift of them. A small mountain. They kept coming until the pile was waist-high and the deer were edging toward it hopefully. Then the stream slowed, and stopped, and one last cracker dropped out of the left antler and rolled down the side of the pile into the snow.

Nokotan put her antlers back on. *Click. Click.*

"For the deer," she said. "And for you. In case I take a long time."

There was a silence over the north field.

Then Karane, from somewhere near the back of the house, said, in a voice that cracked completely:

"*What the hell? How is that even POSSIBLE?*"

Nobody answered her. Nobody could.



Nokotan turned round and walked away across the north field, toward the hills, at an ordinary unhurried pace, eating a cracker.

About thirty paces out, she stopped.

She looked down at the snow in front of her feet. Mau, watching, saw something in the snow there that had not been there a moment before. A hole. A perfectly clean-edged hole in the snow, going down into the dark, in the exact shape of a deer: four legs, a neck, a head, two branching antlers.

Nokotan looked at it with mild satisfaction.

Then she turned and waved, once, to all of them.

And stepped in.

She did not fall, exactly. She went down into the hole the way water goes down a drain: suddenly, and completely, with a faint sound like a cork coming out of a bottle. The hole closed behind her at once, smoothly, as if it had never been there. The snow was smooth and white and unbroken.

She was gone.

The deer stood in the field for a moment, looking at the place where she had been. Then, all together, as if someone had told them to, they turned and went back toward the forest, at a walk, unhurried. A few of them stopped by the pile of crackers first. A few of them stayed by the hay at the bottom of the slope, and would still be there in the spring.

Echidna stood at the edge of the field and stared at the smooth unbroken snow where the hole had been.

She did not move for a long time.

"Well?" said Mau, finally, gently.

Echidna did not answer at once.

"I have kept every book I have ever read," she said at last, very quietly. "For four hundred years. In a hundred worlds." She was still staring at the snow. "None of them prepared me for a hole shaped like a deer."

She turned and walked back toward the forest without another word. Mau watched her go. Her shoulders, under the black robe, were very straight, in the particular way of someone who is going to go home and read every book she owns looking for something she already knows she will not find.



At the long table that night, Fern read the ledger.

She read it all. Day fifty-seven. Arrival House opened at the eighth bell. Intake, none. Guests, none.

"Departures," said Fern. "One." A pause. "Deer."

She wrote it. And then, under it, in small square capitals, so that anyone who ever read that page would see it, she wrote two more words. She read those aloud too.

"*Not a precedent.*"

Nobody said anything.

Rimuru, by the cold hearth, had been quiet all evening. Now his eyes went somewhere else, the way they went when Ciel was speaking, and stayed there for a while, and came back.

"She says," said Rimuru, "that Construction looked again. At the end. At the hole." He paused. "She says it came back with more questions than ever, and that she has told it, permanently, to stop." He looked down the table at Mau. "And she says that whatever you are thinking of, you are absolutely not to try it."

"I wasn't going to," said Mau.

*He was,* said Ciel, to two heads.

"I *wasn't*—"

At the far end of the bench, Yuta put down his spoon.

He had not said a word about the deer all week. Not one. Not when the hundred of them came down the plaza road, not when Nokotan asked Mau if he was the human who talked to deer, not when Frieren said *maybe it followed you home* again on the slope. He had split wood, and eaten his porridge, and said nothing at all.

He looked down the long table at Mau now.

"Fine," said Yuta. "You were right about the deer."

The table went quiet.

Mau looked at him.

"Say it again," said Mau.

"No," said Yuta, and picked up his spoon, and went back to his porridge.
