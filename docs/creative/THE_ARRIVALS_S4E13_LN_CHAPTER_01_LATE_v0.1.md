The southern merchant was late.

He came up the bridge road on the fifty-ninth day, a full week after he should have come. Mikasa saw him first, from the north end of the plaza, where she had taken to standing for an hour every afternoon since the voice. A cart, a mule, a man walking beside the mule, coming slowly up out of the river valley between the white fields. Not the merchant's usual pace. Slower. The cart was riding wrong, low at one corner.

She went down the road to meet him. Eren went with her, without being asked.

When they got close enough to see, Mikasa stopped.

The cart had been hit by something.

One back wheel was splintered and lashed together with rope. The side boards on the left were stove in, cracked through in three places, in a pattern that was not an accident or a rock or a ditch. Something had struck the cart from the side, hard, and gone away again. There were long scores in the wood, four of them, parallel, a hand's width apart, as if something had raked it.

And there was a second man walking with the merchant. Younger. With a spear.

"You hired a guard," said Mikasa.

The merchant looked at her. He was a stout, cheerful man, usually. He had come up from the south every two weeks for a year, with cocoa and needles and salt and gossip, and had never once looked frightened. He looked frightened now.

"Things on the road," he said. "That weren't there before."



They brought him into the Muslin and sat him at the long table and gave him soup, and the house gathered round to listen, the way it had gathered round Nokotan a week before. But it was a different kind of listening.

"Three days out of the river towns," said the merchant, with both hands round the bowl. "On the south road. Where it goes through the low woods by the ford." He drank some soup. "Something came out of the trees. At dusk. Big." He held his hand up, high, higher than his own head. "Like a bear, but not. Too many legs. It went for the mule. I threw a sack of salt at it, God knows why, and it stopped, and looked at me, and went back into the trees." He put the bowl down. "Never seen anything like it. Twenty years on that road. Never anything like it."

Nobody said anything.

"The river towns are talking," said the merchant. "There've been others. A farmer on the east side lost three sheep to something nobody saw. Lights in the hills. A boy swears he saw a thing like a man made of shadows standing at the end of his field, just standing, three nights running." He looked round the table. "And the voice. Everyone heard the voice. *The world is evolving.* Everyone in the river towns heard it, same night as you. They don't know what it means. They're scared." He paused. "Some of them are saying it's your fault."

The table went very quiet.

"Not you," said the merchant quickly, looking at Mau, and then away. "I mean. The settlement. The monster town. Some of them are saying that since your Goddess came, and your Trial, and your coins, everything's gone strange." He shrugged unhappily. "I don't believe it. I've been coming here a year. You lot are the only people on my route who pay on time and never water the soup." He looked at Momo. "But I'm one man. And they're scared."

Anko had her notebook out.

She did not write anything for a long moment. Then she turned to a fresh page, and wrote a heading across the top of it, in capitals, and turned it round so that the table could see.

*SIGHTINGS.*

Under it she wrote the merchant's bear-that-was-not-a-bear. The farmer's three sheep. The lights in the hills. The shadow man at the end of the boy's field. Four lines.

Then she went to the evidence room and pinned the page to the wall, beside the rumours, and the contract, and Senku's QUIET tally, and stood back and looked at it.



That evening the house talked about it at the long table, the way the house talked about everything.

It went round. It always went round. Mikasa said they should double the north watch. Eren said they should stop sending Searchers on the long routes entirely. Anko said she wanted a second page ready for the sightings, because she did not think four lines was going to last. Himmel said, quietly, that he had been watching the hills for two weeks, since the day of the old inn's roof, and that he had seen lights too, twice, and had not said anything because he had not been sure. Senku said that whatever was in the low woods by the ford had not come through the line, and would not, if Anko's tracks in the north field meant what they seemed to mean. Rimuru said that the line covered the settlement but not the road, and that the merchant had come up the road.

Fern, at the head of the table, with the ledger, said nothing for a long time.

Then she wrote one line, and read it out.

"*Arrivals, zero,*" said Fern. "Fifty-nine days." She looked up. "Nobody's come over the bridge since the window. Not one. Not the second community, not the river towns, not travellers. The merchant came because we pay him. Nobody else comes because nobody else is coming." She put the pen down. "Arrival House has had one guest in two months, and she was a deer."

"So what do we do?" said Kita.

Fern looked at her for a long moment.

And then she did something Mau had never seen her do. She closed the ledger.

"Nothing," said Fern. "Tonight, nothing."

The table stared at her.

"It's a problem," said Fern. "It's a real problem. It's going to be a bigger problem. Anko's going to need more pages." She looked round the table, at all of them. "But it's a problem for later. Not for tonight." She folded her hands on the closed ledger. "We had a Trial. We had ten days. We had a month of ugly days and snow and chocolate and a war with a catapult. We had a hundred deer." The corner of her mouth moved, very slightly. "And nobody's hurt. And nobody's hungry. And there's a line in the snow that keeps the bears-that-aren't-bears out."

She looked down the table, at Mau.

"We're a family before we're anything else," said Fern. "Tonight we're going to eat dinner. Tomorrow, we can be afraid of the road."

Nobody said anything.

Then Seiko, from her armchair, snapped her fan shut.

"Eat," said Seiko.

And they ate.



Later, after the merchant had been given a bed at Arrival House, which made Jinshi very happy, and his guard had been given a bed beside him, which made Jinshi happier, and his mule had been given a stall and a great deal of hay and one of Nokotan's crackers from the pile that was still in the north field, Mau went out to the yard.

He stood by the ink tree in the dark.

Behind him, the Muslin was lit and loud. In front of him, past the plaza and the last cottages and the white north field, the road went away into the dark toward the river, toward the low woods by the ford, where something too big with too many legs had come out of the trees at dusk and looked at a man who threw salt at it.

*Looked,* said Ciel, very quietly, to one head. *Quiet.*

*Busy,* said Ciel. *Seven days.*

He stood by the tree for a while.

Then Frieren came out with two cups of tea and stood beside him, and gave him one, and they looked at the dark road together without saying anything. After a while she leaned her shoulder against his. He put his arm round her. Behind them, through the common-room window, someone started playing the song from the snow, slowly, on a guitar.

Tomorrow, they could be afraid of the road.
