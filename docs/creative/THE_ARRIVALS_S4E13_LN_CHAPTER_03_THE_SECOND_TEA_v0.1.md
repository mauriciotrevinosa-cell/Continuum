At the end of the first tea, as the seven were going out into the snow, the first witch had stopped at the door.

"The one who hides," she had said, to Mau, "would like the girl with the guitar to come next week. To the tea." She had looked at him with her hands folded. "If the girl wishes. Only if she wishes."

Mau had looked past her at Lust, half behind Wrath's shoulder in the doorway, who had gone scarlet and looked at the snow.

"I'll ask her," he said.

He asked her that night, in the music room, with the stove going on the other side of the buffer wall. Bocchi was sitting on the floor with her guitar and the page of words she would not show anyone. When he told her, she went white, and then very still, and then said, in a voice like a door creaking, "*Me?*"

"Only if you want."

"Why *me?*"

"I think," said Mau, "she heard about the song in the snow. And she hides behind people. And she thought you might understand."

Bocchi looked at her guitar for a very long time.

"I'll think about it," she said, which in Bocchi meant *no*, and then, three days later, at breakfast, without looking up from her porridge, she said, "I'll go," which in Bocchi meant something so brave that Kita had to leave the table.



So on the sixty-first day, at the agreed bell, there were nine at the long table.

The seven. Mau. And Bocchi, at the end of the bench beside Mau, with her guitar case on her knees like a shield, so pale she looked translucent, staring at the tabletop.

The seven looked at her.

It was the first time anyone but Mau had sat at the tea. The house had known about it for three days. The fort had held an emergency session about it. Kita had walked Bocchi to the door of the common room and then gone and sat in the family corridor next to Frieren, the two of them on the floor against the wall, neither reading anything.

"Welcome," said the first witch.

Bocchi made a sound.

"She means thank you," said Mau.

"I know what she means," said the first witch, with the small pleased smile. "I have sat across from someone who could not speak before."

She looked, very briefly, at Lust.



Mau asked about the timber first, because it was his to ask, and because it gave everyone something to look at that was not Bocchi.

"The forest," he said. "East. Where you live. We're going to build a house, and we need timber, and the Searchers need to know if they can cut there." He looked round the table. "It's yours. We'll ask first. Every time."

The seven were quiet.

"It is not ours," said Echidna at last. "We live in it. That is not the same."

"Then whose is it?"

"The trees'," said Echidna, as if this were obvious. "We have lived among them a long time. We know which ones are old." She folded her hands. "Ask the trees."

"How do I ask the trees?"

On the bench, without opening her eyes, Sekhmet said, very slowly: "Take the dead ones."

Everyone looked at her.

"Take the ones that have fallen," said Sekhmet. Every word cost her something, and she paid it anyway. "And the ones that are dying. Standing dead. They are tired. They would like to be useful." A long pause. "Leave the old ones. The big ones, by the cabin. They have been there since before." Another pause. "Since before us."

She stopped. She seemed to have used up a great deal.

"Charcoal from the deadfall," said Daphne, beside the bread, "yes. That is good. It feeds the forest floor less, but the floor has plenty." She took a piece of bread. "You will need a great deal of charcoal. For the lime. For the iron." She ate the bread. "I will show your Searchers which deadfall."

"Thank you," said Mau.

"It is a conversation," said the first witch. "You asked. We answered." The small smile. "That is how it is meant to go."



After that, the tea went on, the way it had gone the first week: ordinary things, quietly. Daphne talked about the barn. Wrath asked whether Maomao had said anything about bones. Maomao had said *I'll think about it*, which, Mau told her, in Maomao meant yes. Echidna asked Mau whether there had been any further word about the hole in the north field, and visibly did not enjoy the answer, which was no.

And all the while, at the end of the bench, Bocchi sat with her guitar case on her knees and did not speak, and on the other side of the table, half behind Wrath's shoulder, Lust sat and did not speak either.

They did not look at each other.

They very carefully did not look at each other. Mau could feel it. It was like sitting at a table between two closed doors, each of which was waiting to see whether the other would open first.

Half the hour went by like that.

Then Bocchi, without looking up, without saying anything to anyone, very slowly opened her guitar case.

The table went quiet.

She took out the guitar. She did not tune it. She held it on her knee under the edge of the table, where almost nobody could see it, and looked at the tabletop, and with one finger she played three notes.

The same three. Very quietly. The ones from the refuge, from the night of Last Defense, from the snow.

She played them again.

Behind Wrath's shoulder, something moved.

Lust looked out. Just her eyes, at first, over the edge of Wrath's bandaged arm. Then, very slowly, more of her. Her face. Her shoulders. She looked at Bocchi, at the end of the bench, playing three notes under the table with her eyes on the wood, and something in her face changed. Recognition, Mau thought. Not of the notes. Of the girl.

Bocchi played them a third time.

Lust came out from behind Wrath completely. She sat at the table, visible, all of her, for the first time at any tea, with her hands knotted in her lap. She looked at Bocchi.

Bocchi, very slowly, looked up.

They looked at each other across the long table, two people who had spent their whole lives hiding behind things.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Then Bocchi, in a voice so small it was barely there, said: "Sorry."

Lust blinked.

"I don't know why I'm saying sorry," said Bocchi, to the table. "I always say sorry. It's—" She stopped. "It's what I say instead of *hello*."

There was a long pause.

Then Lust said, in a voice almost as small, almost exactly the same: "Me too."

Bocchi's eyes went very wide.

"I hide," said Lust, very quietly. She was looking at her hands. "Behind her. Behind bookcases. Behind anything." She glanced, very briefly, up at Wrath, who did not move, but whose bandaged hand had come to rest on the bench beside Lust's, not touching. "People look at me, and I want to be somewhere else. So I go there." A pause. "Inside."

"Me too," said Bocchi, in a whisper.

"But you played," said Lust. "In the snow. In front of thirty people. Eleven times." She looked up at Bocchi. "I heard. Everyone in the forest heard. The sound came all the way." Her voice shook. "How?"

Bocchi looked at her guitar.

"Someone held my hand," she said. "And said three notes. And I played them." She swallowed. "And then I just—kept going. Until it was the song."

Lust was quiet for a long time.

"Carmilla," she said.

Bocchi blinked.

"My name," said Lust. Her face was scarlet. She did not hide. "Carmilla. I wanted—" She stopped. "I wanted you to have it. First."

Bocchi held her guitar very tightly.

"Hitori," she said, after a moment, in the smallest voice yet. "Gotoh Hitori. But everyone calls me Bocchi. Because it means *alone*." She looked at the tabletop. "I'm not, anymore. But they still call me it."

Carmilla looked at her across the table.

Then, very slightly, so slightly that only Mau and Wrath and the first witch saw it, she smiled.



At the end of the hour, exactly at the end, the first witch made a mark in her notebook.

"Two," she said.

The seven went out into the snow. At the door, Carmilla stopped. She did not hide behind Wrath. She turned round and looked back at the long table, at Bocchi, still sitting at the end of the bench with her guitar on her knee.

She raised one hand, very slightly. Not a wave. Less than a wave.

Bocchi raised hers back.

Then the door closed.

In the family corridor, Kita stood up so fast she nearly fell over.

"Well?" she said. "*Well?*"

Bocchi came down the corridor with her guitar case held against her chest, very pale, very quiet. She stopped in front of Kita and Frieren.

"I said sorry," said Bocchi.

"Oh no," said Kita.

"She said *me too*," said Bocchi.

Kita stared at her.

Then Bocchi's face did something it almost never did, which was smile without being afraid of it, and she said, "I made a friend," in a voice of total astonishment, and Kita put her arms round her, guitar case and all, and held on, and Frieren, on the floor against the wall, closed her grimoire and looked up at Mau over the top of Kita's shoulder with an expression he would remember for a long time.
