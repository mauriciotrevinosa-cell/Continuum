Daphne took over the barn on the sixty-second day, and by the sixty-third, Fern was afraid of her.

Not in a bad way. Fern said so, afterward, at the long table, very carefully, because she did not want anyone to misunderstand. It was the way you are afraid of a person who is better at the thing you are best at than you are.

It started with the count.

The barn by the kitchen had been Fern's for a year. She had counted it every week since the Muslin was built: every sack of flour, every barrel of dried peas, every string of onions hanging from the rafters, every crock of Seiko's pickles in the cool corner. She knew to the handful what the settlement had and what it ate and how long it would last. It was written in the back pages of the ledger in a table so neat that Senku had once asked to frame it.

Daphne went into the barn at dawn on the sixty-second day with nothing in her hands, and came out at noon, and found Fern at the long table, and said: "You are eleven sacks of oats short of what your ledger says."

Fern looked up.

"That's not possible," she said. "I counted four days ago."

"Mice," said Daphne. "In the north corner, behind the peas. A family of them. They have been eating very well since before the snow." She sat down across from Fern. "Also, three of your flour sacks are damp at the bottom. They will spoil in two weeks. Use them first." She took a piece of bread from the basket. "Also, the onions on the east beam are sprouting, because the east beam is warm from the kitchen chimney on the other side of the wall. Move them to the west beam." She ate the bread. "Also, there is a better place to put the pickles."

Fern stared at her.

"How long were you in there?"

"Six hours," said Daphne. "I counted everything. Twice." She looked at Fern with her soft, serious face. "You count very well. Better than anyone I have ever known. But you count what is written down. I count what is there." She took another piece of bread. "It is a different thing. I learned it a long time ago. When I was trying to feed a great many people with nothing, you learn to count what is really there."

Fern was quiet for a long moment.

Then she turned the ledger round, opened it to the back pages, to the beautiful neat table, and pushed it across to Daphne.

"Show me," said Fern. "Where the mice are."



Within days the barn was a different place.

The mice were gone. Daphne did not kill them. She found where they came in, and blocked it with a stone, and carried the whole family out to the hay at the bottom of the slope, where Nokotan's deer had left a great deal of spilled grain, and told them, in a perfectly serious voice, that this was their barn now. The refuge children watched her do it. After that, they followed her everywhere.

The damp sacks were used first. The onions were moved. The pickles went to a corner under the floor that Daphne found by tapping the boards, which stayed exactly as cold in winter as it would in summer. Fern wrote it all down. Daphne did not write anything. She did not need to. She kept the whole barn in her head, every sack and string and crock, and could tell you at any hour of the day exactly what was in it and how long it would last.

"Forty-one days," she said, at the long table, when Fern asked. "For everyone here. At what you eat now. If nobody new comes." She paused. "If the merchant does not come again, thirty-eight. If Milim keeps eating three bowls of porridge, thirty-six."

"*Hey,*" said Milim.

"It is not a criticism," said Daphne. "It is a count." She looked at Milim with great gentleness. "Eat your porridge. I will make there be more."

"How?" said Senku.

Daphne turned and looked at him.

"Glass," she said.



Senku's glass had been a sore point for a year.

He had made it months ago, in the little furnace behind the workshop. Clear, mostly. Flat, mostly. Good enough for a few windows, and the lens for Suika's glasses, and the projector he had been promising Marin for the movie room. Not good enough for anything big, because there was not enough sand, and not enough charcoal, and not enough time.

"A greenhouse," said Daphne.

Senku's face did something complicated.

"I know what a greenhouse is," he said. "I've wanted to build one since the old inn. I worked it out. It's not the glass. It's the scale. A greenhouse big enough to matter needs more glass than I can make in a year with the sand we've got."

"The Searchers are bringing sand," said Daphne. "From the bend in the river. I have read your board."

"Not enough."

"Then a small one," said Daphne. "To start. Against the south wall of the barn, where the sun is. Just big enough for seedlings. Wheat and peas and greens, started under glass in the cold, so that when the ground thaws they go out already growing, weeks early." She folded her hands. "Weeks early is the difference between a hungry spring and a fed one."

Senku looked at her for a long moment.

"You've done this before," he said.

"I have done everything before," said Daphne. "Most of it badly." She looked down at her hands. "In my world, I made creatures. To feed people. Great beasts that would grow and grow and feed whole villages. I thought it was the cleverest thing anyone had ever done." She paused. "They ate the villages."

The long table went very quiet.

"So I do not make creatures anymore," said Daphne. "I grow things. Slowly. Under glass. In the ground. The way things are meant to grow." She looked up at Senku. "It is less clever. But nobody gets eaten."

Senku was quiet for a while.

Then he took out his notebook, and turned to a clean page, and wrote at the top of it, in small square capitals, *GREENHOUSE (SMALL)*.

"South wall of the barn," he said. "I'll need the measurements."

"I have them," said Daphne. "I measured it this morning. Twice."

Senku stared at her.

Then he laughed, a short surprised bark, and said, "Ten billion percent," and pushed the notebook across the table to her, and she began to write in it, in a soft round hand that was nothing like his, and he leaned over to watch, and they did not look up from it for the rest of the evening.



By the sixty-fourth day, there was a small glass house against the south wall of the barn.

It was not much. A frame of Wakana's timber, a dozen panes of Senku's glass, a door that stuck. Inside it, in long shallow trays of soil that Daphne had chosen herself from three places in the settlement, there were rows of seeds. Wheat. Peas. Something leafy that Momo wanted for the kitchen. Something Maomao wanted for the clinic, which nobody else was allowed to touch.

The refuge children watered it every morning, under Daphne's eye. The girl with the bean was in charge of the watering can. She took the job with total seriousness.

On the sixty-sixth morning, Mau went past the greenhouse on his way to the washing court and saw Daphne kneeling inside it, on the floor, in the steam, looking at one of the trays.

He stopped.

She was looking at a seedling. One. The first green thread of something, pushing up out of the black soil in the warm wet air under the glass, in the middle of winter, with snow on the roof above it.

Daphne was looking at it the way Frieren looked at the ink tree.

She did not see him. He did not go in. He stood outside the glass in the cold with his basket on his hip and watched a woman who had once made beasts that ate villages kneel on the floor of a small greenhouse and look at a single green shoot as if it were the most important thing she had ever done.

Then he went on to the washing court, and wrung his shirts the right way, and did not tell anyone, because it was not his.
