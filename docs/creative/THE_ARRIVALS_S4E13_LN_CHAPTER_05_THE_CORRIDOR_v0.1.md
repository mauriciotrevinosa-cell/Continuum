## Chapter 10 — The Corridor

**Reading order:** 10

Qifrey was afraid of corridors.

He had told Coco so, at the atelier door, on the night of the contract, when she asked: *terrified.* Then he had said *no* to her face, because she was looking at him with her whole heart and he could not bear to be the reason she did not go. Now, on the morning of the sixty-third day, he stood at the door of a small dark cabin in the old forest, with snow on his shoulders and Coco's hand in his, and looked through the door at the back of its ordinary front room, at the corridor of books going on into a soft warm light much further than a cabin could go.

"It's just a hallway," said Coco.

"It's an impossible hallway."

"Stark sat down," said Coco. "When he came with Mau. He had to put his head between his knees." She squeezed his hand. "You don't have to sit down."

"I may sit down," said Qifrey.

Echidna came down the corridor to meet them, in her black robe, with her milk-white hair, and looked at the two of them in her doorway with that sharp, total interest.

"The one who draws," she said, to Coco. "And her teacher, who is afraid of my corridor."

Qifrey's face did not change. But his hand tightened on Coco's.

"Come," said Echidna, and turned, and went back down the corridor, and they followed.



It went on for a long time.

Coco had been told that. Mau had said it, and Fern had said it, and Stark had said it with his face going slightly green. But being told was not the same as walking it. The shelves went up out of sight on either side, floor to ceiling, packed with books. Some of them were bound in leather and some in wood and some in things Coco did not want to think about. Some of the titles were in languages she knew, and some in languages she almost knew, and some were not in letters at all but in shapes that moved slightly when she looked at them directly. The light came from nowhere. It was warm, and smelled of old paper and dust and something like rain.

Qifrey walked very straight and very carefully and did not look at the shelves.

"How many?" said Coco, halfway.

"I have never counted," said Echidna, ahead of them. "Counting them would be a kind of ending. I prefer not to end things."

They walked.

At last Echidna stopped, at a place where the corridor widened slightly into an alcove with a reading desk and two chairs and a lamp that also did not need oil. She took down a book from a high shelf without looking, the way you take down a cup from a shelf in your own kitchen, and set it on the desk.

It was thin. Bound in something pale and cold, like stone. On the cover, in silver, there was a single glyph.

Coco knew it. She had drawn it eighty-one times in water on the atelier floor.

Not the same. The opposite. Her lattice, the six-sided one with every line opening the other way, was the *undoing*. This was the binding. The one in the crystalline glyph book Qifrey had lent her. The one she had been reading for a month. The one that turned a living thing into crystal.

"Sit," said Echidna.

Coco sat. Qifrey stood behind her chair with one hand on the back of it.

"This," said Echidna, "is the oldest book I have on the undoing of bindings. It was written by a woman in a world I will not name, who spent her life trying to unmake one particular spell, which had been cast on someone she loved." She opened it. "She did not succeed. But she got closer than anyone I have ever read."

Coco looked down at the page.

She could not read it. It was in a language made of shapes. But there were glyphs drawn in the margins, in a fine hand, in faded ink. Glyphs she knew. Her lattice was there, on the third page, drawn small, almost exactly as she had drawn it in water. And beside it, a second lattice, nested inside the first. Smaller. More complicated. Lines inside lines inside lines, folding inward toward a centre that was left blank.

"What's that?" said Coco. "In the middle. Why is it empty?"

Echidna looked at the page for a long time.

"That," she said, "is what she never found."



She read it to them.

Slowly, translating as she went, in a quiet precise voice, with one finger on the line. The woman in the other world had understood the binding completely. She had worked out the undoing completely: the outer lattice, every line reversed, every angle opening instead of closing. Coco's lattice. She had drawn it, and drawn it, and tested it on stone and on glass and on water frozen into the shape of a bird. And it had worked on stone, and glass, and the frozen bird. It had not worked on the person.

"Because the person was alive," said Echidna. "Inside. Still. In a way the stone was not." She turned a page. "The binding does not only turn the outside to crystal. It turns the inside. Every part. And it does it in a particular order, a particular *shape*, which is different for every living thing, because every living thing is shaped differently inside." She tapped the empty centre of the nested lattice. "The outer undoing reverses the binding in general. But to reverse it in a *person*, you must know the shape it took inside that person. Exactly. The way it moved through them. The order. Otherwise you undo it in the wrong order, and—" She stopped.

"And what?" said Coco.

Echidna did not answer at once.

"And the woman in the other world tried it anyway," she said at last. "On the person she loved. With the centre left empty." She closed the book, gently. "That is where the book ends."

The alcove was very quiet.

Qifrey's hand had tightened on the back of Coco's chair until his knuckles were white.

"So you'd have to know," said Coco slowly. "From inside. How it went. In her."

"Yes."

"How do you know that? How does anyone know that?"

"You would have to be inside it," said Echidna. "In the crystal. While it happened. And come back out. And remember." She looked at Coco steadily. "Nobody has ever been inside the binding and come back. That is what it is for."



Coco did not cry.

She sat at the reading desk in the corridor of impossible books and looked at the closed pale cover with its silver glyph, and she did not cry. Qifrey would remember that, afterward. He had seen her cry over a great many things: a spilled inkpot, a broken nib, a glyph that would not close. He had seen her cry on the night of the ten days, in the fort, holding her coin. She did not cry now.

"Then I keep looking," said Coco.

Echidna looked at her.

"There's a centre," said Coco. "It's empty. That means something goes there. It's not *nothing*. It's just something nobody's found." She put her hand flat on the cover of the book. "So I keep looking."

"It may not exist."

"Then I'll find out it doesn't exist," said Coco. "That's still finding out." She looked up at Echidna. "Mau told me what you asked him. For the morning spell. *Tell me something I don't know.*" She swallowed. "What's the price? For reading this?"

Echidna looked at her for a long time.

"Tell me," she said, "something that I do not know."

Coco was quiet.

Then she said it. Quietly. To the book, not to Echidna, the way Mau had told the woman in black about waiting.

"It was me," said Coco. "Who did it. To her." Her voice did not shake. "I drew a spell I wasn't supposed to draw. I didn't know what it was. I was a little girl and I thought it would be beautiful. And she was standing in the middle of it when it closed." She looked at the silver glyph. "Everyone says it wasn't my fault. Qifrey says it. Agott says it. Mau says it." She was very still. "But it was my hand. And nobody can say it wasn't my hand."

Behind her, Qifrey had closed his eyes.

Echidna did not say anything for a very long time.

"No," she said at last. "I did not know that." She looked at the girl at her reading desk. "I have read a great deal about bindings. I have read about the ones who cast them, and the ones who suffered them, and the ones who tried to undo them." She paused. "I have never read the account of a child who cast one by accident on her mother, and then grew up, and came to my corridor to find the way back." She put one finger on the book's pale cover. "That is not in any of my books."

"Is that enough?" said Coco. "For the price?"

Echidna looked at her.

"It is more than enough," she said. "Come back as often as you like. Read anything. The corridor is yours." She glanced up at Qifrey, behind the chair, with his eyes still closed. "Bring your teacher. He is less afraid than he was when he came in."

Qifrey opened his eyes.

"I am exactly as afraid," he said.

"No," said Echidna, with the faint half-smile. "You have stopped looking at the floor."



They walked back down the corridor in the late afternoon, Coco in front with the pale book held against her chest, which Echidna had let her take, which Echidna had never let anyone take. Qifrey walked behind her.

At the door of the cabin, before they stepped out into the snow, Coco stopped.

"Qifrey," she said, without turning round.

"Yes."

"The centre. *From inside.* Somebody would have to be in the crystal and come back." She was looking out at the snow, at the old trees, at the path home. "And remember the shape."

"Yes."

"Is there anyone," said Coco slowly, "who can go somewhere nobody's ever come back from, and come back, and remember?"

Qifrey did not answer.

He did not have to. They both knew. There was a man in the settlement who had died four hundred and thirty-seven times in a white place nobody else could enter, and had come back every time, and remembered.

Coco did not say his name.

She held the pale book against her chest and walked out into the snow, toward home, and Qifrey followed her, with her coin in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left side, over his heart. Neither of them spoke the whole way back.

But that night, in the Witch Hat room, by the light of a candle, Coco took out her private notebook and turned to the page where she had drawn the lattice. Under *For WHEN there's ink*, she drew, very carefully, a second, smaller lattice nested inside the first, lines inside lines folding inward.

She left the centre empty.

Then, beside it, very small, she wrote one word, and looked at it for a long time, and did not cross it out.

*Mau?*
