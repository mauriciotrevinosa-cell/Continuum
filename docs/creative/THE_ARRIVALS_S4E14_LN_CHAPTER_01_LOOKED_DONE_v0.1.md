# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 22
## Chapter 11 — Looked. Done.

**Reading order:** 11

On the morning of the sixty-sixth day, at the hour, before Frieren's foot found his shin, a voice came to two heads.

*Looked,* said Ciel. *Quiet.*

A pause.

*Done.*

Mau opened his eyes in the dark.

In the loft above the closet, he heard Rimuru sit up. Not slowly, the way Rimuru usually woke, flowing from slime shape into something with elbows over the course of several minutes. All at once. A sharp rustle, and then silence, the silence of someone listening very hard.

*Done,* said Ciel again. And then, with something in her voice that Mau had heard only twice before, once about morale and once about a man who never said *I'm fine*: *It is good. I made it good.*

Frieren's foot found his shin. *One. Still here.*

"She's finished," said Mau, very quietly, into the dark.

Frieren was quiet for a moment.

"The thing she was busy with," she said.

"Yes."

"Is it good?"

"She says it's good." He lay still. "She says she made it good."

Above them, in the loft, Rimuru said, very softly, to nobody, in a voice Mau had never heard him use: "Thank you."



At breakfast, the whole house knew something had happened before anyone said anything.

It was in Rimuru's face. He sat by the cold hearth in his place and did not eat, and kept looking at Wakana's plan on the wall, at the drawing of the real house with its dotted wings and its tunnels and its room with no name, the way you look at a letter you have been waiting for and have not opened yet.

He cleared his throat.

The table turned.

"She says," said Rimuru, "*looked, quiet.*"

Senku's chalk squeaked.

"And she says," said Rimuru, and stopped. He looked down the long table at all of them. "She says *done*."

Nobody moved.

"Done what?" said Milim.

"The thing she's been busy with," said Rimuru. "For a month. Since story night." He looked at Wakana's plan again. "The thing that goes under walls."

Wakana, at the far end of the bench, put his cup down very slowly.

"Tell us," said Fern. Her pen was already moving.



So Rimuru told them. Not all at once. He had to keep stopping, to listen, and then say it again in words people could follow, because Ciel was explaining it the way Ciel explained everything, precisely and at length and in a vocabulary nobody else at the table had.

"It's called Foundation," said Rimuru. "That's what she's calling it. That's what it is." He put his hands flat on the table. "It came from what I told you on story night. About building. About the ground first, and the water, and the drains, and doing it in stages. It was sitting inside me, she says. Since I came here. Since before. It had nowhere to go." He paused. "When I told you, it found somewhere to go."

"What does it do?" said Senku.

"It shapes the ground," said Rimuru. "Once."

The table went very quiet.

"Once," said Fern.

"Once," said Rimuru. "It's single use. When it's done, it's gone. She can't make another one. I can't learn it again. It's like—" He looked for the word. "It's like a breath. You take one, you let it out. That's it."

"What can one breath do?" said Mikasa.

Rimuru listened.

"Three things," he said. "All at once. In one act." He held up one finger. "The ground. Earth and stone. She can level it, cut it, raise it, hollow it. Foundations. A cellar. Tunnels. Drains." A second finger. "The water. The river, where it passes the settlement. She can shape it. A weir. A channel. A millrace, if Senku wants one."

Senku made a sound.

"And the area," said Rimuru. "Everything inside the line. And a little beyond it, to reach the river." He paused. "Everything I can see from the highest point in the settlement, at once, standing still."

"And the third thing?" said Fern.

Rimuru did not answer at once.

He was listening to Ciel. His face had changed. Mau could hear her too, and understood why.

*Tell them,* said Ciel, *that the third was not in it.*

"She says," said Rimuru slowly, "that the third thing wasn't in it. When it appeared. On story night." He swallowed. "It was ground and water. That was all. Crude. She said she could make it better if I gave her a month." He looked down the table. "She spent the month making it better. More precise. So it could do the ground and the water in the exact shapes we want, and not just roughly." He paused. "And she spent most of the month on something else."

*Tell them,* said Ciel.

"She added a third thing," said Rimuru. "On her own. Nobody asked her." He looked down the long table, past Senku, past Wakana, past Fern with her pen stopped, to the end where the Witch Hat girls were sitting together, with Qifrey. "One living thing. Inside the area. She can make it grow." A pause. "Years of growing. All at once."

The table was silent.

At the end of the bench, Coco had gone white.

"She says," said Rimuru, very quietly, "that she listened to Agott count the ink. In the atelier. On the thirtieth. *Two winters, maybe three.*" He looked at Agott. "She says she heard the number on the back of your hand, and what it meant. That one day the light over the scratches would go out, and the four of you would stop being what you are." He paused. "She says she did not think that was acceptable."

Agott was staring at him.

"She was looking at the guardrails," said Agott. "Rule four. That's all she's supposed to look at."

*I was looking at the guardrails,* said Ciel, to two heads, with immense dignity. *The atelier is three doors from the room with the east window. Agott counts aloud.*

"She says," said Rimuru, "that you count very loudly."

Agott opened her mouth. Nothing came out.

Beside her, Tetia had put both hands over her face. Richeh, who was a guest, and allowed to say things, said, "Oh," very softly, the way she had said it the first time she saw the row of bottles on the atelier floor.

Qifrey had not moved at all.

And Coco, at the end of the bench, very pale, was looking past all of them, through the window, at the yard, where a small thin tree with ten silver-green leaves stood in a ring of stakes in the snow.



Fern wrote it down.

She wrote it in the ledger, on a clean page, in her small square hand, under a new heading. *FOUNDATION.* Then the three things. *Ground. Water. One living thing.* Then, after a moment, *Once.*

She looked up.

"One breath," said Fern. "Three things. Everything inside the line." She looked round the table. "And everyone at this table is about to want something different from it."

Nobody laughed. It was not a joke.

"So," said Fern. "Tomorrow. The table." She closed the ledger. "Everyone brings what they want. On a slip of paper. One each." She looked at Agott, and at Coco, and then down the table at the Witch Hat end. "And the rule is Qifrey's. From the ink. Nobody defends their own slip."

Qifrey looked up.

"You remember that," he said.

"I remember everything," said Fern. "It's my job."



That night Mau could not sleep.

He lay in the room with the east window with Frieren asleep beside him and looked at the ceiling, and thought about the ink tree. Ten leaves. A hundred years. *Ninety-nine years and some months to go.* He had said that to her, in the yard, in the autumn, and she had called him an idiot, and her hovering hand had finally held on.

*Both of us. A hundred years. The tree. I'm going to be there.*

He had promised her that. On the reversion night. On the worst night of both their lives.

"Ciel," he said, very softly, into the dark.

*Yes.*

"The third thing. The living thing." He swallowed. "You did it for the ink. For them."

*Yes.*

"Only for the ink?"

There was a long pause. Longer than Ciel usually left.

*Rule four,* said Ciel at last. *I look at the guardrails. I tell you every time I look.* Another pause. *I did not look at anything else.*

"Ciel."

*The room with the east window,* said Ciel, *looks onto the yard.* A pause. *Frieren also speaks aloud. To the tree. When she thinks nobody is listening.*

Mau lay very still in the dark.

*I did not look,* said Ciel. *I heard.* And then, very quietly, so quietly he almost did not catch it: *Good night, Mau.*

She did not say anything else.

Beside him, in her sleep, Frieren's cold foot found his shin.
