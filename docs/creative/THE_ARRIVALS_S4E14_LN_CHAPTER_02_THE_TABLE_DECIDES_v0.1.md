## Chapter 12 — The Table Decides

The slips went into Momo's second-best pot.

That was Fern's idea. Everyone who wanted something from Foundation wrote it on a slip of paper, one slip each, folded once, and dropped it into the pot on the long table on the morning of the sixty-seventh day. Then Fern took them out one at a time and read them aloud, and laid them out on the table in a long row, the way Qifrey had once laid out the bottles of ink on the atelier floor.

And nobody defended their own.

That was Qifrey's rule. Fern had remembered it. Every slip had to be argued for by somebody else, or it was not argued for at all.

There were eight slips.



The first was Daphne's. She had written it in her soft round hand, very small.

*Water for the fields. Channels from the river to the south and east, where the ground is good. So that in spring nothing has to be carried.*

Senku defended it. He stood up with his notebook, and talked for four minutes about the volume of water a spring wheat field needed per day, and the number of people it would take to carry it in buckets, and the number of days in a growing season. Then he sat down and said, "Ten billion percent," and Daphne looked at the table and did not say anything, but her ears had gone pink.

The second was Senku's.

*A weir across the river below the bridge. A millrace. Enough drop to turn a wheel.*

Wakana defended it. He talked about saws. About a wheel that could turn a saw all day without anyone's arms, and cut timber in an hour that took a team a day by hand. About a wheel that could work bellows for the forge, and a grindstone, and a stamp for the clay. "Everything we build after this," said Wakana, "we build faster, if there's a wheel."

"And someday," said Senku, very quietly, not quite defending his own slip, "lights."

Nobody said anything to that. But Rimuru, by the cold hearth, looked at Senku for a long moment with an expression Mau could not read.

The third was Mikasa's.

*A floor under the house. A cellar under the whole of it. And from the cellar, two ways out under every section. One toward the refuge. One away.*

She had drawn it on the plan in the autumn, on the sixth evening, and Senku had said *that's a lot of digging* and *years of it*, and then taken the pencil and drawn the first tunnel himself. Eren defended it. He stood at the window and did not turn round.

"Exits before rooms," said Eren. "She said that. I said *good*." He paused. "Still good."

He sat down. Mikasa did not look at him. But she did not look away either.

The fourth was Wakana's. *Foundations. Level ground under the plan, cut to stone, so that what we build on it stands for a hundred years.* Rimuru defended it, which made Wakana go red. Rimuru said that in his whole career he had never once been given ground that was already level, and that if Wakana got that, he would be the luckiest builder in any world.

The fifth was Rimuru's own. Nobody knew it was his until Fern read it, and recognized the handwriting, and looked up at him. *Drains. Three times as wide as they need to be. Because the house will grow.* Wakana defended it. He said that a man who had built a nation had learned the hard way what happens when you make the drains too small, and that they should listen.

The sixth was Maomao's. *A well. Deep. Clean. Near the clinic.* Kusuri defended it, at length, with statistics about fever.

The seventh was Umaru's.

It was in crayon, with the Ministry's seal.

*A BATH. A BIG ONE. WITH HOT WATER. LIKE IN RIMURU'S STORIES ABOUT HIS COUNTRY.*

There was a pause around the table.

"That's not a ground thing," said Agott.

"It's water," said Umaru, from the fort.

"It's *luxury*."

"It's *health*," said Umaru, with enormous dignity. "Maomao says warm water is good for joints. Seiko has joints."

Seiko, in her armchair, did not deny it.

Rimuru, by the cold hearth, had gone very still. He was listening.

"Ciel says," he said slowly, "that there's warm water. Under the north side of the settlement. Deep. Very deep. Warm from the ground." He paused. "She says the ground part can reach it. If someone wants it to." He looked at Umaru, and something came into his face that was almost pain and almost joy. "A hot spring. Like Tempest."

The fort exploded.

Hakari defended the bath. Then Karane. Then, unexpectedly, Maomao, who said that she had been treating chilblains all winter and a bath would put her out of half her work, and that she would like that very much.



And then the last slip.

Fern took it out of the pot and unfolded it and looked at it, and her face changed slightly.

It was not signed. But it was in a hand everyone in the house knew, because it was the hand that labelled every bottle on the atelier floor. Small and careful and very precise, the hand of a girl who drew glyphs.

*The tree.*

That was all it said.

Nobody spoke.

Coco, at the end of the bench, was looking at the table, not at anyone.

"Who defends it?" said Fern quietly.

Silence.

Then, at the end of the bench, a chair scraped.

Agott stood up.

She stood up in the middle of the common room, the way she had stood up on the atelier floor at the ink budget, with her arms folded and her face set in its usual expression of mild contempt for everyone present. On the back of her hand, in ink, there was a number, and under it a newer number, and the older one was crossed out.

"It's the most expensive thing on this table," said Agott. "It's the only living thing. There's only one breath and only one living thing, and if we spend it on the tree, nobody gets anything else to grow. Not the fields. Not the greenhouse. Not anything." She paused. "If it were my slip I'd say no."

She looked down at the back of her hand.

"It isn't my slip," said Agott. "It isn't even Coco's, really. It's everyone's. Every person in this house walks under the light over the scratches every night, and doesn't think about it. And every night it costs a little, out of a bottle that's nearly empty." She held up her hand, with the numbers on it, so that the whole table could see. "Two winters. Maybe three. And then the light goes out, and the four of us—" She stopped. Her chin had come up. "And then the four of us are just girls," said Agott, very steadily, "in a strange world, with a teacher who can't teach us anything."

The table was very quiet.

"The tree is ink," said Agott. "That's all it is. Ink for the light. Ink for the warming glyph under the kitchen that we said no to. Ink for Senku's drainage glyph that Richeh's drawn four hundred times in water." She looked at Coco. "Ink for whatever she's been drawing at night in her private notebook that she thinks nobody knows about."

Coco looked up, very fast.

"Grow the tree," said Agott, "and we're witches again. For good. Not for two winters." She unfolded her arms. "That's the argument."

She sat down.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Coco was looking at Agott as if she had never seen her before.

"What," said Agott.

"Nothing," said Coco. "Nothing." And then, in a much smaller voice: "*Again*."

"I'm not doing it a third time," said Agott, and looked at the ceiling.



Fern looked down the long row of slips on the table. Eight of them. Water for the fields. A weir. Tunnels. Foundations. Drains. A well. A bath. And the tree.

"Rimuru," she said. "Can it do all of it?"

Rimuru listened for a long time.

"She says yes," he said at last. "All of it. In one breath." He swallowed. "If I hold the shape. Exactly. Every channel and every tunnel and every drain and the weir and the spring and the tree, all at once, in my head, standing still, from the highest point." He looked at the row of slips. "She says it's a lot. She says I'll sleep for a day afterward. Maybe two." A pause. "She says it's what she made it for."

"Then all of it," said Fern.

She wrote it in the ledger.

"And the tree," said Mau.

Everyone looked at him. It was the first thing he had said all morning.

"I'm not defending it," he said. "It's not my slip. I just—" He stopped. "I wanted to say it out loud. That it's on the list. The tree."

Beside him, Frieren had not said anything all morning either. She had sat through all eight slips with her hands in her lap and her face very still. She did not say anything now.

But under the edge of the table, where nobody else could see, her hand found his sleeve, and held on.

"Day seventy," said Rimuru. "Three days. To draw it. Every line." He looked at Wakana and Senku and Mikasa and Daphne. "I need it exact. All of you. On one sheet. So I can hold it."

Wakana was already unpinning the plan from the wall.
