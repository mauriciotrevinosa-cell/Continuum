## Chapter 13 — The Third Tea

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1

**Continuity state:** Day 68 | Wrath invites Maomao | 'I hate it when people are hurt'; she healed because she could not bear it | no spells here, but hands and bones | Maomao examines her badly set knuckles: 'I mend everyone' | 'Minerva.' | 'Three.' | the first witch: next week is the last; 'Ask me next week, and I will tell you what I can'

At the end of the second tea, as the seven went out into the snow, Wrath had stopped at the door.

She did not say anything at first. She stood there with her bandaged hands clenched at her sides and her jaw set, the way she always stood, as though braced against a blow nobody was about to throw. Then she said, very stiffly, to a point somewhere over Mau's left shoulder:

"The one who mends. In your clinic."

"Maomao."

"She said she would think about it. The bones." Wrath's jaw worked. "I would like her to come. Next week. If she wishes." A pause. "I would like to ask her myself."

So on the sixty-eighth day there were nine at the long table again: the seven, and Mau, and Maomao.

Maomao came exactly at the bell, with her apron on and her little notebook in its pocket, and sat down at the end of the bench beside Mau with the air of a woman attending a mildly interesting medical consultation, and folded her hands on the table, and looked at the seven with frank professional interest.

The seven looked back.

"So," said Maomao. "Bones."



It was not, Mau thought, a conversation he could have predicted.

Wrath did not speak at first. She sat very upright, with her bandaged fists on her knees, and looked at Maomao, and her face worked, as though there were a great many words behind it and she could not find the door they were supposed to come out of. The first witch watched her with the small pleased patience she gave all of them. Carmilla, beside Wrath, did not hide. She sat in the open now, visible, and put one hand very lightly on Wrath's arm.

"I hate it," said Wrath at last.

Maomao waited.

"When people are hurt," said Wrath. Her voice was low and rough and shook with something held back. "I hate it. I have always hated it. More than anything. More than any of the things they call me for." She looked down at her own bandaged hands. "When someone is hurt in front of me, I want to tear the world open and shake it until it stops."

"That's not a very clinical attitude," said Maomao.

"No," said Wrath.

"It's a very common one," said Maomao. "Among people who end up working in clinics." She took out her notebook. "Go on."

Wrath looked at her for a long moment.

"In my world," she said, "I could mend. With my hands. Very fast. I struck a wound, and it closed." Her fists tightened on her knees. "People thought it was strange. That a woman named for anger should heal. They did not understand that it is the same thing. I healed because I could not *bear* it. Every wound I closed was me shaking the world until it stopped."

"And here?" said Maomao.

"Here we have signed," said Wrath. "No spells. Not on anyone in the settlement. Not ever." Her jaw set. "I agreed. I meant it. It is right. Your house has been hurt by things it did not choose, and should not have to trust a stranger's magic in its bones." She looked at Maomao. "But I still have hands. I know bones. I know how they break, and how they set, and how they knit. I have known for a very long time." Her voice dropped. "And when your settlement was hurt, at the Last Defense, I stood at the edge of the forest and felt it, and I could not come. I did not have the right." She stopped. "I would like the right."

Maomao wrote something in her notebook.



"Show me your hands," said Maomao.

Wrath hesitated. Then she held them out across the table, fists still clenched.

"Open them."

Wrath opened them. Slowly. The bandages were old and grey and wound tight around every knuckle. Under them, where the fingers showed, the skin was rough and scarred, a lattice of old marks.

Maomao took one of the hands in both of hers, without asking, the way she took the hands of every patient who sat on the end of her examination table, and turned it over, and looked at it. She pressed the joints, one by one. She bent each finger, gently, and watched Wrath's face as she did it.

"You've broken every one of these," said Maomao.

"Many times."

"Hitting things."

"Hitting wounds," said Wrath. "To close them."

"And they healed crooked, because you never let anyone set them, because you were too busy setting everyone else's." Maomao turned the hand over again. "Typical."

Wrath said nothing. But something in her face, very slightly, gave.

Maomao let go of the hand.

"I'll take you," she said. "Two days a week. The clinic. You set bones and hold people down while I stitch and carry anyone who can't walk." She closed the notebook. "No hitting."

"No hitting," Wrath agreed.

"And you let me look at those hands properly. In the clinic. With the light." Maomao looked at her. "Several of those knuckles were never set right, and I can't fix all of them, but I can fix some. And I'm going to."

Wrath stared at her.

"You would mend *me*," she said.

"I mend everyone," said Maomao. "It's not personal." She picked up her tea. "It's very irritating, actually. I'd much rather be doing chemistry."

There was a pause at the long table.

Then, from the other end, the first witch made a sound that Mau had never heard her make, a small dry sound that might have been a laugh. And Wrath, sitting very upright with her scarred open hands on the table in front of her, looked at Maomao, and her face did something it had perhaps not done in a very long time. It eased. All of it. The jaw, the brow, the set of the mouth. As if someone had finally let her put down a weight she had been carrying since before any of them were born.

"Minerva," she said.

Maomao looked up.

"My name," said Wrath. Her voice was rough. "Minerva." She looked at her hands. "I wanted the one who mends to have it."

Maomao looked at her for a long moment over the rim of her cup.

"Minerva," she said. "Two days a week. Bring your hands."

She drank her tea.



At the end of the hour, exactly at the end, the first witch made a mark in her notebook.

"Three," she said.

Then, as the seven rose, she paused, and looked at Mau across the long table with an expression he had not seen on her before. It was not the small pleased smile. It was something quieter.

"Next week," she said, "it is mine."

"You said yours was last."

"Mine is last," said the first witch. "Next week is the last." She folded her hands. "Satella gave hers to you. Echidna to the girl who draws. Daphne to your scientist and your ledger. Sekhmet, Carmilla, Minerva, each to the one who opened a door." A pause. "That is six."

"And you're seven."

"I am seven," said the first witch.

She looked at him for a long moment, with her hands folded.

"Ask me next week," she said, "and I will tell you what I can."

She turned and followed the others out into the snow.

Maomao, at the end of the bench, finished her tea, and put down her cup, and took out her notebook, and wrote something in it. Mau did not ask what. But when she closed it, he saw that she had written, at the top of a fresh page, in her small neat hand: *M. Hands. Thursday.*

And under it, smaller, after a moment's thought, she had added: *(temporary).*

Then she crossed out *(temporary)*, and went back to the clinic.
