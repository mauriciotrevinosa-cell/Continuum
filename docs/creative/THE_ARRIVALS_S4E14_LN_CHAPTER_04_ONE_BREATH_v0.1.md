The highest point in the settlement was the bell tower over Arrival House.

It was not very high. A square wooden tower with a pointed roof and a single bell, built by Wakana when Arrival House went up, so that it could ring the hours and the intake bells and, when it had to, the alarm. A ladder went up inside it to a small platform under the bell, with a waist-high rail on four sides and a view, on a clear day, of everything inside the line and a little beyond: the plaza, the Muslin, the yard and the ink tree, the washing court and the slope, the refuge cottages, the white fields north and south and east, and the river, where it came down out of the hills and bent past the settlement and went on under the bridge toward the south.

On the morning of the seventieth day, Rimuru climbed it.

Mau climbed it behind him.

Nobody had asked him to. But there were two faces, and this was something the faces did together, and Rimuru had looked at him at breakfast and said nothing, and Mau had understood, and got his coat.

At the top, Rimuru unrolled the sheet.

It had taken three days. Wakana and Senku and Mikasa and Daphne, and then Maomao and Umaru and Coco, and then half the house, crowded round the long table with pencils, drawing every line on one enormous sheet of Senku's best paper pinned flat with four of Momo's pots. The plan of the real house, with its foundations and its cellar and Mikasa's tunnels running out from under every section to two exits each. Rimuru's drains, three times too wide. The weir across the river, and the millrace cut along the bank. Daphne's channels lacing out to the south and east fields. Maomao's well, beside the clinic. A circle on the north side, where Ciel said the warm water was, deep down. And in the middle of the yard, a small careful drawing of a tree.

Rimuru held the sheet open against the wind and looked at it for a long time.

Then he looked out over the rail, at the real thing.

"I have to hold all of it," he said. "At once. Every line. Exactly where it is on the paper, exactly where it is out there." He was very calm. "She says if I lose a line, it goes wherever it goes."

"You won't lose a line," said Mau.

Rimuru looked at him.

"You built a country," said Mau. "From drains."

Rimuru laughed. It was short and surprised, and some of the tension went out of his shoulders.

"From drains," he agreed.



Down below, the settlement was waiting.

Everyone was outside. They had been told to be. *Not on the lines*, Fern had said, at breakfast, reading from the ledger. *Not inside the house plan. Not on the riverbank. Not in the north circle. Not within ten paces of the tree.* So they stood in the spaces between the lines, in the snow, in the cold bright morning, in clusters. The whole house. The refuge families. The washing court. The cooper, with his hat in his hands. Jinshi and the staff of Arrival House at the foot of the tower. The southern merchant and his guard, who had stayed on, at the edge of the plaza with their mouths open.

Mau could see all of them from the top of the tower.

Daphne, at the edge of the south field, alone, with her hands folded, looking at the ground where the channels would be. Senku and Suika on the riverbank, well back from the edge, Senku with his notebook open and his pencil not moving. Mikasa at the corner of the house plan, where the first tunnel would begin, with Eren beside her. Wakana in the middle of the plan itself, which was not allowed, until Fern went and got him by the sleeve and walked him out. Maomao by the clinic, with Minerva, both of them looking at the place where the well would be. Umaru on the north side, in her hood and her sash, standing at the very edge of the circle, with the whole fort behind her.

And in the yard, ten paces from the ink tree, in a ring, the Witch Hat girls and Qifrey. Coco in front, with her hands clasped. Agott with her arms folded. Tetia, and Richeh. Qifrey a little behind them.

And beside them, very still, with her grimoire held against her chest, Frieren.

She looked up at the tower.

Mau looked down at her.

He could not see her face from that far. He did not need to.



"Ciel," said Rimuru.

*Ready,* said Ciel. Her voice was very calm and very clear, to two heads. *I have the shape. Hold it with me. Every line. Do not let go until I say.*

Rimuru closed his eyes.

Mau watched him do it. He watched Rimuru stand at the rail of the bell tower with the sheet open in both hands and his eyes closed, holding a house and a river and a field and a spring and a tree in his head, all at once, exactly. His face went very still. His hands on the paper stopped trembling. His breathing slowed.

*Three,* said Ciel.

The wind dropped.

Mau felt it. The whole settlement felt it. The wind that had been blowing steadily from the north all morning simply stopped, and the air went still and heavy and expectant, the way air goes before a storm.

*Two.*

The bell above their heads began, very faintly, to hum.

*One.*

Rimuru breathed in.

And let it out.



It did not happen all at once. That was the first thing Mau understood. It happened in a single breath, but a breath has a shape, and the shape went outward from the tower in every direction at once, like a ripple from a stone.

First the ground.

It began under the house plan. A sound came up out of the earth, low and deep, below hearing almost, more felt than heard, a long held note like a cello string drawn very slowly. The snow on the plan began to move. It slid off the ground in sheets, all of it at once, as if the earth underneath had tilted, and then it did tilt. The whole site of the real house settled, slowly, a hand's breadth, into perfect level. The ground under the snow was no longer earth. It was stone. Pale grey stone, smooth and flat, cut in the exact outline of the plan, every wall and every room, footings rising a hand's height out of it in clean straight lines where the walls would stand.

Wakana, at the edge of the site, made a sound like someone being hit.

Then the cellar. A great rectangle of the stone floor, the whole footprint of the house, sank. Slowly, smoothly, the way water drains out of a basin, a full storey down into the ground. The walls of the hole were stone, smooth and dressed, as if a mason had spent ten years on them. Steps cut themselves into one corner, going down.

And from the floor of the cellar, the tunnels ran out.

Mau could see them from the tower. Not the tunnels themselves, but the snow above them. Long straight lines in the white fields, sinking very slightly as the earth underneath was hollowed out, running away from the house site in every direction Mikasa had drawn. Two from under every section. One toward the refuge. One away. And at the end of each line, where the ground rose, at the foot of a slope or the side of the hill or the back of the washing court, the snow fell inward suddenly and there was a doorway, a dark stone arch in the white, opening onto a passage that went down into the dark.

At the corner of the house plan, Mikasa had taken one step forward without meaning to. Eren's hand was on her arm.

Then the drains, under all of it, under the plaza and the paths and the yard. Mau could not see those at all. He only felt them, in the long low note coming up through the boards of the tower: something running under everything, wide and deep, three times wider than it needed to be.



Then the water.

At the river, below the bridge, the ice cracked.

The sound came up the valley like a gunshot, and then another, and then a long tearing roar as the river's skin of ice split from bank to bank. Senku, on the bank, had grabbed Suika by the back of her coat. Below the ice, out of the dark water, stone was rising. A long low wall of it, grey and dressed and smooth, coming up out of the riverbed straight across the channel from one bank to the other, pushing the broken ice aside. It rose until it stood a man's height above the old water line. And the river, stopped, piled up behind it, black and swirling, and rose, and rose, and then spilled over the top of the new weir in a long smooth glassy fall, and roared.

And along the near bank, a channel cut itself. The millrace. A straight stone-lined cut running alongside the river from above the weir to below it, so that some of the piled water turned aside into it and ran down it fast and deep, toward the place where Senku had drawn a small careful circle and written, in small square capitals, *WHEEL*.

Senku was standing on the riverbank with his notebook in one hand and Suika's coat in the other, and he was not writing anything at all.

Then the channels. From the millrace, and from above the weir, water went out into the fields, south and east. It went out in narrow stone-edged channels that cut themselves through the snow in long branching lines, the way frost grows across a window, exactly where Daphne had drawn them. The water steamed as it ran, black against the white, in the cold.

At the edge of the south field, Daphne had sat down in the snow.

Then the north side. The circle. Umaru, at its edge, in her hood and her sash, took a step back as the ground in the middle of it opened. A shaft, round and deep, going straight down into the dark, with a sound like something very far below drawing breath. And out of the shaft, steam. A great slow column of it, white and thick, rising into the still cold air, and under the steam, coming up the shaft from somewhere very deep, a smell Mau did not know. Mineral and warm and faintly of eggs.

Then water. Warm water. Rising up the shaft and spilling over the lip into a wide shallow basin that the ground had shaped around it, steaming, in the middle of the snow.

Umaru stood at the edge of the basin with the whole fort behind her and stared at it.

"Hot," said Umaru, faintly. "It's *hot*."

By the clinic, quietly, a second shaft had opened. A well. Maomao looked down into it for a long time and then, very slowly, smiled.



On the tower, Rimuru was shaking.

Mau saw it start. His hands on the paper, first. Then his arms. Then his whole body, standing at the rail with his eyes closed, holding a house and a river and a field and a spring and a well and every drain under the settlement in his head at once, exactly. His face had gone grey. His breath was coming short.

*Hold,* said Ciel. *Hold. One more.*

Mau put his hand on Rimuru's back.

He did not know why. He did not have anything to give him. He could not hold the shape. But Rimuru's back was shaking under his palm, and he left his hand there, flat, steady, the way Maki had taught him on the night of the talk. *Look at me. Breathe with me.*

Rimuru breathed with him.

*One more,* said Ciel. *The living thing.*

Down in the yard, ten paces from the ink tree, in a ring of witches, Frieren was looking up at the tower.

And the ink tree began to grow.
