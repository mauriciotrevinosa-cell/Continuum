It began with the leaves.

Ten of them. Silver-green, held up out of the snow like small hands, the way they had been all winter. Mau, at the rail of the bell tower with his palm flat on Rimuru's shaking back, saw them tremble. All ten at once, though there was no wind. Then there were eleven. Then fourteen. Then he could not count them, because they were coming too fast: new leaves unfolding out of the bare thin twigs one after another, silver-green, opening like hands.

The trunk moved.

It was a thin thing, the trunk of the ink tree, no thicker than Coco's wrist, in its ring of stakes in the snow. It moved the way a person moves when they wake up and stretch: a long slow shudder from the root to the tip. Then it began to thicken. Mau could see it from the tower, and down in the yard the ring of witches could see it closer. The bark split and spread and grew over itself, darkening from pale green to grey, ridging, roughening, the way a tree's bark changes over years. Wrist-thick. Arm-thick. Thick as a leg. Thick as Mikasa's waist.

The stakes broke.

Wakana's ring of stakes, the ones he had driven into the yard on the day the seed sprouted, so that nobody would build on it, snapped outward one after another with small sharp cracks as the trunk swelled past them. The board on Wakana's stake leaned and fell. WHO WATERS IT. *Everyone.* Three names in charcoal. It fell face-up in the snow at the foot of the tree, and stayed there.

The tree went up.

It went up the way a held breath goes out, all at once and very slowly. The thin top shot upward and divided and divided again, and the divisions spread outward into branches, and the branches put out twigs, and the twigs put out leaves, silver-green, thousands of them now, a great soft roof of them spreading out over the yard. Snow slid off the Muslin's roof where the branches reached it. Snow fell from the branches themselves in long soft curtains as they spread, so that for a moment the whole yard was full of falling white, and in the middle of it the tree was still rising.

Down in the yard, the ring of witches had stepped back. Coco had both hands over her mouth. Agott had unfolded her arms. Tetia was crying. Richeh had sat down in the snow. Qifrey was standing very still with his eyes closed, and his lips were moving, and Mau could not hear what he was saying.

Frieren had not moved.

She stood ten paces from the tree with her grimoire held against her chest, in the falling snow, and looked up at it as it rose, and did not move at all.

*Done,* said Ciel.

The tree stopped.

The bell above their heads stopped humming. The low note coming up through the earth stopped. The wind, which had been holding its breath with the rest of the settlement, came back from the north all at once, cold and ordinary, and moved through the new leaves of the ink tree with a sound like rain.

On the tower, Rimuru let go of the sheet.

It blew away over the rail, out across the plaza, a great white square tumbling end over end in the wind. Nobody tried to catch it.

Rimuru's knees went.

Mau caught him.

He did not fall far. He did not fall the way a person falls. In Mau's arms, all at once, quietly, he stopped being a person. He was a slime. A soft, cool, pale-blue weight, about the size of a large cat, utterly limp, sliding down into the crook of Mau's elbow like water settling in a bowl.

*All of it,* said Ciel. Her voice was very soft. *Every line. Nothing lost.* A pause. *Master is asleep. He will sleep a day. Perhaps two.* Another pause. *He held it. All of it. I did not think anyone could hold all of it.*

Mau stood at the top of the bell tower with Rimuru asleep in his arms and looked out over the settlement.

The stone floor of the real house, level and pale. The cellar, a storey deep. The dark arches of the tunnel exits in the white fields, at the foot of every slope. The weir across the river, roaring. The millrace. The channels lacing out to the south and east, steaming, black against the snow. The column of steam rising from the north side, and Umaru and the whole fort standing at the edge of a basin of hot water in the middle of winter, not moving. The well by the clinic.

And in the middle of the yard, where a thin tree with ten leaves had stood that morning, a tree.

A real one. Tall. Its crown spread over half the yard, silver-green, moving in the wind. Its trunk was thick and grey and ridged, with roots that had pushed up through the snow at its base like the knuckles of a great hand. Under its branches the snow had all fallen away, so that there was a wide round space of bare dark earth, sheltered, dry, under a roof of leaves.

Big enough to sit under.



He carried Rimuru down the ladder very carefully, one-handed, with the slime held against his chest. At the bottom of the tower, Diablo was waiting. He had come across the plaza at a speed that was not quite a run, and he held out his arms, and Mau put Rimuru into them, and Diablo held his master the way you hold something more precious than anything you have ever been given, and did not say *kufufufu*, and did not say anything at all.

"A day," said Mau. "Maybe two. Ciel says."

Diablo bowed his head over the sleeping slime.

"I will be outside his door," he said.

Then Milim was there, landing in the plaza in a spray of snow, and Diablo let her look, and she looked at Rimuru for a long time with an expression of great seriousness, and then said, "He's snoring," which he was, very faintly, a small bubbling sound. And something in the whole plaza let go.



Mau walked to the yard.

He did not hurry. He walked across the plaza in the snow, past people who were laughing and crying and staring at the weir and the steam and the tunnels, past Senku sitting on the riverbank with his face in his hands, past the stone floor of the real house where Wakana was on his knees running his palms over the pale level stone like a man who has been given back something he lost. Around the corner of the Muslin. Into the yard.

The witches had gone.

Not far. Mau could see them at the edge of the yard, by the atelier door, all four of them and Qifrey, standing together, looking at the tree. But they had stepped back from it. All of them. Coco must have done it, he thought. Coco, who saw everything. She had looked at the tree and at the woman standing ten paces from it, and taken Agott's sleeve and Tetia's hand, and pulled them gently back to the atelier door, and Qifrey had followed. The ink could wait until tomorrow.

Frieren was standing under the tree.

She had walked in under the branches, into the wide round space of bare earth where the snow had fallen away. She was standing by the trunk with one hand flat against the bark, looking up into the leaves. Her grimoire was on the ground at her feet. She had dropped it, he thought, without noticing.

He walked in under the branches and stopped beside her.

The leaves moved overhead with their sound like rain. The light came down through them silver-green and soft, and the air under the tree was still and very slightly warmer than the air outside. It smelled of bark, and earth, and something sharp and clean that he did not know, that might have been ink.

Frieren did not look at him.

"You broke your promise," she said.

He did not say anything.

"A hundred years," said Frieren. Her hand was still flat on the bark. "You said. On the worst night. When you didn't know if you'd live through the morning. You said *both of us, a hundred years, the tree, I'm going to be there*." She looked up into the leaves. "You said I couldn't take it back."

"I remember."

"It hasn't even been a year," said Frieren.

"I know."

"Not a hundred years. Not even one." She turned and looked at him at last, and her face was doing something he had never seen it do, which was everything at once. "It came early. It came *ninety-nine years and some months early*. You promised me a hundred years, and it's here, and we're standing under it, and it hasn't even been *one*." Her voice cracked. "So it's broken."

"It's broken," said Mau.

"You broke it."

"Ciel broke it."

"You're responsible for Ciel."

"I'm not responsible for Ciel. Nobody's responsible for Ciel."

"You're *responsible*," said Frieren, and her eyes were wet, and she was almost laughing, "for every promise you make to me. And this one's broken. And you know what that means."

He looked at her.

"What does it mean?"

"It means," said Frieren, "you owe me a new one."

The leaves moved overhead.

"A new promise," said Mau.

"A new one," said Frieren. "Since this one's done. Since it came early. Since you're *standing* here." She took her hand off the bark. "And it has to be longer. It has to be much longer than a hundred years. Because I'm not going to be cheated twice."

"How long?"

Frieren looked at him for a long moment.

"Longer," she said.

"That's not a number."

"It's the only number," said Frieren. "Longer. Longer than a hundred. Longer than whatever you're about to say. Longer than me." Her voice went very quiet. "You said, at the sea, at night, *then I'm going to stand here a long time*. I want that one. Only longer."

He did not say anything for a while.

Then he sat down.

On the bare dark earth under the ink tree, with his back against the trunk, in the soft silver-green light. It was dry, and slightly warm, and the bark was rough against his spine.

He looked up at her.

After a moment, Frieren sat down beside him. Close. Her shoulder against his. Her knees up. She picked up her grimoire from where she had dropped it and held it in her lap and did not open it.

They sat under the tree.

It was the first time anyone ever had.

"Longer," said Mau. "Both of us. I promise."

Frieren leaned her head on his shoulder.

"Idiot," she said.

The leaves moved overhead with their sound like rain. At the atelier door, Coco was crying, and Agott had her arm round her, and was looking very hard at the sky. In the plaza, someone had started laughing and could not stop. On the north side, the whole fort had taken off its shoes and was standing in the hot spring up to its ankles, screaming. In a room in the Muslin, a small blue slime was asleep on a pillow, snoring, with a demon sitting very straight outside the door.

And under the ink tree, on the seventieth day, two people sat with their backs against the trunk and did not move until it was dark.
