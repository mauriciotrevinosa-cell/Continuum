## Chapter 16 — Ink

Qifrey tapped the tree on the morning of the seventy-first day, at dawn, with his four students around him and the rest of the house kept back at the edge of the yard by Fern and a length of rope.

He had a knife for it. A small one, very old, with a handle of dark wood worn smooth, which he had carried across from his own world in a pocket of his coat and never once taken out in front of any of them. He held it now, under the branches of the ink tree, and looked at the trunk for a long time before he did anything.

"Where I come from," said Qifrey, "there was one tree."

His students were very quiet.

"Every drop of ink in our world came from it, or from its seeds. It was very old. Older than anyone. When I was a boy, I was taken to see it." He touched the bark with two fingers. "The man who tapped it let me hold the bowl. I remember the first drop. It came out silver. Then it turned dark in the air." He was quiet for a moment. "I thought I would never see anything so beautiful again."

He did not say anything else about it. His students did not ask. They had learned, over the years, which of Qifrey's doors to knock on and which to leave closed.

He chose a place low on the trunk, on the side that faced the morning, where the bark was smooth between two ridges. He set the point of the knife against it. Then he paused, and turned, and held the knife out, handle first, to Coco.

Coco stared at it.

"It was your request," said Qifrey. "In the atelier. On the floor. *We plan for a hundred years.*" He did not lower the knife. "You should be the one."

Coco took it. Her hands were shaking.

Agott stepped up beside her without a word and put her hand over Coco's on the handle, steadying it. Tetia put her hand on Coco's shoulder. Richeh, who was still a guest, hesitated, and then put her hand over Agott's.

Four hands on one small old knife.

They cut together. A short shallow notch in the bark, no longer than a finger. Qifrey held a small clay bowl beneath it.

For a moment nothing happened.

Then a single drop welled up out of the cut. Silver. Bright silver, like moonlight on water, catching the dawn. It hung at the lip of the notch for a long moment, trembling, and then fell into the bowl.

As it fell, in the air, it turned dark.

Coco made a sound.

Another drop. Silver, then dark. Then another. A slow steady drip, silver in the cut and dark in the bowl, the way Qifrey had remembered it for his whole life.

Qifrey knelt in the snow under the branches with the bowl in both hands and watched it fill, and did not say anything. His face was turned away from his students. But Mau, at the edge of the yard behind Fern's rope, could see his shoulders, and could see that he was crying. Without a sound. The way a man cries who has carried something across a world in a coat pocket and never expected to set it down.



By noon there was a full bottle.

They drew the ink very slowly, Qifrey said, because the tree was young, even if it looked old, and you did not take too much from a young tree all at once. One bottle. They would take one bottle a week, and no more, and the tree would heal over the notch and grow, and in a year they would have more than they could ever use.

One full bottle, on the atelier floor, on the clean cloth, beside the row of the old ones.

Agott counted it.

She counted it the way she counted everything, on the cloth, every bottle and vial in a row. The old ones that were nearly empty. The one at the end that was barely a stain. And the new one, full, dark, catching the light.

Then she took her pen, and looked at the back of her hand, at the two numbers written there. The old total, crossed out, and the newer one under it.

She crossed out the newer one.

Under it, she did not write a number. She wrote a word.

*Enough.*

She looked at it for a long moment. Then she licked her thumb and rubbed out all three: the two numbers and the word. She rubbed until the back of her hand was clean and pink, for the first time since they had come to this world.

"There," said Agott. "I'm not counting anymore."

Coco looked at her.

"You'll count next week," said Coco.

"I'll count next week," Agott admitted. "But I'll count up."



They drew their first glyphs that afternoon.

Not big ones. Qifrey would not allow big ones. The ink was precious, he said, even when there was enough of it. You did not waste a thing just because you were no longer afraid of running out.

Richeh drew first, because she had drawn hers four hundred times in water and could do it in her sleep. Senku's drainage glyph. The one he had asked for at the ink budget, on the atelier floor, and been told was not how glyphs worked, and had argued was how glyphs ought to work. She drew it on a flat stone, small and perfect, and carried it out to the edge of the new drains under the yard, and set it in the earth. It glowed, very faintly, and then went dark.

"What does it do?" said Senku, who had been hovering at the atelier door all afternoon like a man waiting outside a delivery room.

"It tells you," said Richeh, "when the drain's going to fail. Before it fails." She looked at him. "You said that's how glyphs ought to work. I spent months working out how to make it work that way." She went pink. "It turns red. If the water backs up."

Senku looked at the small dark stone in the earth for a long time.

Then he looked at Richeh.

"You're a genius," he said. "Ten billion percent. You're a genius."

Richeh, who was still a guest, and allowed to say things, said, "I know," and then went so red that Tetia had to lead her back inside.

Tetia drew a water circle on the bottom of a barrel, so that it would always be clean. Coco watched her do it with her hands clasped and her face lit up.

Then Agott drew hers.

She went to the kitchen. Nobody had expected that. She walked through the common room with a small brush and a pot of the new ink, and into Momo's kitchen, where Momo was kneading dough at the long table, and knelt down on the floor under the table, on the stone, without asking.

"Agott?" said Momo. "What are you doing?"

"Your request," said Agott, from under the table. "At the ink budget. A warming glyph under the kitchen floor. So the bread would rise in winter." She was drawing as she spoke, small precise lines on the stone. "I defended it. With numbers. Then I voted against it, because bread could rise by the stove." She finished the last line. "That was wrong. Bread rises better from underneath."

She came out from under the table and stood up and dusted off her knees.

The glyph on the floor under the table glowed, faintly, and then went dark. And the stone, under Momo's feet, very gradually, began to be warm.

Momo looked down at the floor.

Then at Agott.

"You remembered," said Momo. "From the ink budget. That was—"

"I remember everything," said Agott. "I just don't usually say."

She went out of the kitchen. Momo stood at the long table with her hands in the dough and the warm floor under her feet and did not say anything for a long time. Then she went back to kneading. That night the bread rose higher than it ever had, and Momo said at dinner, in front of everyone, that it was the best bread she had ever made, and that it was Agott's, and Agott looked at the ceiling and said it was the dough, obviously, and ate three pieces.



Coco did not draw anything that day.

Mau found her in the evening in the atelier, alone, sitting on the floor beside the cloth with the row of bottles on it. She was holding the one at the end. The one that was barely a stain. The one nobody had ever been willing to throw away.

She had the new bottle beside her, open.

He stood in the doorway. She knew he was there. She did not look up.

Very carefully, with a small glass funnel, she poured a little of the new ink into the old bottle. Just a little. Until the stain at the bottom was not a stain anymore. Until the old glass had ink in it again, dark and real, catching the lamplight.

She stoppered it. She set it back at the end of the row.

"There," said Coco.

Then she took out her private notebook, and opened it to the page with the two lattices, the outer one and the smaller one nested inside it, with the empty centre. She looked at it for a long time.

She did not draw it. Not in ink. Not yet. The centre was still empty.

But under *For WHEN there's ink*, she crossed out *WHEN*, and above it, in small careful letters, she wrote:

*Now.*

Then she closed the notebook and looked up at Mau in the doorway, with her face wet and shining.

"We're witches again," said Coco.

"You never stopped," said Mau.

"No," Coco agreed. "But now we don't have to count."
