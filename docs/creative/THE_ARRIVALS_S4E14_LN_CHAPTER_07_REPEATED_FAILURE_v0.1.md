Senku's wheel turned for the first time on the seventy-second day, and by the seventy-third, he was trying to make it do something it was not allowed to do.

He did not know it was not allowed. Nobody did. That came later.

The wheel itself was beautiful. Wakana had built it in two days, out of the deadfall timber Kaneki had been bringing in from the old forest at night with Daphne's blessing: a great undershot wheel, twice as tall as a man, with paddles of split oak, hung on an axle of the hardest wood in the settlement where the millrace ran fast and deep along the bank below the new weir. When they let the water into the race and it hit the paddles, the wheel shuddered, and groaned, and turned.

And kept turning.

The whole house came down to the riverbank to watch it. It did not do anything yet. It simply turned, slowly, steadily, with the water roaring off its paddles in white spray and the axle creaking. Senku stood on the bank and watched it go round, and round, and round, with an expression nobody in the house had ever seen on his face, which was peace.

"Saw," said Wakana, beside him. "Tomorrow. I'll gear it to a saw."

"Saw," Senku agreed.

"Then bellows. For the forge."

"Bellows."

"Then the grindstone. And the clay stamp."

"And then," said Senku, very quietly, watching the wheel go round, "lights."

Wakana looked at him.

"That's a long way off," said Wakana.

"It's not," said Senku. "It's not a long way off. That's the thing." He did not take his eyes off the wheel. "I've got a wheel that turns. I've got copper, a little, from the bog-iron slag. I've got lodestone; Okarun found a lump in the cliff upriver. I've got glass." He was talking faster now, the way he talked when an idea had hold of him. "A wheel turns a magnet inside a coil. The coil makes current. The current goes down a wire to a filament inside a glass bulb, and the filament glows." He turned to Wakana. "That's all a light is. That's all *electricity* is, for that. I could do it in a week."

Wakana did not say anything.

"Light," said Senku. "Real light. In the common room. In Maomao's clinic, so she can stitch at night. In the music room, so Bocchi doesn't have to play in the dark." He looked back at the wheel. "In a week."



It took him three days. He did not get light.

He built the generator on the morning of the seventy-third day, in the workshop, with Suika holding things and the memory of Chrome looking over his shoulder, he said, which nobody understood. A coil of copper wire, wound by hand, very fine, around an iron core. A magnet, cut from Okarun's lodestone, mounted on a shaft that would turn inside the coil. A belt from the wheel's axle to the shaft. Two wires running from the coil to a small glass bulb he had blown himself, with a thread of carbonized bamboo fibre inside it, which he had spent an entire night making.

That evening he connected the belt to the wheel and let the water in.

The wheel turned. The belt ran. The shaft spun. The magnet went round inside the coil.

The bulb did nothing.

Senku checked the wires. The wires were fine. He checked the coil. The coil was fine. He checked the magnet.

The magnet was not a magnet anymore.

He held it up. He put an iron nail against it. The nail fell off. The lump of lodestone that had pulled every nail in the workshop toward it the day before, so hard that Suika had had to pry them off, was now a lump of ordinary grey stone.

"That's not possible," said Senku.



He tried again the next morning.

Okarun went upriver to the cliff and found another lump of lodestone, a bigger one. Senku cut it and mounted it and connected everything again, and checked every joint twice, and let the water in.

The wheel turned. The magnet turned. A spark jumped, once, a tiny blue spark, at the join between the wire and the bulb.

Then the copper wire snapped.

Not at a join. In the middle of the coil, where it had been wound, where there was no strain on it at all. It simply parted, cleanly, as if it had been cut, and the two ends sprang apart, and the spark went out.

Senku rewound the coil. It took him all afternoon. He let the water in.

The wire snapped again. In a different place. Cleanly.

"That's not possible," said Senku.

"You said that yesterday," said Suika.

"It wasn't possible yesterday *either.*"



That afternoon, the belt broke.

That evening, the shaft cracked, along the grain, in a piece of wood Wakana had chosen himself and would have staked his life on.

On the seventy-fifth day, at dawn, everything held. The wheel turned, the belt ran, the magnet spun inside the coil, the wires were whole, the bulb was sealed. For one second, the filament inside the bulb glowed. Faintly. Orange. A tiny worm of light in the dim workshop.

Then the bulb shattered.

Not cracked. Shattered. All at once, into a fine glittering dust that drifted down over the workbench like snow.

Senku stood in the workshop and looked at it for a long time.

Then he sat down on the floor, among the dust, and put his head in his hands.

"Something," he said, through his hands, "is doing this."

Suika, holding the empty bulb socket, did not say anything.

"It's not chance," said Senku. "I've done the probability. Seven failures, seven different components, every one of them sound, every one of them tested. Each one fails in a way that's physically possible but absurdly unlikely. In sequence. At the exact moment it would have worked." He lifted his head. "That's not chance. That's a *pattern*. Something doesn't want this to work."

Rimuru was awake by then. He had slept for a day and a half after Foundation, a small blue slime on a pillow with Diablo outside the door, and woken up ravenous and very slightly smaller, he claimed, than before. He had been watching from the workshop door since he woke, and saying nothing.

Now he came in and sat on an upturned crate across from Senku, among the glittering dust.

"Ciel," said Rimuru.

*Looking,* said Ciel.

There was a long pause.

*There is nothing in the workshop,* said Ciel. *No seam. No system. No hand. Nothing I can read.* Another pause. *But there is a shape. In the failures. It is not chance. Senku is right.* A pause, longer. *It is very large. And very patient. And it is not hostile.*

"Not hostile?" said Rimuru.

*It is not trying to hurt anyone,* said Ciel. *It broke a bulb. It did not break the wheel. It did not break the saw. It did not break the bellows.* A pause. *It broke only the thing that makes light.*



That night, Senku went back to the workshop alone.

He did not try again. He sat at the bench among the glittering dust of the seventh bulb, in the dark, without lighting the lamp, and looked at the ruins of his generator.

"What do you want?" he said, out loud, to the dark workshop. He felt like an idiot. He said it anyway. "I'm not hurting anybody. I'm making *light*. For the clinic. For the kids. What's wrong with light?"

Nothing answered.

He sat there a long time.

Then, from no direction, very quietly, a voice spoke.

He knew it. Everyone in the settlement knew it. They had heard it on the bridge on the tenth day, when every stayer found a coin in their palm, and again on the forty-eighth night, saying four words. It was not loud. It was not soft. It came to the workshop and to nowhere else. Only to him.

Three words.

*Protection. Repeated failure.*

Then nothing.

Senku sat in the dark among the glittering dust for a very long time.



He told the long table at breakfast. Exactly. Word for word. Fern wrote it down.

There was a silence.

"*Protection,*" said Anko slowly. "That's her word. From the Trial. *Settlement protection withdrawn.*"

"Repeated failure," said Fern.

"She's not saying no," said Senku. He had been thinking about it all night. His face was grey with tiredness, and very calm. "If she meant no, she'd have stopped the wheel. Or the weir. Or Foundation. She let Rimuru reshape the whole settlement in one breath. She let us have a hot spring. She let us have a saw." He looked down the table. "She's not saying no. She's saying *not this*. Not yet. Not that fast."

"Why?" said Kita.

Senku did not answer at once.

"Because it's her world," said Rimuru, quietly, from the cold hearth.

Everyone looked at him.

"It's her world," said Rimuru. "Not ours. Not Senku's. Not mine. Every single one of us came here from somewhere else, and every single one of us, the minute we got here, started trying to turn it into where we came from." He looked at Senku. "Senku wants electric light, because that's what light is, where he's from. I wanted roads and hot springs and drains, because that's what a town is, where I'm from. And she let me have most of it." He paused. "But I think there's a line. Somewhere. Past which it stops being her world with us in it, and starts being our world built on top of hers." He looked down at his hands. "I think she's protecting the world. From us. A little. Not because we're bad. Because we're a lot."

The table was very quiet.

"Repeated failure," said Senku, slowly. "Not punishment. Just—failure. Every time. Until I stop."

"Until we go slower," said Rimuru. "Stages." The corner of his mouth moved. "I told you. You build what you need now, and you leave room for what you'll need next. And when the next thing comes, you build that." He looked at Senku. "The wheel turns. The saw saws. The bellows blow. That's now. Light might be next. Or it might be later. Or it might be when she says." He shrugged. "We'll know. Because the bulb won't break."

Senku looked at him for a long time.

Then he laughed. It came out of him tired and cracked and genuinely amused.

"She's rate-limiting me," said Senku. "A goddess is rate-limiting me. For *being too fast*." He put his head down on the table. "That's the most insulting thing that's ever happened to me in two worlds."

"Ten billion percent," said Suika, beside him, and patted his back.



The saw was running by the end of the week. Then the bellows. The wheel turned day and night on the riverbank below the weir, and the workshop rang with it, and timber that would have taken a team a day to cut came off the saw in an hour.

The generator stayed in pieces on a shelf at the back of the workshop, under a cloth.

Senku did not throw it away. Every so often, when nobody was looking, he took the cloth off and looked at it. He did not try again.

But on the shelf beside it, in a small wooden box, very carefully, he had saved one thing. A pinch of glittering dust, from the seventh bulb. The one that had glowed, for one second, orange, in the dim workshop, before it shattered.

He had written a label for the box, in small square capitals.

*NOT YET.*
