# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 22
## Chapter 18 — The Fourth Tea

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 22  
**Reading order:** 18

On the seventy-fifth day, at the agreed bell, there were eight at the long table: the seven, and Mau.

Nobody had been invited this week. The first witch had said, at the end of the third tea, that it would be hers, and the house had understood without being told that this one should be only the eight of them. Bocchi had not asked to come. Maomao had not asked to come. Frieren sat in the family corridor with her back against the wall and her grimoire on her knees, as she always did, and did not read it.

The seven came in out of a thin grey snow and sat down in their places. Satella at Mau's left. Echidna at his right, glancing every so often through the window at the yard, where the ink tree stood with its great silver-green crown, as if it were a book she had not finished. Daphne by the bread. Minerva very upright, with fresh white bandages on her knuckles, put there by Maomao on Thursday. Carmilla beside her, not hiding. Sekhmet on the bench, lying down, eyes closed.

The first witch at the end of the table, facing him, with her hands folded.

Mau poured the tea.

For a long time nobody said anything. It was not uncomfortable. The first teas had been full of things to say: the voice, the deer, the timber, the bones. This one was quiet, the way a room is quiet at the end of a long day when everyone in it knows what everyone else is waiting for.

"You said to ask," said Mau at last.

The first witch inclined her head.

"I said I would tell you what I could."

"Then I'm asking," said Mau. "Who are you?"



The first witch looked at him across the long table for a long time.

She was, he thought, the one he knew best and least of all of them. He had sat across from her every second week for a year in a room at the old inn with a chair that wobbled. She had asked him every question about his seams in a precise, unhurried voice. She had smiled her small pleased smile every time he drew a line and held it. She had stood at the edge of his yard for two days on the night his seams went dark. She had said, at the door, *you owe us two*. She had said *bring someone, it is a long walk*. She had looked at Satella across a table and put a hand over hers.

He did not know her name. He did not know anything about her at all, except that she was never early and never late, and that she folded her hands before she began.

"The others gave their names," said the first witch, "to the one who opened a door for each of them. Satella to you. Echidna to the girl who draws. Daphne to your scientist and your ledger. Sekhmet to the girl with the antlers, I think, though she would not admit it. Carmilla to the girl with the guitar. Minerva to the one who mends." She looked down at her folded hands. "Each of them was given something. A door. A place. A use. And each of them gave back the thing she had kept longest."

"And you?" said Mau.

"I was given a table," said the first witch. "At which nobody interrogates anybody. Where I may ask, and be answered, and be asked." She looked up. "That is a great deal. More than you know."

"Then what's yours?"

The first witch was quiet.

Down the table, Satella had gone very still. So had Echidna. Even Daphne had stopped eating. On the bench, Sekhmet had opened one eye.

"I signed the contract," said the first witch, "as Pride."

"I saw."

"I am not ashamed of it," she said. "Pride is what holds the shape of a thing. It is why I am never early and never late. It is why I fold my hands. It is why I could sit across from a frightened man in a wobbling chair, every second week, for a year, and ask him about the seams that were tearing him apart, and never once let him see that I was afraid for him." She paused. "Pride holds. That is what it is for."

"But it's not your name."

"No."

"Then what is?"

The first witch looked at him for a long moment.

Then the small pleased smile came. But it was different, this time. Gentler. Sadder. The smile of someone looking at something a very long way behind them.

"You may call me Pride," she said. "The name was for someone smaller."



Nobody said anything for a long time.

Mau looked at her across the table. At her folded hands, and her composed face, and her eyes, which were very old and very steady.

*The name was for someone smaller.*

He thought about it. He thought about a girl who had once been small, and had had a name, and had grown into something that held the shape of things, and folded its hands, and was never early and never late. He thought about what you might do with the name of someone you used to be, someone small, who was not you anymore. Whether you would give it away at a table, to a man you had sat across from for a year.

Or whether you would keep it, the way you keep a small thing in a drawer marked *later*.

"Okay," said Mau.

The first witch's eyebrows went up, very slightly.

"Okay?"

"Okay," said Mau. "Pride." He picked up his cup. "If that's what I can call you, that's what I'll call you." He paused. "And if the someone smaller ever wants to come to tea, there's a seat."

The first witch did not say anything.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then, very slowly, for the first time in a year of sessions and four weeks of teas, she unfolded her hands, and laid them flat on the table, open, palms down, on either side of her cup.

It was such a small thing. Mau did not know why it made his throat tight.

Beside him, Satella had put her own hand over her mouth. Across the table, Echidna was looking at the first witch with an expression Mau had never seen on her face, which was something very like tenderness.

"Seven," said the first witch, quietly.

She did not take out her notebook. She did not make a mark. She only said it, to the table, to all of them.

"Seven," said Echidna.

"Seven," said Daphne.

On the bench, without opening her eyes, Sekhmet said, "Seven," very slowly, as if it cost her nothing at all.



After that, they talked about the house.

It was Satella who started it, in her soft, hoarse, unused voice, looking out through the window at the yard, at the ink tree, and past it at the pale stone footings of the real house, level and waiting in the snow.

"Will there be a room?" she said. "In the house. For us." She held her ALWAYS card against her chest. "The contract says. When the house is built."

"There'll be rooms," said Mau. "Seven. In the north wing. Wakana's drawing it." He paused. "Senku has a proposal. Tomorrow. At the long table." He hesitated. "I think it's going to be a lot."

"A lot," said Echidna, with interest.

"He got carried away," said Mau. "After the bulb. He said a goddess was rate-limiting him, so he was going to design the most ambitious house in two worlds out of spite."

Echidna's half-smile came.

"I would like to see that," she said.

"Everyone's going to see it," said Mau. "Tomorrow. Breakfast."

"May we come?"

Mau looked at her. At all of them. At seven women in strange robes around a long table in the common room of the Muslin, in a thin grey snow, asking whether they could come to breakfast.

"You live here," he said. "Almost. You don't have to ask."

There was a pause.

"We will ask anyway," said the first witch. Her hands were still flat on the table, open. "That is the shape of it. We ask. You answer." The small smile. "It is a conversation."



At the end of the hour, exactly at the end, they rose.

At the door, the first witch stopped, as she always did. She turned and looked back at Mau, still sitting at the long table with eight cups.

"Thank you," she said. "For *okay*."

"It wasn't much."

"No," she agreed. "That is why."

She went out into the snow.

In the family corridor, Frieren looked up from the page she had not been reading.

"Well?" she said.

Mau sat down on the floor beside her, with his back against the wall.

"She didn't give me her name," he said.

Frieren looked at him.

"She said I could call her Pride," said Mau. "She said the name was for someone smaller." He was quiet for a moment. "And then she opened her hands."

Frieren closed her grimoire.

She did not ask what that meant. She had been small once herself, a very long time ago, in a village that no longer existed. She had grown into something that suppressed its mana, and never let anyone see what it was afraid of, and held the shape of things for a thousand years.

She leaned her shoulder against his, and they sat in the corridor together and did not say anything until it was dark.
