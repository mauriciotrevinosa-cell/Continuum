# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 22
## Chapter 19 — Concrete and Rebar

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 22  
**Reading order:** 19  
**Continuity state:** Day 76, breakfast, witches knock first | Senku's huge concrete/rebar plan: 'six years, maybe eight' | 'I got carried away' | Rimuru: 'Have you ever seen a temple?'

The witches came to breakfast on the seventy-sixth day. They knocked first.

It caused a small commotion. Nobody had expected them so early, or at all, except Mau, who had forgotten to mention it. They stood in the doorway in a row, seven of them, at the hour when Rimuru was clearing his throat at the cold-hearth end of the table, and the whole common room turned round.

"You said we did not have to ask," said the first witch. "We are asking anyway."

There was a pause.

Then Umaru, from the fort, in her hood and her sash, said: "The Ministry declares breakfast open to Ring Three," and Momo, without a word, began laying seven more places, and the witches came in and sat down at the long table among everyone, and Rimuru said "*looked, quiet*," and Senku's chalk squeaked, and Daphne reached for the bread before anyone else.

Then Senku stood up.

He had a roll of paper under his arm.



It was a very large roll of paper. It was larger than the sheet Rimuru had held on the bell tower for Foundation. It was, Mau estimated, about the size of the long table. Senku carried it to the cleared space in the middle of the common room, between the long table and the fort, and knelt, and unrolled it across the floor, weighting the corners with four of Momo's pots and a sleeping Sekhmet, who had been lying near the hearth and did not wake.

Everyone leaned over to look.

"This," said Senku, "is the house."

Nobody said anything.

It was a drawing of a building. That was the first thing anyone could have said about it, and for a while it was the only thing. It was a drawing of a building so large, so detailed, and so densely labelled in Senku's small square capitals that the paper was more grey than white. There were three floors, and a cellar, and a fourth floor on top that was mostly glass. There was a tower at one corner, with a clock face drawn on it. There was a second tower at the other corner, with a domed roof and a slot in it, and an arrow pointing to the slot that said *TELESCOPE (EVENTUALLY)*.

"Senku," said Fern slowly.

"Let me explain," said Senku.



He explained.

It took forty minutes. Nobody interrupted him, because nobody could find a gap.

The walls, he said, would be concrete. Not mud brick. Not stone and mortar. Concrete, poured, reinforced. He had worked out a recipe from the lime the Searchers were bringing from the north hills and the ash from Kaneki's charcoal and crushed stone from the cliff upriver. It was the same concrete, near enough, that an empire in his world had used to build things that were still standing two thousand years later. And inside the concrete, iron bars. Rebar. Forged from the bog iron, drawn into rods, laid in a grid inside every wall and every floor, so that the whole house would be one solid piece of reinforced stone that could stand through earthquakes and floods and fire and monsters and the end of the world.

"Ten billion percent," said Senku, "it would never fall down."

The windows would be glass. All of them. Every room. Clear glass, made in the new furnace that the wheel's bellows would run, with sand from the river bend. Enough glass, eventually, for the fourth floor, which would be a greenhouse for Daphne, all year round, above everyone's heads.

Daphne, at the long table, had stopped chewing.

The heating would come from the hot spring. Pipes, lead or copper or fired clay, running from the spring on the north side under every floor of the house, so that the water's warmth came up through the stone and nobody would ever be cold again. Hot water in every washroom. A bath on every floor.

"Every *floor*?" said Umaru.

"Every floor," said Senku.

Umaru looked at him with something very close to love.

The clock in the tower would be driven off the waterwheel, by a shaft and a gear train he had already designed. It would ring the hours so that the bell in Arrival House would not have to. The telescope in the dome would be for later. Much later. When he had worked out how to grind a lens that large.

And when the bulb stopped breaking, said Senku, the house would have wiring already laid in the walls. Copper, run through clay conduits inside the concrete. Waiting.

He stopped.

He sat back on his heels at the edge of the enormous drawing, and looked up at all of them, and his face was flushed and tired and blazing.

"That's the house," said Senku.

The common room was completely silent.



Fern was the first to speak. She had the ledger open on her knee and a pen in her hand, and she had not written a single word in forty minutes.

"How long?" she said.

Senku looked at the drawing.

"With the wheel," he said, "and the saw, and the bellows. And every Searcher on materials, every day. And everyone in the house who can lift something." He did the arithmetic. Everyone watched him do it, his lips moving slightly. "Six years."

There was a pause.

"Maybe eight," said Senku.

There was a longer pause.

"For the concrete alone," said Senku, more quietly, "we need about four hundred cartloads of crushed stone from the cliff. And lime from the north hills, where the lights are. And iron for the rebar, which means smelting bog iron, which means charcoal, which means the forest, which means asking the witches every week for more dead trees than the forest probably has." He looked at the drawing. "And glass. A great deal of glass. The fourth floor alone is more glass than I can make in two years." He paused. "And the pipes. And the clock. And—"

He stopped. He looked at his own drawing, spread across the floor of the common room with four pots and a sleeping witch holding down its corners, and seemed to see it for the first time.

"I got carried away," said Senku.

Nobody laughed.

"After the bulb," said Senku. "She said *repeated failure* and I thought, fine, if I can't have light I'll have everything else, I'll design the best house anyone's ever designed in two worlds, and she can rate-limit *that*." He rubbed his face. "I stayed up four nights. I didn't think about whether we could build it. I only thought about whether it would be good."

"It's very good," said Wakana quietly.

Everyone looked at him.

Wakana was kneeling at the edge of the drawing, looking at it with an expression of total, helpless admiration, the way he had looked at the stone footings after Foundation.

"It's the best house I've ever seen," said Wakana. "In any world." He paused. "And we'd be living in the Muslin until Ori's grown up."

Ori, at the long table, said, "I'm already grown up," with great dignity, and nobody argued.

Senku put his head down on his knees.

"Six years," he said, muffled. "Maybe eight."



Rimuru had been sitting by the cold hearth through all of it, with his chin on his hand, saying nothing. Now he got up and came over and crouched down beside Senku at the edge of the enormous drawing.

He looked at it for a long time.

"It's beautiful," said Rimuru. "Really. It's the most beautiful plan I've ever seen. I mean that." He touched the clock tower with one finger. "In Tempest, it took me three years to get a building half this good, and I had ten thousand people and a dragon." He looked at Senku. "Keep it. Pin it on the wall next to Wakana's. Build it someday. All of it. The clock and the dome and the telescope. When we have time."

Senku lifted his head.

"But not now," said Rimuru.

"Then what?"

Rimuru looked round the common room. At Wakana. At the plan on the wall, with its dotted wings. At the pale level stone footings outside the window, waiting in the snow. At the forest to the east, and the dead trees that Sekhmet had said were tired and would like to be useful.

"Have you ever," said Rimuru, "seen a temple?"
