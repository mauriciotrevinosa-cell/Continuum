# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 22
## Chapter 20 — Timber

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 22  
**Reading order:** 20  
**Continuity state:** Day 76 | Rimuru's Eastern timber frame, no nails; living in it by spring, the rest in stages | everyone's spoon: Himmel's vermilion gate (Marin), Seiko's high seat, Milim Beam, FORT bigger | Satella's inner window | Frieren: 'There are going to be walls' | Senku pins plan: SOMEDAY

"A temple," said Senku.

"In my country," said Rimuru. "The old kind. Wooden. Some of them are over a thousand years old." He sat down on the floor at the edge of Senku's enormous drawing, cross-legged. "Wood. Mostly just wood. Big posts, big beams, a heavy roof. And no nails."

Wakana's head came up.

"No nails," said Rimuru. "Joinery. Every piece of timber cut so it locks into the next one. Mortise and tenon. Dovetails. Joints so complicated you can't see how they go together, and so tight you can't get them apart." He looked at Wakana. "You know this. Your fort frame. In the Muslin. Fern's treaty said no nails, and you built it without a single one, and it held Milim standing."

Wakana did not say anything. He was looking at Rimuru with his whole face.

"They flex," said Rimuru. "That's the trick. A concrete wall is strong because it doesn't move. A timber frame is strong because it does. When the ground shakes, it sways, and settles, and stands. The temples in my country have stood through a thousand years of earthquakes, because they bend." He turned to Senku. "And all you need for one is wood. Good wood. Dead wood, even, if it's dry. Sekhmet said the forest has a lot of trees that are tired and would like to be useful."

On the floor by the hearth, Sekhmet, who was still holding down one corner of Senku's drawing, said, "Yes," without waking up.

"The walls," said Rimuru, "aren't really walls. They're panels. Between the posts. Some of them slide, so you can open a room into the next one." He glanced at Wakana. "Walls made to come out."

Wakana made a small sound.

"The outer walls are clay," said Rimuru. "Clay and straw, packed into a lattice of split bamboo, or reeds, or whatever we have. Thick. It keeps the heat in, in winter, and out, in summer. Aira and Momo are bringing clay from the riverbank already. The straw's in the barn." He looked at Daphne. "If Daphne can spare it."

"I can spare it," said Daphne.

"Wide eaves," said Rimuru. "So the snow slides off and the rain doesn't touch the walls. A raised floor, over the cellar, so it stays dry. A long veranda along the south side, open, under the eaves, where you can sit and look at the yard." He looked out of the window at the ink tree. "At the tree."

"And the roof?" said Senku. His voice had changed.

"Tiles," said Rimuru. "Clay. Wakana says the riverbank clay fires red." He paused. "Or bark shingles, from the deadfall, to start. Tiles later. In stages."

The common room was very quiet.

"How long?" said Fern.

Rimuru looked at Wakana. Wakana looked at the stone footings outside the window, pale and level in the snow, cut by Foundation in the exact shape of the plan.

"The ground's done," said Wakana slowly. "The cellar's done. The saw's running." He was counting something in his head. "If the Searchers keep the timber coming, and everyone in the house can pull a rope—" He stopped. He looked at Rimuru. "We could raise the frame in a week. The core of it. The common room and the kitchen and the east section." He swallowed. "We could be living in it by spring."

There was a pause.

"By spring," said Senku.

"The rest in stages," said Rimuru. "The wings when we need them. The tiles when the kiln's ready. Your clock tower when we've got time." He looked down at Senku's drawing. "Your telescope, someday."

Senku looked at the drawing for a long time.

Then he started to laugh. Tiredly, helplessly, with his face in his hands.

"*No nails*," he said. "Six years of concrete, and he says *no nails*." He lifted his head. "That's the most elegant structural engineering in two worlds. And it's a shed. A very big, very beautiful shed."

"It's a temple," said Rimuru.

"It's a ten billion percent shed," said Senku, "and I love it."



And then, because it was the house, everyone put a spoon in.

Himmel went first. He stood up at the long table with the air of a man who has been waiting for exactly this moment all his life.

"Columns," said Himmel.

Everyone looked at him.

"At the front," said Himmel. "At the entrance. Two of them. Big ones. Tall." He spread his hands. "Heroic. So that when someone comes up the road and sees this house for the first time, they know at once that it is a place where great things happen."

"It's a timber frame," said Wakana. "Everything is columns."

"Two *special* columns," said Himmel. "Standing on their own. At the gate. Holding up nothing." He paused. "Except the sky."

"Red," said Marin.

Everyone turned.

Marin was on her feet at the end of the bench, with her eyes very bright.

"Paint them red," said Marin. "Bright red. Vermilion. In Rimuru's country, the old temples have gates like that, two posts and a beam across the top, painted bright red, so you can see them from miles away." She looked at Rimuru. "Right?"

Rimuru looked at her for a long moment, with an expression Mau could not read.

"Right," he said quietly.

"So Himmel gets his columns," said Marin, "and they're red, and they're a gate, and anyone coming up the road sees them first." She turned to Himmel. "Heroic *and* beautiful."

Himmel looked at her with deep respect.

"Marin," he said, "you have understood me completely."

It went on like that.

Seiko wanted a high seat in the new common room, a proper raised platform at the north end, so that she could look down at all of them and their idiocies from a suitable elevation. Nobody argued. Milim wanted the ridge beam, the great main beam that ran along the top of the roof, to be the Milim Beam, and wanted to be the one who lifted it into place. Wakana, after a long pause, said yes, *gently*, and Milim said *I KNOW*. Momo wanted the kitchen twice as big as the Muslin's, with Agott's warming glyph under the whole floor, and Agott said she would draw it, and looked at the ceiling. Maomao wanted the clinic beside the new well, with a shelf at the very top of the medical store that only she could reach, for the things that stopped your heart. Fern wanted a room for the ledgers, with a door that locked. Senku objected to the lock, in principle. Fern added a second lock, in principle.

Kessoku wanted the music room with its buffer wall, and Bocchi's private stair. The five from the big room wanted the big room, five beds, no walls, and a permanent kotatsu sunk into the floor, which Nano had already designed. The Witch Hat girls wanted an atelier with a north light, for drawing. Agott wanted her alcove with its curtain, and said so, and did not look at anyone.

Umaru wanted the fort.

"FORT (EXTENSION)," said Umaru, standing on the bench in her hood and her sash. "Senku drew it in the autumn. On the plan. A bay off the common room." She pointed at Wakana's plan on the wall. "Big enough for twenty people, he said. And six cushions." She drew herself up. "We are thirty. And seven witches visit by pass. And there are six cushions for whoever comes next. And there's a drawer in the toll booth marked *later*."

The common room went quiet.

"The fort," said Umaru, "needs to be bigger."

Wakana looked at the plan for a long moment. Then he took his pencil from behind his ear and went to the wall and, without a word, erased the outline of FORT (EXTENSION) and drew it again, half again as large, with a raised platform at one end for a toll booth.

Umaru sat down on the bench, very slowly, and did not say anything for a long time.



The witches did not ask for anything.

Mau noticed. They sat at the long table among everyone, eating breakfast, and listened to the whole house argue about columns and kotatsu and the size of the fort, and they did not say a word. When Wakana asked them, directly, what they wanted for the north wing, the first witch only folded her hands and said: "A room each. A door each. That is all we asked."

But Satella, beside her, looked up.

"A window," said Satella, very softly, in her hoarse unused voice. "In my room. Not to the outside." She looked down the long table, at all of them, at the noise and the bread and the arguing. "Onto this."

Wakana looked at her for a long moment.

Then he went back to the plan, and in the north wing, in the room nearest the common room, he drew a small window, in the inner wall, looking in.

And Frieren, who had not said anything all morning, looked up from her tea.

"The shelf," said Frieren.

Everyone stopped.

Mau turned to look at her. She was sitting beside him at the long table with her cup in both hands and an expression of perfect, serene, ominous innocence.

"In the autumn," said Frieren. "When Wakana drew the east section. You asked where the shelf goes." She sipped her tea. "And I said we'd argue about it when there was a wall to put it against."

She looked out of the window at the stone footings.

"There are going to be walls," said Frieren.

Mau closed his eyes.

Around the long table, very quietly, the entire house began to laugh.



Senku took his enormous drawing off the floor that afternoon, very carefully, and rolled it up, and carried it to the wall beside Wakana's plan, and pinned it there. All of it. The three floors and the cellar and the glass fourth floor, the clock tower and the dome and the telescope, the concrete and the rebar and the pipes from the hot spring.

At the top of it, in the empty margin, in small square capitals, he wrote one word.

*SOMEDAY.*

Then he went to the workshop to sharpen the saw.
