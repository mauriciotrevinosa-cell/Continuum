## Chapter 21 — Raising the Frame

**Volume:** 22  
**Reading order:** 21

They raised the frame of the real house in nine days, and every person in the settlement put a hand on it.

It began with the timber. The Searchers had been bringing it in from the old forest for a fortnight, dead trees and standing dead, the ones Sekhmet had said were tired. Kaneki worked through the nights with a team from the refuge families. Daphne walked the forest with them by day, laying her hand on each trunk before they cut it and saying *yes* or *no*. And the waterwheel turned on the riverbank below the weir day and night, and the saw sang, and the logs went in at one end of Senku's saw-house as trunks and came out the other as posts and beams and planks, square and true, in an hour.

Then the joints.

That was Wakana's. All of it. He drew every joint himself, on the wall of the workshop in charcoal, full size: the mortise and tenon, the dovetail, a dozen kinds of interlocking scarf joint whose names Rimuru knew in his own language and Wakana learned in an afternoon and then improved. Then he cut them. Or showed people how, and stood over them while they did. He had been a carver of dolls' faces in another world, where a slip of a fraction of a hair ruined everything. A tenon that had to fit its mortise so tightly that you needed a mallet to drive it home held no terrors for him.

The cooper came to help on the second day.

Nobody had asked him. He came to the workshop with his own tools in a roll of leather, and stood at the door, and looked at Wakana's charcoal joints on the wall for a long time.

"Pegs," he said.

Wakana looked up.

"You'll want pegs," said the cooper. "Oak. Through the joints. To lock them. Drawbored, so they pull tight." He unrolled his tools. "I've made a hundred thousand staves in my life. A peg's just a short round stave." He looked at Wakana. "How many do you need?"

Wakana looked at the plan.

"About four thousand," he said.

The cooper nodded once, as if this were a perfectly reasonable number, and sat down on an upturned barrel in the corner of the workshop, and began.



On the fifth day, they raised the first bent.

Rimuru explained it at breakfast, drawing on the back of a ledger page. A bent was a section of the frame, he said: two posts and the beam across the top, joined flat on the ground, then lifted up all at once with ropes and poles, swung upright, and dropped into place on the stone footings. Then the next bent, beside it, and the beams that joined them. Then the next. Like raising the ribs of a great animal, one after another, until there was a skeleton.

"Everyone," said Rimuru. "Every person who can pull a rope. On the ropes."

Every person who could pull a rope came.

It was a cold bright morning, the snow beginning, very slightly, to soften at the edges. The whole settlement came out into the yard and around the stone footings of the real house. The house, all of it, Rings One and Two. The fort, all thirty, in their coats. The witches, all seven, Sekhmet included, who was carried out on a bench by Minerva and set down at the edge of the footings to watch. The four refuge families who had stayed. The washing court, including a large red-handed woman who sang badly and did not look at Mau once and pulled harder than anyone. The cooper. The southern merchant and his hired guard, up on his next run, who had stayed two extra days for the frame-raising because, the merchant said, he had never seen anything like it in twenty years and was not going to miss it.

The first bent lay on the stone footings, two great posts and a beam, pegged together, with long ropes running from its top to teams on the far side.

Wakana stood at the end of it.

"On three," said Wakana. His voice was shaking. "Pull steady. Not hard. Steady. When it's up, hold it, and the pole teams set it." He looked round at all of them. "Don't let go until I say."

"One," said Wakana.

The ropes went taut.

"Two."

Mau was on the third rope, between Yuta and Stark, with Frieren behind him and Ori behind her. He could feel the rope through his gloves, the weight of the great frame on the other end of it. He could feel the whole settlement holding its breath.

"Three."

They pulled.

The bent rose. Slowly. The top of it lifted off the stone, and the posts swung upward, and the beam went up into the air, higher and higher, until it was standing against the white sky, and the pole teams ran in underneath with their long forked poles and set them against it. Then, with a sound Mau would remember for the rest of his life, a deep wooden *thunk*, the feet of the posts dropped into the sockets Foundation had cut for them in the stone.

It stood.

Wakana stood in front of it and looked up at it, two posts and a beam against the sky, and his face crumpled, and he did not say anything.

"Next," said Rimuru gently.

They raised eleven bents that day.



On the ninth day, they set the ridge beam.

It was the last and biggest piece. The long main beam that would run along the very top of the roof from end to end, the spine of the whole house, cut from the biggest standing-dead tree in the forest, which Daphne had walked to and laid her hand on for a long time before she said *yes*. It lay on the ground at the foot of the frame, forty feet of square-cut timber, so heavy that Senku said it would take sixty people on ropes to raise it.

Milim walked up to it.

"Gently," said Wakana.

"I *know*."

"*Gently*, Milim. If you drop it, or throw it, or hit the frame with it—"

"I *KNOW*," said Milim. She looked at the beam. She looked at the frame, the whole skeleton of the house standing against the sky, nine days of every person in the settlement putting a hand on it. She looked at Wakana.

Then she bent down, and put her hands under the middle of the ridge beam, and lifted it.

She did not throw it. She did not fly up with it. She lifted it as if it were a great sleeping animal that she did not want to wake, and walked up the long ladder at the end of the frame, slowly, one rung at a time, with forty feet of timber balanced across her shoulders. She walked along the top of the frame, along the joints, very slowly, to the middle. And set it down. Into the notches Wakana had cut for it, at the top of every pair of rafters, end to end, so exactly that it settled into all of them at once with a single long low wooden sigh.

Then she stood on top of it, on the very spine of the house, and looked down at all of them.

"*Gently*," said Milim, enormously proud.

Wakana, at the foot of the frame, sat down in the snow.



Rimuru climbed up after her.

He had told them about this at breakfast too. In his country, he said, when the ridge beam went up on a new house, there was a ceremony. Not a solemn one. The builders went up on the roof and threw rice cakes down to everyone below, for luck, and everyone caught them, and the children screamed, and it was the best day of the whole building.

Seiko and Momo had been making rice cakes for two days.

Now there were baskets of them at the foot of the ladder, small round white ones, hundreds, and Rimuru carried the first basket up and stood on the ridge beam beside Milim and looked down at the whole settlement crowded around the frame.

"For luck," said Rimuru. "For the house."

And he threw them.

The rice cakes went out over the crowd in a white arc, and the crowd shrieked and surged and grabbed for them. The children went mad. Milim threw a handful so hard they went over the Muslin's roof and landed in the plaza. Then Yuta climbed up and threw some. Then Wakana, who could not stop crying and threw them very badly. Then Senku, who calculated the optimal trajectory and then forgot to throw. Then Mau.

He stood on the ridge beam of the real house with a basket of rice cakes on his hip and looked down.

All of them. Everyone. The house, and the fort, and the witches, and the washing court, and the refuge families, and the cooper with his hat full of rice cakes, and the merchant and his guard. All of them looking up, laughing, with their hands out.

He saw Frieren at the front, under the ink tree, with her hands not out, looking up at him.

He threw her one. Just one. Gently.

She caught it.

She looked at it in her hand. Then she looked up at him on the ridge beam, and the small crooked smile came at the corner of her mouth, and she ate it.

"Yuta," said Mau, without looking round.

"Mm?"

"Rice cakes."

There was a pause.

"Don't," said Yuta.

"Gojo," said Mau. "On the train. *You're doing great, I'm so proud of you, I'm going to be sick.*"

Yuta, on the ridge beam beside him, made a sound that was half a laugh and half something else, and threw his last rice cake as hard as he could out over the crowd and the yard and the ink tree and the Muslin, so far that nobody saw where it landed.

"He'd have loved this," said Yuta quietly.

"Yeah," said Mau.

They stood on the ridge beam together and looked down at the frame of the house, all of it, standing against the sky, pegged with four thousand of the cooper's oak pegs and held up by no nails at all, while the whole settlement ate rice cakes in the snow below.
