## Chapter 22 — The Bath

The bath was finished before the house was.

That was Rimuru's doing. While the clay teams packed the walls of the real house, lattice by lattice, with Aira and Momo bringing mud up from the riverbank in barrows and the Witch Hat girls treading straw into it barefoot and shrieking at the cold, Rimuru took six people and went round to the north side, where the steam rose.

Foundation had found the water. It came up out of the ground there, through a crack in the rock that had not existed before the seventieth day, into a wide shallow basin of pale stone, and ran off down a channel into the river. It steamed all day and all night. The snow never settled within ten feet of it. On the day of Foundation, the whole fort had taken off its shoes and stood in it up to its ankles, screaming. Since then, people had been going round to the north side at odd hours to put their hands in it, and coming back with pink fingers and strange expressions, as if they had been to church.

Rimuru built a bath around it.

Not a pool. A bath. He was very particular about the difference, and explained it at breakfast, more than once. The basin, deepened and lined with river stones, smooth ones, each chosen by hand. A low wooden house over half of it, open on the south side, with a washing floor of slatted cedar and wooden stools and wooden buckets, where you washed before you got in. The other half open to the sky, the rocks rising round it, the snow banked up behind, so that you could sit in hot water to your chin and watch the stars. A tall bamboo fence down the middle, from the back wall right out into the water, dividing it in two. And a smaller bath at the far end, in its own little house, with a door that latched from the inside, and a slate by the door with chalk on a string.

"For families," said Rimuru. "Or couples. Or anyone who wants to bathe without the whole settlement. You write your name on the slate for an hour."

The slate was full by lunchtime on the first day. Nobody admitted to having written on it.



It opened on the ninetieth day, in the evening, at dusk, with the stars coming out.

Umaru opened it.

There was no question of anyone else. It had been her slip: *A BATH. A BIG ONE. WITH HOT WATER. LIKE IN RIMURU'S STORIES ABOUT HIS COUNTRY.* Fern had framed it. It hung by the door of the bathhouse, in Umaru's large crooked capitals, behind a pane of Senku's glass.

Umaru stood under it in her hood, with her sash over her coat and a towel over her arm and a wooden bucket in both hands, and looked at the whole settlement crowded into the snow in front of her.

"The Ministry of Fort Affairs," said Umaru, "declares the bath open."

"Rules first," said Rimuru.

Umaru deflated slightly.

Rimuru pinned a board by the door. Wakana had cut it; Marin had lettered it, in red and blue.

*THE BATH.*
*1. Wash first. All of you. Then get in.*
*2. No towels in the water.*
*3. No swimming.*
*4. No splashing.*
*5. No Milim Beam.*
*6. Get out before you faint.*
*7. Drink water after.*

"Number five," said Milim, "is *personal*."

"Number five is for everyone," said Rimuru.

"Nobody else *has* one."

"And yet."

Marin had painted the curtains, too. Two long cloths hanging over the two doors, one on each side of the fence, split down the middle so you could walk through them. One was red. One was blue. On the red one, she had painted a woman in a towel, very elegant, winking. On the blue one, she had painted a man in a towel, flexing.

Himmel stood in front of the blue curtain and looked at it for a long time.

"Marin," he said, "is this me?"

"It's a general man," said Marin.

"It looks very like me."

"It's a very general man," said Marin.



There was, then, the question of Rimuru.

Nobody had thought about it. Then, all at once, everybody had.

"He's with *us*," said Milim, grabbing his arm and pulling him toward the red curtain. "He always bathes with me. In Tempest. With Shuna and Shion and everyone—"

"*Milim*—"

"He's a man," said Himmel, from the blue curtain, with the confidence of a man who had never once in his life been in doubt about anything. "He told us. In his story. A general contractor, thirty-seven years old."

"He's a slime," said Senku. "Slimes don't have—"

"*Senku.*"

"I'm only saying, biologically—"

"He's a *slime*," said Umaru, from the door, as Minister. "Slimes are Ministry jurisdiction." She considered. "He goes on the fence."

Everyone looked at the fence. It was a tall bamboo fence, running from the back wall of the bathhouse all the way out into the open water, eight feet high. Along the top of it, Wakana had laid a flat cedar rail, to cap the bamboo.

"On the fence," said Rimuru.

"In a bucket," said Umaru. "On the rail. In the middle. Neutral ground." She folded her arms. "Ministry decree."

And so that was where Rimuru bathed, on the first night of the bath, and every night after that, for as long as anyone could remember. A small blue slime in a wooden bucket of hot water, sitting on the cedar rail on top of the bamboo fence, exactly in the middle, under the stars, with steam rising on both sides.

*I find this arrangement,* said Ciel, *extremely satisfactory.*

"You would," said Rimuru, from the bucket.



On the red side, they washed first, because Fern made everyone wash first, and checked.

Then they got in.

Mau heard it from the other side of the fence. Everyone did. It was a long, low, collective sound, like a whole choir letting out its breath at once, that went up into the dark over the steaming water and the snow-covered rocks and did not seem to end.

Then Sekhmet.

Minerva carried her in. She had carried Sekhmet everywhere for a fortnight, as a matter of course, on her back or in her arms, the way you carry a heavy and much-loved sack of something. She carried her across the washing floor, and Fern made her wash Sekhmet first, and she did, very gently, scowling. Then she waded into the hot water up to her waist and lowered Sekhmet into it, slowly, until only her face was above the surface, her long hair floating out round her like weed.

Sekhmet opened her eyes.

It was the first time anyone had seen both of them open at once, in daylight or lamplight or any light, since the witches had come. They were very pale, and very clear, and they looked up at the stars over the bath for a long time.

"I am going to live here," said Sekhmet. "Now."

"You can't live in the bath," said Fern.

"I can," said Sekhmet. "Watch." And closed them again.

Maomao waded in, and crouched until the water was at her chin, and cupped some in her hand, and looked at it, and smelled it.

Then she tasted it.

"Maomao," said Fern.

"Sulphur," said Maomao. Her eyes had gone very bright. "Some iron. A little salt. Something else I can't name." She tasted it again. "Good for chilblains. For the skin. For stiff joints. For the cough the refuge children have had all winter." She looked at the steaming water with an expression that on anyone else would have been love. "Half my work. Gone. Into a hole in the ground."

"Don't drink it," said Fern.

"I'm not drinking it," said Maomao, and drank a little more, thoughtfully.

Frieren lay back in the water and floated.

She did not swim; rule three. She simply lay back with her arms out and her hair spread round her, silver in the lamplight, and floated, with her eyes closed and her ears very slightly pink at the tips from the heat, and an expression on her face that Fern, sitting on the rocks beside her, had not seen in a very long time.

"Frieren-sama," said Fern.

"Mm."

"You look happy."

"I'm warm," said Frieren. "For the first time since the autumn, I'm warm all the way through." She did not open her eyes. "It's very distracting."

Satella sat on the rocks at the edge, in a towel, with her feet in the water, for a long time. She did not get in. She sat and watched all of them, Fern and Frieren and Maomao, Kessoku in a row with their chins on the water, Marin teaching Coco to put a folded towel on her head, Momo and Aira and Seiko, the witches. She held her knees.

Then Echidna, without a word, held out her hand.

Satella took it, and got in.



Milim did not swim, or splash.

Milim got in very, very carefully, under the eye of Rimuru's bucket on the rail, and sat down in the hot water up to her chin, and stayed there, perfectly still, for almost a minute, with her face going redder and redder.

Then she said, "Rimuru, I'm *bored*," and stood up, and climbed the rocks at the far end, and looked over the fence.

On the blue side, there was a sound like a flock of birds taking off at once.

"*MILIM*—"

"I'm not *looking*," said Milim, offended, looking. "I'm talking to *Mau*." She leaned her elbows on the top of the bamboo. "Mau. Mau. Is it nicer on your side? It sounds nicer. Himmel's telling a story."

"*Milim*," said Fern, from the red side, in a voice that made the water go cold.

Milim sighed enormously and climbed down.

"It *sounded* nicer," she said, to nobody, and got back in, and was quiet for almost two minutes.



On the blue side, Himmel was telling a story.

He had washed first, thoroughly, and got in, and settled himself on a smooth rock with the water at his chest and his arms spread along the stones behind him, and his hair slicked back, with the air of a man who had found the place he would like to spend the rest of his life. Around him in the steam were Mau, and Yuta, and Stark, and Senku, who had brought a thermometer, and Okarun, and Jinshi, who bathed beautifully, and Hakari, who sang, and Wakana, who had fallen asleep against the rocks within four minutes and had to be watched, and Kaneki, and Eren. Qifrey was at the far end with his eyes closed. Diablo was standing, fully clothed, outside the blue curtain, on guard, by choice.

"There was a hot spring," said Himmel, "on our journey. In the north. In the mountains. We'd been walking for a month through snow. Heiter's beard had frozen solid. Eisen's axe had frozen to his back." He spread his hands in the steam. "And on the last night, in a pass, in a blizzard, we found it. A hot spring. In the rocks. Just like this. And Frieren—"

"That's not what happened," said Frieren, from the other side of the fence.

Everyone on the blue side looked at the fence.

"Frieren?" said Himmel.

"It wasn't a blizzard," said Frieren's voice, through the bamboo, unhurried, from somewhere in the steam. "It was light snow. And it wasn't the last night. It was the fourth night. And it wasn't a hot spring in the rocks. It was a hot spring with an inn next to it, and the innkeeper charged you double because you were a hero, and you paid it, because you were a hero."

The blue side was very quiet.

"Well," said Himmel, "the *spirit* of it—"

"And Heiter's beard didn't freeze," said Frieren. "He spilled wine in it, and it went stiff, and he told everyone it froze."

"Frieren-sama," said Fern's voice, also through the fence, "what did *you* do? At the hot spring?"

There was a pause.

"Nothing," said Frieren.

"She fell asleep in it," said Himmel, instantly, delighted. "For six hours. We had to take turns keeping her head above the water. Eisen did two shifts."

"That's not what happened."

"It's *exactly* what happened."

"It was five hours."

On both sides of the fence, the bath dissolved.

It went on for an hour. Himmel told a story. Frieren corrected it, through the fence. Fern asked questions, through the fence. Stark took Fern's side about something and was told, through the fence, that he had not been there and could not have a side. Milim asked if anyone on the blue side wanted to hear about the time she had fought a whole army of demon lords in a hot spring in a volcano, and everyone on both sides said yes, and she told it, and it was obviously not what had happened either, and nobody corrected it. Rimuru, in his bucket on the rail, between both halves of the house, listened to all of it with the stars overhead, and said nothing, and at some point, Mau was fairly sure, cried a little, which in slime form was impossible to see.

Mau sat in the hot water with his back against the smooth stones and listened to Frieren's voice coming through the fence, correcting Himmel, in the steam.

Yuta, beside him, nudged him with an elbow under the water.

"You're smiling," said Yuta.

"No, I'm not."

"You're smiling at a *fence*."

Mau sank down until the water was over his mouth, and did not answer, and went on smiling at the fence.



The slate by the little bath at the far end said, in a column of chalk, in seven different hands that nobody admitted to:

*Yuta / Maki.*
*Marin / Wakana.*
*Inventory. (F.)*
*Anko / Nazuna — after midnight.*
*Jinshi.* And under it, in a different, much smaller hand: *No.*
*Senku — temperature study.* And under it: *Suika too.*
*M / F.*

Seiko stood in front of the slate for a long time with her paper fan, reading it, with an expression of profound satisfaction.

"*Inventory*," she said. "Hah."

The last hour on the slate was nearly midnight. The lamps in the main bath had gone out by then, and the house had gone back to the Muslin in a long, steaming, laughing line through the snow, pink to the ears, with towels on their heads, and the north side was dark except for the stars and the steam.

The little bath had its own small house, and its own small door that latched, and its own small window of Senku's glass that looked out on the snow and the black hills and the stars. The water came into it through a bamboo pipe from the main spring and was very hot.

Mau sat in it to his chest with his back against the stones.

Frieren sat across from him, with her knees drawn up and her chin on them, in the steam. Her hair was pinned up. Her ears were pink.

For a long time they did not say anything. It was very quiet. The water came out of the bamboo pipe with a small continuous sound, and steam went up past the little window, and outside the stars were very bright over the snow.

"Five hours," said Mau at last.

Frieren looked at him.

"You fell asleep," said Mau, "in a hot spring. For five hours. And they took turns."

"Eisen did two shifts," said Frieren.

"He'd have had to."

"He didn't mind." She looked out of the small window at the stars. "He told me afterward, years afterward, that it was the only time on the whole journey he saw me sleep like that. Without suppressing anything. Without listening for anything. Just asleep." She was quiet for a moment. "I didn't know that, at the time. That they were watching. I thought I'd only closed my eyes."

Mau did not say anything.

"I'm not going to fall asleep in this one," said Frieren.

"You can if you want."

"I know," said Frieren. She looked back at him, across the hot water, with her chin on her knees. "That's why I'm not going to."

She moved across the little bath, through the steam, and sat down beside him, against the stones, and leaned her head on his shoulder. Her hair was damp against his neck. The water was very hot. Outside the small window, the stars turned very slowly over the black hills.

She was asleep in about four minutes.

Mau sat very still, in the hot water, with the latch on the door, and the stars outside, and Frieren asleep on his shoulder without suppressing anything or listening for anything, and kept her head above the water, and did not mind at all.

He did two shifts.



At dawn, the main bath was empty, except for two.

Kaneki came off the night shift at the timber yard with sawdust in his hair and his hands raw from the cold, and Eren came off the north watch with frost on his collar, and they met at the bathhouse door without having planned it, and looked at each other, and went in.

They washed. They got in. They sat in the hot water on the blue side, with the fence beside them and the empty bucket on the rail above it and the snow on the rocks going pink with the first light, and did not say anything for a long time.

"She'd like this," said Kaneki at last.

Eren did not ask who. He was the only person in the settlement who knew.

"Yeah?" he said.

"She'd pretend not to." Kaneki looked at the steam going up into the pink sky. "She'd stand at the door and say it was stupid, sitting in a hole full of hot water with half the settlement. And then she'd get in, and she'd never get out." The corner of his mouth moved. "Like the sleeping one."

Eren nodded.

Kaneki was quiet for a long time. He looked down at his hands in the water, raw from the cold and the saw, going pink.

"One coin," he said.

Eren looked at him.

Kaneki did not say anything else. He did not explain. He only looked at his hands under the hot water for a while, and then leaned his head back against the smooth stones and closed his eyes, as the sun came up over the black hills and the steam went gold.

Eren did not ask. He sat beside him in the hot water until the sun was all the way up, and then he said, "Breakfast," and Kaneki said, "Yeah," and they got out, and drank water after, because it was on the board.
