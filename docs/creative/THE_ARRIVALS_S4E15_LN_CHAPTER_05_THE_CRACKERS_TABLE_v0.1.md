*When: The evening of Day 81, in the middle of the nine days of the frame. The bents of the real house stand against the sky across the yard like the ribs of something enormous. Everyone is exhausted. Since the river, nine days ago, Frieren and Maki have gone back down to the water together four times, and someone has noticed.*

Seiko caught them on the way back from the river.

It was the evening of the eighty-first day, after dinner, with the lamps going down in the Muslin and the bents of the real house standing black against the last of the light across the yard. Frieren and Maki came up the bank past the timber yard together, the width of a hand apart, the way they had come up it four times in the last ten days. Not talking. They had been talking, down by the water. Now they were not.

Seiko was sitting on the step of the Muslin with her paper fan.

She did not get up. She watched them come across the yard toward her, slowly, the way she watched everything. And when they reached the step, she lifted the fan an inch.

"Four times," said Seiko.

Maki stopped. Frieren stopped beside her.

"Four times in ten days," said Seiko. "Down to the river after lunch. Back at dusk." She looked at the two of them over the fan. "And every time you come back up that bank, your faces are different from when you went down it." She lowered the fan. "I'm old. I watch faces. It's the only thing I'm good for any more."

"Sorry," said Maki. Stiffly. "We'll—"

"You'll come in," said Seiko.

They looked at her.

"The river's cold," said Seiko. "And it's going to be colder, and you're going to keep going down there, because whatever you're doing down there is working." She stood up, slowly, the way old women stand. "So you can do it at my table instead. It's warm. There's tea." She turned round. "And crackers. I make crackers."

She went inside.

Maki and Frieren looked at each other.

"I don't think," said Frieren slowly, "that was a question."

"No," said Maki.

They went in.



Seiko's corner of the Muslin was behind a folding screen at the far end, by the back door, where the old woman had slept since the house was one floor of posts and slotted boards. Nobody went behind the screen. It was not a rule. It was simply understood, the way it was understood that you did not sit in Seiko's high seat at the long table or touch her fan.

Behind it there was a low table. A brazier, glowing. A kettle on it, already steaming, as if she had known. Three cushions round the table. And a tin. A large, battered, painted tin, with flowers on the lid, the paint worn through at the corners.

Seiko sat down on the cushion at the head of the table. She did not offer them the other cushions. She did not need to.

They sat.

She poured tea into three cups. Then she opened the tin, and took out crackers, and put them on a plate in the middle of the table. Rice crackers. Small, round, brown, glazed with something that smelled of soy and sugar.

"Eat," said Seiko.

They ate.

Frieren took a bite of hers and stopped.

"These are very good," she said.

"I know," said Seiko. "I've been making them for sixty years." She picked one up herself. "I don't make promises. I make crackers. People can rely on crackers." She bit it. "Promises go stale."

Maki looked at the old woman across the table for a long moment.

"Why did you bring us in?" she said.

Seiko did not answer at once. She chewed her cracker, slowly, with great care, the way old people chew. She drank her tea. She put the cup down.

"Because I know what two women walking down to a river every few days means," she said. "I've been one. Many times." She looked at Frieren. "You. Since the tree. You look at him at the long table the way you look at a book you're afraid to finish." She looked at Maki. "And you. You've never slept properly. Not since you came. You sleep like a soldier. With one ear." She folded her small hands on the table. "And now the two of you have found each other, down by the river. Good. That's good." She paused. "But a riverbank is cold. And I'm old, and the evenings are long, and I'm tired of watching your faces from my step and guessing."

Neither of them said anything.

"In my world," said Seiko, "when I was young, there were women in the village who met once a week. At night. At my grandmother's table. To drink tea, and eat what someone had made, and say the things they couldn't say anywhere else." She looked at her tin. "My grandmother had one rule. It's the only rule I've ever kept in my life."

"What was it?" said Frieren.

Seiko looked at her over the top of her teacup.

"Nobody leaves this table," she said, "carrying what they came in with."



The screen moved a little before eleven.

All three of them turned round.

Mai was standing at the edge of it. In her sleeping clothes, with a blanket round her shoulders, and her hair loose. Her face had the cool, faintly irritated look it always had. But she was holding the edge of the screen very tightly.

"I heard voices," she said. "And smelled tea." She looked at the three of them round the low table. At her sister. "And you weren't in your bed."

Maki did not say anything.

Frieren watched her not say anything. Watched her face. Watched the thing in it that only Frieren, at this table, knew the shape of. A woman looking at her sister, alive, standing at the edge of a screen in a blanket, from before. From before a floor, and a sword, and *destroy everything*.

"Sit down," said Seiko.

Mai looked at her.

"There's no cushion," said Mai.

Seiko reached behind her without looking and pulled out a fourth cushion from a pile in the corner, and put it on the floor at the table, between Maki and Frieren. Exactly between. Then she took a fourth cup from the shelf, and poured, and put a cracker on the plate in front of the empty cushion.

"There's always a cushion," said Seiko. "Sit."

Mai sat.

She sat very straight, between her sister and the elf, with the blanket round her shoulders and the tea in front of her. She did not drink it. She looked at Maki sideways.

"What is this?" she said.

"Tea," said Maki.

"At eleven at night."

"Nearly," said Maki.

Mai looked at her for a long moment. Then she picked up the cracker and ate it, irritably, the way she did everything near her sister.

Her face changed.

"Oh," said Mai. Despite herself. "These are good."

"I know," said Seiko.



The fifth one came at half past eleven.

They heard it before they saw it. A furious little voice, from somewhere near the floor, on the other side of the screen, getting louder.

"—*smell* those from the other end of the house, you shrivelled old *crone*, sitting up at all hours with your *crackers* like you own the place, some of us are trying to *sleep*, some of us have a *separate address*—"

Round the edge of the screen, at about the height of a cat, came a lucky-cat figurine. Waving one paw. With a face of thunderous indignation.

Turbo Granny.

She stopped dead when she saw the table. At the four women round it. At the tin.

Seiko did not look round.

"Ah," said Seiko, pleasantly, to her tea. "The dead hag."

"*Living* hag," said Turbo Granny. "Technically. In a manner of speaking. And at least I don't go round stuffing people into *cats*—"

"I stuffed one person into a cat."

"*Me*. You stuffed *me* into a *cat*."

"It was the cat or nothing," said Seiko. "You should be grateful. It's a very nice cat."

Maki was looking from one to the other with an expression of deep, slow fascination. Mai had put down her cup. Frieren's face had not changed at all, which meant she was enjoying herself enormously.

"How did you get here?" said Seiko. "You live on the boy's windowsill. On the far side of the Muslin."

"I *walked*," said Turbo Granny. "With my *paws*. Because I *smelled* those *crackers*." She glared at the tin. "You've been making them in secret. At night. For sixty years. And never once offered me one."

"You never once asked," said Seiko.

"I'm *asking now*."

Seiko considered her for a long moment over the fan.

Then she took a cracker out of the tin, and broke off a very small piece of it, the size of a fingernail. She held it out, down toward the floor, between finger and thumb.

Turbo Granny looked at it.

"That's an *insult*," she said.

"That's the size of your *mouth*," said Seiko.

Turbo Granny glared at her for a long moment. Then she took it. With both paws. And ate it, furiously.

Her face changed.

"Oh," said Turbo Granny. Very quietly. "That's *disgusting*. That's the best thing I've ever eaten." She held out her paws. "Another one."

"Sit down first," said Seiko.

"There's no cushion for—"

Seiko reached behind her without looking, and took a tiny cushion off the shelf, the kind you would put under a vase, and put it on the corner of the low table. She put a piece of cracker on it.

Turbo Granny looked at the tiny cushion for a long time.

Then she climbed up onto the table, muttering, and sat on it.

"I'm not *staying*," she said.

"Nobody asked you to," said Seiko, and poured her a thimble of tea.



They stayed until long after midnight.

It was not like the roof. Maki thought that afterward, lying in bed beside Yuta in the grey, not sleeping, the way she never slept. She had heard about the roof. Everyone had. Mau and Yuta and Rimuru, once a week, on the ridge of whatever building they were in, saying nothing much, and then saying the thing. The rule: *if any of us starts carrying something he shouldn't, the other two say so*.

This was not that. It was louder. It had an old woman and a dead one in it, insulting each other over a tin of crackers. It had her sister, sitting very straight between her and the elf, being irritated at everything, eating more crackers than anyone.

But at some point, long after midnight, when the brazier had gone low and the kettle had been filled twice and Turbo Granny had eaten so much cracker she had to lie down on her tiny cushion, Seiko put her cup down and looked round the table.

"Well," said Seiko. "Who's going first."

Nobody said anything.

"The rule," said Seiko. "Nobody leaves carrying what they came in with." She folded her hands. "I'll go first, if you're all going to be cowards about it. I'm the oldest. Not counting the elf. Or the hag."

"I'm *younger* than you," said Turbo Granny, from her cushion, with her eyes shut. "In *spirit*."

Seiko ignored her.

"I'm going to die first," said Seiko.

The table went very still.

"Before any of you," said Seiko. "Before Momo. Before that boy of hers with the hair. Before the house is finished, maybe. Maybe not. I'm very old. Old women die. It's what we do." She said it the way she said everything. Flatly. Over the fan. "Momo knows. She doesn't talk about it. She makes soup instead. She makes more soup every week. Have you noticed? She's making soup for when I'm not here to eat it."

Nobody said anything.

"I don't mind," said Seiko. "I want that understood. Written down, if necessary. I've had a long life. A loud one. I've buried a husband and more friends than I can count. I've seen ghosts and aliens and a granddaughter fall in love." She looked at her tin. "I'm not frightened of going. I'm frightened of her watching me go. And making soup about it. For years." She was quiet for a moment. "That's what I came in with. Somebody else carry it for a week."

The brazier ticked.

"I'll carry it," said Frieren.

Seiko looked at her.

"I'm good at people dying," said Frieren. "I'm the best there is at it. Everyone I've ever known has died." She did not look away. "I'm not good at it. Somebody told me that, by the river, nine days ago. Nobody's good at it." She paused. "But I'm used to carrying it. So give it to me. For a week."

Seiko looked at her for a long moment over the fan.

Then she nodded, once.

"Your turn," she said.



Frieren did not say much. She said *longer*. She said *a tree that came ninety-nine years early*. She said *I asked him for something he can't give me, and he said yes, and I can't stop thinking about it*. She did not say *four hundred and thirty-seven*. She had said that by the river, to Maki, and that was Maki's to carry. She said the other thing instead. The thing she had not said to anyone.

"I'm afraid he'll go in front of something," she said, "and I'll be at the fence."

Nobody said anything.

"I've been at the fence before," said Frieren. "With Himmel. For ten years. Every time something came out of the dark, he was in front of it before I'd moved." She looked at her tea. "I didn't understand it, then. I didn't mind. I thought it was what heroes did." She paused. "And then he died. Old. In his bed. And I understood all of it at once, fifty years too late." She folded her hands. "And Mau does the same thing. Exactly the same. Except I understand it now. Every time." Her voice did not change. "I'm going to spend his whole life at the fence. Understanding."

Maki was looking at her.

"Give it to me," said Maki. Quietly. "For a week."

Frieren looked at her.

"I've been at the fence too," said Maki. "With Yuta. For a whole life." She almost smiled. "He goes in front of things as well. They all do. Every one we pick." She held out her hand, palm up, on the table. "Give it here."

Frieren looked at the hand.

Then she put her own on it. Cold. Small. And took it back.

"Your turn," said Seiko. To Maki.

Maki looked at the table.

She looked at the tin. At the crackers. At Turbo Granny asleep on her tiny cushion. At Seiko. At Frieren.

At Mai, beside her, very straight, eating another cracker. Not looking at her. Listening.

Maki did not say anything for a long time.

"Not tonight," she said at last.

Seiko looked at her over the fan. For a long moment. As if she could see exactly what it was, and how heavy, and why.

"That's allowed," said Seiko. "Once." She paused. "Not twice."

"Not twice," said Maki.

Frieren, across the table, did not look at Maki. She looked at her tea. And her face did not do anything at all, which was how Maki knew she was holding it. The thing Maki had told her by the river. Holding it very carefully, so nobody at this table would see it in her face. Especially the one sitting between them.

"And you," said Seiko. To Mai.

Mai put her cracker down.

"I didn't come in carrying anything," she said. Coolly. "I came in because I smelled tea."

"Everyone comes in carrying something," said Seiko. "Even if they don't know it. That's how they find the table." She folded her hands. "Go on."

Mai looked at her. Then at the table. Then, sideways, very briefly, at her sister.

"Fine," said Mai.

She was quiet for a moment.

"I make one bullet a day," she said. "That's what I can do. One. And it costs. My hands shake for hours afterward. Nobody sees because I don't let them." She was looking at her cup. "In my world it was enough. Barely. In this house—" She stopped. "In this house there's a slime who builds a dam in a breath. And a dragon who lifts beams. And an elf who kills things with a word. And him. Mau. Who makes things out of himself the way I do, and it costs him the way it costs me, except his are—" She stopped again. "Bigger."

Nobody said anything.

"I sit at the back," said Mai. "At the long table. In the crowds. I watch. I'm good at watching." Her voice was perfectly cool. "Nobody needs one bullet a day. Not here." She paused. "And my sister looks at me like I'm going to disappear."

Maki went very still.

"You do," said Mai. Not looking at her. "Every day. Since I came. At breakfast. At dinner. Across the yard. You look at me like you're counting. Like you're making sure I'm still there." She picked up her cracker again. "I don't know why. You won't say. You never say anything." She bit the cracker. "That's what I came in with. If you want it."

The table was silent.

Maki did not say anything. Her face had gone very white.

Frieren put her cup down.

"I'll carry that one," said Frieren.

Mai looked at her. Surprised.

"For a week," said Frieren. She did not look at Maki. She looked at Mai, calmly, with her serene face. "Your sister looks at you like you're going to disappear. And you don't know why. And it makes you feel like you're already gone." She paused. "Give it to me. I'm very good at carrying things I don't understand. I've done it for a thousand years." She held out her hand on the table, palm up, the way Maki had. "And I'll tell you something, if you want. For free. Not in exchange."

"What?" said Mai.

"People look at people like that," said Frieren, "when they love them so much it frightens them." She did not look away. "That's all. That's the only reason anyone ever looks at anyone like that. I'd know. I look at someone like that every night."

Mai stared at her.

Then, slowly, she looked at her sister. At Maki, white-faced, very still, across the low table, not saying anything.

Maki did not look away. For once.

Neither of them said anything at all.

Mai put her hand on Frieren's palm. Briefly. Then took it back.

"Fine," she said. "For a week." She picked up her tea. Her hand, round the cup, was not quite steady. "This is stupid," she said, to nobody. "This is a stupid table."

"It is," said Seiko. "It's a very stupid table." She opened the tin and put four more crackers on the plate. "Same time next week."



On the corner of the table, on her tiny cushion, Turbo Granny opened one eye.

"I didn't go," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"I didn't go," said Turbo Granny. Crossly. "Carrying. Whatever. Nobody asked me."

"You were asleep," said Seiko.

"I was *resting my eyes*." She sat up on the cushion. She looked round the table, at the four women, at the tin, at the brazier. "Fine. Here's mine." She folded her paws. "I like the boy."

Seiko raised one eyebrow.

"Okarun," said Turbo Granny. "The boy. I've lived in him. I've lived in his pocket. I've lived on his windowsill. I've been inside his *body*." She glared at the table. "And I *like* him. He's an idiot. He's the worst. He's the bravest idiot I've ever met." She stopped. "And I'm a yokai. In a cat. And one day he's going to grow up and get old and die, and I'm going to be on some windowsill somewhere, in a cat, forever."

The table was very quiet.

"That's mine," said Turbo Granny. "Somebody take it. I don't want it."

Seiko looked at her for a very long time over the top of her fan.

Then she held out one old, small, wrinkled hand, palm up, on the table. Beside the tiny cushion.

"Give it here," said Seiko. "You old fool."

Turbo Granny looked at the hand.

"I'm not *giving* you anything," she said. "I'm *lending* it."

"For a week," said Seiko.

"For a *week*," said Turbo Granny. And put one small paw on Seiko's palm. And took it back very fast, as if it had been burned.

"Same time next week," said Seiko again.

"I'm not *coming*," said Turbo Granny.

She came.



Fern wrote it in the ledger the next morning, at the long table, when Seiko came out from behind the screen at breakfast and said, to the whole table, *I'd like it written down that I have a table now. Nights. Once a week. It has a rule.*

*Seiko's table. Weekly. Nights. Frieren, Maki, Mai. (The dead hag, under protest.)*

*Rule: Nobody leaves carrying what they came in with.*

Umaru had read it over her shoulder and gone to the fort and come back with the slate.

*THE CRACKERS TABLE. (OFFICIAL.) (MEMBERS ONLY.) (NO, MILIM.)*

Milim had cried. Then Seiko had given her a cracker. Then Milim had stopped crying and asked if she could be a member, and Seiko had said *when you're older*, and Milim had said *I'm over a thousand*, and Seiko had said *then when you act it*, and Milim had gone away to think about that for a whole day.

On the roof of the new house, which did not have a roof yet, only bents against the sky, Mau said to Rimuru and Yuta, on the seventh night:

"Frieren laughed at dinner," he said.

They looked at him.

"Twice," said Mau. "Out loud. At something Milim said." He looked at the bents against the stars. "And at the hour, her heart isn't going as hard when she says *one*. I can feel it. Through the mattress." He paused. "She's been going to Seiko's. Behind the screen. With Maki. And Mai. And Turbo Granny, apparently, which nobody can explain. And she comes back lighter."

Yuta did not say anything. But he was smiling.

"What do they do there?" said Rimuru.

Mau thought about it.

"I don't know," he said. "She won't tell me." He almost smiled. "She says it's not mine to carry."
