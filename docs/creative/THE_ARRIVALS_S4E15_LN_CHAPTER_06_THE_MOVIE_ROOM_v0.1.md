The first room finished inside the real house was not a bedroom.

It should have been. Fern had a list. The list said: roof, outer walls, floors, kitchen, common room, bedrooms, everything else. It was a sensible list, and the whole house agreed with it, and then on the ninety-third day, when the bark shingles were on and the clay walls were drying and the floors were down in the east section and nothing else was, Fern came round the corner of the new common room with her ledger and found that someone had finished the movie room.

It was a long low room at the back of the east section, with no windows. The floor was raised in three wide steps toward the back, like the seats of a theatre, and covered in cushions and rugs and blankets from every bed in the Muslin. The far wall had been plastered smooth with lime and river clay, and washed white, three times, until it shone in the lamplight like a sheet of paper. And on the top step, at the back, on a little wooden stand of its own, there was a box.

Marin was sitting on the bottom step with her chin in her hands, looking at the white wall.

"Marin," said Fern.

"I know," said Marin.

"The list—"

"I *know*," said Marin. She did not look round. "Wakana did the floor at night. Aira did the plaster. The Witch Hat girls did the cushions. Senku did the box." She paused. "I did the wall. Three times."

Fern looked at the white wall for a long time.

Then she opened her ledger, and found the list, and between *common room* and *bedrooms*, in very small handwriting, she wrote: *movie room (done)*.



The box was Senku's.

He explained it at breakfast, which meant he explained it for twenty minutes, with diagrams. It was a magic lantern. That was the old name for it, he said, in his world, from three or four hundred years before he was born, long before films. A box with a light inside. A curved mirror behind the light, to throw all of it forward. A thick lens in front, to gather it. A slot, where you put a picture painted on glass. And a second lens, in a tube, at the very front, to throw the picture out across the room and onto a wall, as large as you liked.

"The lenses took me two months," said Senku. "The glass from the furnace isn't clear enough. I had to grind them by hand. Suika held the wheel." He paused. "I promised Marin a projector. This is it."

"And the light?" said Fern carefully.

Senku did not answer at once.

"An oil lamp," he said. "With a wick. Rem's lamp oil."

The common room was quiet. Everyone was thinking about the same thing. A bulb, on the seventy-fifth morning, glowing orange for one second before it went to glittering dust.

"Fire," said Senku. "Not electricity. Fire's been in this world since the beginning." He did not look up. "I think it's fine."

Nobody asked him what he would do if it wasn't.



The slides were the hardest part.

Glass was scarce. Senku had enough for twenty-four, small squares, each the size of a palm, ground flat and polished on one side. You painted on the polished side, very small, with very fine brushes, in colours that the light would shine through. Daphne found plants that gave colours: a red from a root, a yellow from a flower in the barn, a blue that Daphne would not say where she had got. Marin mixed them. The Witch Hat girls painted.

It took them a week, in the atelier, with the north light.

It would have taken less, except for Qifrey.

Qifrey checked every slide. Every one, under a lens, before it was allowed to dry. Because, he explained gently, the girls had spent their whole lives learning that a line drawn in the right shape, in the right order, did something. That was what magic was, where they came from. You drew it, and it happened. And when you were painting a tiny picture with a very fine brush, at night, by lamplight, tired, it was very easy for a curve and a cross-stroke and a circle to come together in the corner of a slide in a shape that meant something.

"It's ordinary paint," said Agott. "Not the ink."

"It's ordinary paint *tonight*," said Qifrey, mildly, peering at a slide of a flying saucer. "And tomorrow, someone will be tired, and reach for the wrong pot." He handed the slide back. "And then we'll have a projector that sets the movie room on fire."

On the fifth night, Coco painted a slide of a sunrise over a river, and Qifrey looked at it under the lens for a long time, and then quietly scraped off one small curve in the corner with the edge of a knife.

"What was it?" said Coco, white.

"A light," said Qifrey. "A small one." He smiled at her. "You'd have made a lantern with two lights in it. Very bright." He handed the slide back. "Paint it again. Without the curve."

Coco painted it again.



Wakana made the moving ones.

Nobody had known that slides could move. Senku had mentioned it, once, at breakfast, in passing: in his world, he said, the old lantern-showmen had made slides in two layers of glass, with the picture split between them, so that when you slid one layer across the other, part of the picture moved. A man raised his hat. A windmill turned. A ship rocked on the waves.

Wakana did not say anything at breakfast. Then he did not come to dinner for three nights.

On the fourth night, he brought Senku six slides in little wooden frames, each with a brass lever or a crank on the side. One was a ship that rocked when you moved the lever. One was a cat whose tail went back and forth. One was a girl who waved. One was a bird whose wings went up and down. One was a slime that bounced.

And one was a round frame with a crank, and two discs of painted glass inside, one behind the other, covered in bright overlapping petals of colour, red and yellow and blue. When you turned the crank, the two discs spun in opposite directions.

Senku turned the crank and looked at the colours turning through the glass, and did not say anything for a long time.

"Wakana," he said finally, "that's a chromatrope."

"I don't know what that is," said Wakana. "I just thought it would be pretty."

"It's a chromatrope," said Senku. "They were invented about two hundred years before me. You just invented it again. In three nights." He handed it back very carefully. "It's ten billion percent pretty."



They did the first showing on the ninety-sixth night.

Everyone came. The whole house, and the fort in its hoods, and the witches in their robes. Sekhmet was carried in by Minerva and laid down across three cushions at the front, where she announced that she would watch with her eyes closed. The refuge children, all six of them, were crammed onto the bottom step in a row with their knees up. The washing court came, and stood at the back, with their arms folded. The cooper came, with his hat off. The movie room had been built for forty people. There were more than seventy in it, on the steps and the cushions and the floor and along the walls and in the doorway, and someone was sitting on someone else on most of the steps, and it was very warm.

Kessoku had set up in the front corner, by the white wall, with their instruments. Bocchi's guitar. Ryo's bass. Kita's guitar. Nijika's drum, which was still a soup pot with a hide stretched over it, and a pot lid for a cymbal. Rimuru had told them, at breakfast, that the first films in his world had had no sound at all. Somebody played music in the theatre while they ran. A piano, usually. Sometimes a whole band.

"We'll play," Nijika had said. "Whatever it is. We'll make it up."

Bocchi had gone very pale and said nothing and come anyway.

Senku stood at the back, on the top step, by the lantern. Suika stood beside him, holding the box of slides in both arms like a baby.

"Lamps," said Senku.

They put out the lamps. One by one, round the walls, until the movie room was dark. Completely dark. Seventy people breathing in the dark, and somebody's knee, and somebody's elbow, and Sekhmet snoring very softly at the front.

Senku opened the little door in the side of the lantern, and struck a match, and lit the wick.

Everyone held their breath.

They all knew what they were waiting for. Mau could feel it, all round him, in the dark: seventy people listening, very hard, for the sound of glass breaking. A lens cracking. A flame going out for no reason.

The wick caught. Senku turned it up. The light inside the box grew, and the curved copper mirror behind it caught it and threw it forward, through the first lens, through the empty slot, through the second lens, out across the dark movie room, over everyone's heads, in a long pale beam full of slowly turning dust.

It hit the white wall. A circle of light, wide as a door, warm and golden and soft at the edges.

Nothing broke.

Senku let his breath out. Mau heard it, from three rows down. A long, shaky breath, in the dark.

"Fire," Senku said, very quietly, to nobody. "Fire's fine."

Then he slid the first slide into the slot.



It was the chromatrope.

Wakana had asked. He had not asked for anything else, in the whole building of the house, in nine days of raising the frame and four thousand pegs and a week of laying floors. Only this. That the first thing on the white wall should be the pretty one.

Senku turned the crank.

On the white wall, as wide as a door, the colours opened.

Red and yellow and blue, petals of them, overlapping, turning through each other, spinning out from the centre and spinning in again, opening and closing like a flower that could not decide. The whole movie room lit up with them. The colours went across seventy faces in the dark, turning, red and gold and blue, on Coco's open mouth and Ori's wide eyes and Satella's still face and Momo's hands pressed together. In the corner, without anyone telling her, Bocchi began to play. Something slow, and turning, that went round and round the way the colours did.

Nobody said anything at all.

Somebody at the back of the room, by the doorway, among the washing court, very quietly began to cry. Mau did not turn round to see who it was. He thought he knew.

The colours turned for a long time. Then Senku stopped the crank, and the petals slowed, and stopped, and the movie room was full of seventy people letting out their breath at once.

On the bottom step, one of the refuge children said, in a very small voice, "*Again.*"

Senku turned the crank again.



Then Okarun's.

It was the very bad film about aliens. Okarun had told it, at story night, weeks ago, all of it, start to finish, until he was shouted down. Then he had told it to Marin again, privately, slowly, while she wrote it down. Then Tetia and Richeh had painted it, twelve slides, in one night, laughing so hard Qifrey had to check every slide twice.

It was terrible. It was the best thing anyone had ever seen.

There was a flying saucer, made of two plates. There was a scientist with very large eyebrows. There was a woman who screamed, on four separate slides, each time in a different colour. There was an alien with antennae, who looked, everyone agreed at once, extremely like Okarun. Okarun narrated, from the floor at the front, with total sincerity, explaining each slide in detail and with great emotion. Kessoku played. Nijika hit the pot lid every time the woman screamed. Kita sang a love theme for the scientist and the alien that had not been in the film. Ryo played the same three notes on the bass for twelve slides and claimed it was minimalism.

When Wakana's moving slide of the flying saucer came up, and Senku moved the lever, and the saucer rocked across the white wall, the whole movie room screamed.

Momo laughed so hard she fell off the step. Seiko hit Okarun with her fan. On the cushions at the front, without opening her eyes, Sekhmet said, "*Again*," and nobody knew whether she meant the saucer or the woman screaming.

At the end, the alien went home. Okarun said this in a choked voice, with his hand on his heart. On the last slide, Tetia had painted the flying saucer going up into a sky full of stars, very small, and Nijika played a drum roll, and Kita held one long note, and the alien was gone.

There was a pause.

Then seventy people stood up in the dark and applauded a terrible film about aliens, for a very long time, while Okarun sat on the floor at the front with his face red and his glasses steamed up and could not speak.



The last one was Rimuru's.

He had not told anyone what it was. He had drawn it himself, alone, on eight slides, in the atelier at night, and asked Qifrey to check them, and asked nobody else to look. When Senku slid the first one into the lantern, the room went quiet before anything was even on the wall, because Rimuru was standing at the front by Kessoku's corner, small, in his human shape, with his hands in his sleeves, and his face had gone very still.

"This one's mine," said Rimuru. "It's a true story." He paused. "It's the first thing that happened to me. After the knife."

On the white wall, a cave.

Dark. Blue. Drawn in the clean simple lines of the comics he had drawn from memory, once, for someone. Rocks and water and crystals on the walls that glowed faintly.

And in the bottom corner, very small, a blue slime.

"I woke up there," said Rimuru. "In the dark. I couldn't see. I couldn't hear. I didn't have eyes, or ears, or hands. I didn't know what I was." He looked at the small blue slime on the wall. "I thought I was going to be alone in that cave forever."

The second slide.

A dragon.

It filled the whole wall. Rimuru had drawn it enormous, coiled round the cave, black and gold, with its head resting on its forelegs, asleep, inside a great cage of light.

Beside the cage, in the corner, the tiny slime.

At the front of the room, Milim made a small sound. Mau saw her, in the light from the wall. She had sat up very straight on her cushion, with her hands in her lap, and was looking at the dragon with an expression he had never seen on her face before.

"He'd been sealed in that cave for three hundred years," said Rimuru. "By a hero. Alone. In the dark. Nobody ever came. Nobody ever talked to him." He paused. "And then a slime fell in."

Bocchi was playing again, very quietly. Not the turning song. Something lower. Something that sounded like a cave.

"He was so loud," said Rimuru. On the wall, Wakana's moving slide: the dragon's head lifted, opened its jaws, roared at the tiny slime. "So loud. And so lonely. He hadn't talked to anyone in three hundred years. He talked to me for days. About everything. He was terrible at it. He was the worst conversationalist in two worlds." Rimuru's voice had gone soft. "He was my first friend."

The next slide. The slime and the dragon, close together, the slime very small against the great black-and-gold side.

"He gave me my name," said Rimuru. "Rimuru. He just decided it. Like that. And I gave him a family name, so we'd both have it. So that we were the same family." He looked at the wall. "Tempest."

The room was completely silent.

"And then I ate him," said Rimuru.

Someone in the middle of the room made a startled noise.

"To save him," said Rimuru quickly. "To get him out of the cage. I put him inside me, so I could work out the seal from the inside, where it couldn't stop me. And it took a long time. But I did it." On the wall, the last slide: the cave empty, the cage of light gone, and the small blue slime alone in the middle of it, with a faint black-and-gold glow inside it, like a candle in a jar. "He was in there with me for a long time. Talking. Always talking." Rimuru smiled. "He read comics. I drew them from memory, for him. Every one I'd ever read. He loved them. He'd shout about them for days."

He looked at the last slide for a long time.

"He's not here," said Rimuru. "He's in my world. He's fine. He's probably shouting at someone right now." He swallowed. "I just wanted you all to meet him. Because this is the movie room, and it's the house, and he'd have wanted to be in it." He looked down at his hands. "He'd have loved this. He'd have asked for the alien movie again, about forty times."

At the front, Milim stood up.

She went to the white wall, into the light, and stood in front of the painted dragon, with the black-and-gold glow from the slide falling across her face and her hair. She looked up at it.

"He's loud," said Milim.

"He's very loud," said Rimuru.

"He owes me a fight," said Milim. "From before. From before you, even." She looked up at the dragon. "He's a terrible loser." She paused. "He's my friend too."

Rimuru did not say anything.

Milim turned round, in the light, and came back across the room through the cushions, and sat down next to him on the floor at the front, very close, with her shoulder against his. She did not say anything else.

*He would have asked for the alien movie,* said Ciel, very quietly, inside him, so that only Rimuru heard. *Forty-one times. I have calculated.*

Rimuru laughed, a small wet laugh, and put his face in his sleeve.



Afterward, when the lamps were lit again and seventy people were getting up off each other and stretching and arguing about which part of the alien film had been the best part, Senku was still at the back by the lantern, with the little door open, looking at the flame.

Mau went up and stood beside him.

"It didn't break," said Mau.

"No." Senku watched the wick burn. "Fire's hers. Fire's always been hers." He was quiet for a moment. "So's clockwork. So's a spring. So's a needle." He looked up, and his eyes were blazing again, the way they had been on the floor of the common room with his enormous drawing. "A needle and a horn and a disc of wax, turned by a spring. No electricity. Nothing she hasn't already got."

"What does it do?"

"It keeps sound," said Senku. "You sing into the horn, and the needle cuts the sound into the wax, as a groove. And then you play it back, and the needle reads the groove, and the horn sings it to you." He looked down at the front of the room, where Kessoku were packing up their instruments. "Every song they've ever played. Every story night. Okarun's terrible narration. Rimuru's cave." He paused. "Discs. In a year. Maybe less."

At the front, Bocchi, who had heard the word *record*, had gone the colour of the white wall and sat down very suddenly on her guitar case.

Nijika was laughing. She had her drumsticks in one hand and was laughing at Bocchi, and patting her back, and then, in the middle of laughing, she stopped.

"My sister would hate that," said Nijika.

Everyone near her looked round.

"Records," said Nijika. She was looking at the drumsticks in her hand. "She runs a live house. In my world. A little basement club, where bands play." She smiled, a little crookedly. "She always said a recording isn't music. It's a photo of music. Music only happens in the room, with the people in it, once." She turned the drumsticks over. "She'd stand at the back of this room with her arms folded and say it was a stupid little lantern show in a shed."

She was quiet for a moment.

"And then she'd stay for all of it," said Nijika. "And ask for the alien one again."

Nobody said anything.

Nijika put her drumsticks in her back pocket, and picked up her soup-pot drum, and went out of the movie room with Kessoku into the cold, laughing again, telling Bocchi that nobody was going to make her record anything, probably, for at least a year.

But on the way out, Mau saw her hand go to the pocket of her coat. The other pocket. The one where she had carried her coin since the tenth day on the bridge, and had never once taken it out.

She did not take it out now.

She only held it, for a moment, through the cloth.
