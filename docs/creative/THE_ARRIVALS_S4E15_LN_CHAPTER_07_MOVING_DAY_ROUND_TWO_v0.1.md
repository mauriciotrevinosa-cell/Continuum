On the hundredth day, the eaves of the real house were dripping, and Fern opened the ledger to a clean page and wrote, at the top, in ink: *Moving day.*

Then she underlined it twice, which she had never done to anything.

The snow had gone soft in the night. It had not gone, but it had given up something, the way a fist gives up when it opens. Water ran off the long bark-shingled roof of the real house in a hundred bright threads and fell past the wide eaves into the yard without touching the walls, exactly as Rimuru had said it would. The clay was dry. The floors were down, upstairs and down. The sliding panels were in their grooves. The north wing, the last and smallest piece, a single storey of seven rooms in a row off the back of the common room, had gone up in four days from the timber left over from the frame, and its roof had been finished the evening before by lamplight, with Milim holding the lamp very gently.

"Everyone," said Fern, at breakfast, in the Muslin, for the last time.

Everyone looked up.

"Before anyone carries anything," said Fern, "the names."



The plan had been on the wall of the Muslin's common room since the autumn. Wakana's drawing of the real house, both floors, every room, every corridor, every door, and in every room a name, in pencil. The pencil had been rubbed out and rewritten so many times that in places the paper had gone soft and grey. Ori and Milim. Fern. Stark, with the dotted wall. Umaru and Suika, then a door. Kessoku. Bocchi, across from Umaru's door. REM, at the turn. And one room at the very end of the north corridor upstairs with nothing written in it at all.

Fern took the plan down off the wall. She carried it across the yard, through the open doors of the real house, into the new common room, with its high seat at the north end and its cold hearth and its long table that Wakana had built in one piece from the biggest of the deadfall oaks. She pinned it to the new wall, by the door, where the light from the south veranda fell on it.

Then she took out a pen and a pot of ink.

Ordinary ink. Fern's ink. Coco had checked.

"In ink," said Fern. "Each one. You come up, and you watch, and if it's wrong you say so now." She dipped the pen. "After this it's ink."

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Ori came up, in her coat, with her chin up, and stood at Fern's elbow, and watched Fern write *ORI* in ink in the room with the door into the east end. And then, after a pause, under it, *MILIM*. Milim came up behind her and watched too, holding her breath, with her hands clenched together under her chin.

"Trial run," said Ori, "still."

"Still," Milim agreed, enormously happy.

Then Fern. She wrote her own name very fast, without ceremony, in the room two doors from Frieren's. Then Stark, beside it, with the dotted wall between them, and she did not look at Stark while she wrote it, and Stark, standing behind her, did not look at anything else.

Then everyone.

It took all morning. They came up one by one, and in pairs, and in fives, and watched their names go into the plan in ink. The five from the big room came up together and watched Fern write five names in one room with no walls, and Nano checked the spelling of each one against a schedule. Kessoku came up in a clump, and Fern wrote three names, and then Bocchi came up alone, a little behind, and watched Fern write *BOCCHI* across the corridor from Umaru's door, with the small private stair down to the music room drawn beside it, and did not run. Anko and Nazuna. Kaneki and Aira. Mikasa and Eren, in the room with two exits, with *(bunks)* written small, at Mikasa's request, and *(top)* written smaller, at Eren's. Himmel's room, facing the road. Seiko's, beside Momo's. Okarun's, with a small separate square drawn on the windowsill, labelled in Fern's neatest hand: *TURBO GRANNY (separate address).*

"Barely acceptable," said a voice from Okarun's shoulder.

Marin and Wakana, above the workshop. Marin made Fern write it with a little heart and Fern refused, and Marin drew the heart herself afterward, very small, in red, and Fern pretended not to see.

Downstairs: Maomao's room beside the medical room. Maomao watched her own name go in, and then watched Fern's pen move to the far corner of the same room, where Jinshi's bed was drawn as far away from hers as the walls allowed.

Fern waited, with the pen over the paper.

Maomao looked at the corner for a long moment.

"*Jinshi,*" she said. "*Temporary.*"

Fern wrote *JINSHI (temporary)*, in ink.

Jinshi, standing at the back of the common room, looked at the word *ink* for some time, and then turned and looked out of the window, and his ears had gone a colour that half the settlement would have paid to see.



Then the turn.

Fern's pen stopped over the narrow room at the turn of the north corridor. The one Mau had left unmarked in the Muslin, never storage, never anything, with a note pinned inside the door for a whole season. The one Rem had written her own name on, on the door slip, in pencil, one afternoon, without telling anyone.

On the plan, in that room, in pencil, it said: *REM.*

Fern looked up.

Rem was standing at the edge of the crowd round the plan with a teapot in her hands, because Rem was always holding a teapot when there were a lot of people, the way other people held a cup to have something to do with their hands. She met Fern's eyes.

"Ink?" said Fern.

Rem looked at the plan for a long moment. At her own name, in pencil, in her own hand, the only name on the whole plan that nobody else had written.

"Pencil," said Rem.

The common room went quiet.

"It's yours," said Fern carefully. "Either way. It's yours in ink, or in pencil, or in nothing at all."

"I know," said Rem. She smiled a little. "That's why pencil is fine."

Fern looked at her for a long moment. Then she put the cap back on the ink, just for that one room, and moved her pen on to the next.

Mau, at the back of the crowd, did not understand it. He looked at Rem with her teapot, and at her name in pencil, and he did not understand, and he did not ask.

Ori, beside him, understood it completely, and did not say anything either.



Then the last room.

The room at the very end of the north corridor upstairs, past Aira, past the cellar stair. The room with no name. Mau had asked Wakana to draw it, in the autumn, at the very end, after everything else was placed. *For somebody who arrives,* he had said, *and doesn't stay a stranger.* Not intake. Not Arrival House. Something else.

Fern's pen hovered over it.

And Echidna, at the back of the common room, said: "Leave it."

Everyone turned.

She was standing by the window in her black robe with her milk-white hair, with the six other witches round her. They had come at the bell. They had knocked. They had stood at the back all morning while the house wrote itself into the plan in ink, and had not come forward, because nobody had asked them to and they did not yet know whether they were supposed to.

"I asked for that room," said Echidna, "on the forty-seventh day. For my books." She looked at the empty room on the plan. "I said a room only has to have a door. What is behind the door is my concern." She paused. "I did not ask to name it. Leave it with no name."

Fern looked at Mau.

Mau looked at the room at the end of the corridor. At the space where nothing was written. He thought about why he had asked for it.

"Somebody who arrives," he said slowly, "and doesn't stay a stranger."

"Yes," said Echidna.

"You want to put a library in it."

"I want to put a *door* in it," said Echidna. "To my library. Which is elsewhere, and large, and none of your concern." She tilted her head. "And when your somebody arrives, and does not stay a stranger, and does not yet have a room of their own, and cannot sleep, and walks down a corridor in a strange house at night looking for something—" She paused. "There will be a door at the end of it. With a lamp. And a chair. And every book I have ever read." The faint smile at the corner of her mouth. "That is not a bad first room. For a stranger who is not a stranger."

Nobody said anything.

Then Fern, without being asked, left the room with no name exactly as it was. She turned to the north side of the plan, where Wakana had drawn the dotted wings in the autumn, *not promises, places*, and on the west wing, the one that was still only dots, she drew a small square. In pencil. With nothing in it.

"For whoever comes," said Fern.

On the bench by the window, Nijika looked at the small empty square in pencil on the west wing for a long time, with her hand in the pocket of her coat.



Then they carried everything.

It took the whole afternoon. It took every person in the settlement, and a great deal of shouting, and one broken lamp. Every bed in the Muslin came apart and was carried across the yard in pieces and put together again upstairs in the real house. Every chest. Every chair. Milim carried the bunk beds for Mikasa's room in one trip, assembled, over her head, up the main stair, very slowly, with Mikasa walking backward in front of her saying *left, left, your left,* and Eren walking behind saying *you're fine, you're fine*. Wakana carried nothing, and ran from room to room checking joints, and was ignored. Senku carried his workroom, which was mostly jars. Kessoku carried their instruments in a careful procession, Nijika at the front with the soup-pot drum. Seiko did not carry anything at all, and was carried, in her armchair, by Okarun and Momo, to the high seat at the north end of the new common room, where they set her down and she looked out over the whole long room and its idiocies from a suitable elevation and pronounced it *adequate*.

The fort moved last.

Umaru had insisted. The fort, she said, was a sovereign nation, and a sovereign nation did not move until its citizens were housed. So all afternoon the fort stood in the corner of the Muslin's emptying common room, cushions and frame and toll booth and hamster flag, with thirty citizens guarding it in their hoods, while the rest of the house went past in both directions carrying beds.

At the end of the afternoon, when the last bed was up the stair, the fort moved.

It moved in procession. Wakana took the frame apart in sections and the citizens carried each section across the yard above their heads, in order, with the hamster flag at the front. The six NEXT cushions went in a stack in Mai's arms. Anko carried the RECORDS crate. Kusuri carried her glow jars in a basket, one at a time, three trips. Rem carried her own cup, off its hook in the toll booth, and hung it on the hook in the new toll booth herself.

Umaru carried the drawer.

The toll booth had one drawer. It was not large. It had a label on the front, in Umaru's crooked capitals: *LATER.* She had taken it out of the booth herself, very carefully, and she carried it across the yard in both arms, against her chest, with her hood up, and did not let anyone help, and did not let anyone look inside.

Mau knew what was in it. Everyone did. A coin. Her coin. And a fort application form, made out for a girl of about eleven in the river towns, the cooper's daughter, who was still in another world and had not yet been asked.

She carried the drawer into FORT (EXTENSION). It was half again as large as the plan had first said, with a raised platform at one end for the toll booth, exactly as Wakana had redrawn it the morning of the spoons. She slid the drawer into the new booth. It fit. Wakana had measured.

Then she climbed up on the platform, in her hood and her sash, and looked out over the new fort, with its thirty citizens and its six cushions and its seven reserved places for Ring Three by pass, and said, in a voice that was not quite steady:

"The Ministry of Fort Affairs declares the fort *moved*."

Nobody cheered. They were all, for some reason, a little too choked up to cheer for a cushion fort. Then Milim cheered, because Milim always cheered, and then everyone did.



The witches moved in at dusk.

They had nothing to carry. That was the strange thing. Seven women who had lived in a forest for longer than anyone could say, and when they came across the yard to the north wing in the blue evening, they came with almost nothing in their hands.

Daphne had a sack of seed.

Minerva had Sekhmet, over her shoulder.

Carmilla had nothing, and walked half behind Minerva, until she saw Bocchi on the veranda. Then she lifted one hand, very small, at the height of her waist. Bocchi lifted one back, exactly as small. Neither of them said anything.

Satella had her ALWAYS card.

Echidna had nothing at all. Her books were elsewhere.

And the first witch, at the back, in her composed way, never early and never late, was carrying a chair.

It was a plain wooden chair, old and pale and a little worn on the seat. Mau knew it before she was halfway across the yard. He had sat across from it every second week for a year, in a room upstairs at the old inn, the second door, answering questions about his seams. It was the chair that wobbled.

She carried it into the north wing, into the last room, the one at the end, and set it down by the window. Then she sat in it. It wobbled.

Mau stood in the doorway.

"You brought the chair," he said.

"I am fond of it," said Pride.

"It wobbles."

"Yes." She folded her hands. Then, after a moment, she unfolded them, and laid them flat on her knees. "It has always wobbled. For a year, every second week, you sat across from it and never once asked anyone to fix it." She looked up at him. "Neither did I. We both preferred it the way it was." She paused. "I would like to keep it the way it was. Somewhere that is not the old inn."

Mau did not say anything. He nodded, and stood in her doorway for a moment longer, and then went away, and did not tell anyone, and the next morning found that Wakana had looked at the chair and had very deliberately not fixed it.

The other rooms filled, slowly, in the blue dusk.

Daphne's window looked out across the fields toward the barn. She put the sack of seed on the sill and sat on the bed and looked at it and ate a whole loaf of Momo's bread, alone, very slowly, which Mau had never seen her do before, because Daphne did not eat slowly.

Sekhmet's room had a second door. Rimuru had drawn it. It opened straight out onto a short covered path that led round the back of the north wing to the bath. Minerva carried her through the room, through the second door, down the covered path, and lowered her into the hot water in the dusk, and Sekhmet said, "Thank you," with her eyes closed, and stayed there until midnight.

Minerva's room was next to Sekhmet's. She stood in it for a long time, looking at the walls and the door and the small window, with her bandaged hands at her sides, as if she did not know what to do with a room that nobody was going to attack. Then Maomao came by with fresh bandages, and sat her on the bed, and changed them, and left without saying anything, and Minerva sat on the bed afterward looking at her clean white hands, and something in her shoulders let go.

Carmilla's room was very small, by her own request, at the quiet end. She left the door open a crack, all night, which nobody had asked her to do.

And Satella's room was nearest the common room. She went in and closed the door, and for a long time nothing happened.

Then, in the wall between Satella's room and the common room, at the height of a person sitting on a bed, a small square of light appeared. A shutter, sliding back. The little inner window Wakana had drawn, the morning of the spoons, at Satella's request, onto the long room.

Through it, Mau could see her face. She was sitting on her bed, very close to the little window, with her ALWAYS card in her lap, looking out through it at the common room. At the long table. At the lamps. At Momo laying out bowls for the first dinner in the real house, and Senku arguing with Fern about the lock on his workroom door, and the fort's thirty citizens coming in through the arch with their hoods down, and Seiko on her high seat. At the noise of it.

She did not come out. She did not need to. She sat at her window, onto all of it, and watched, and her face in the little square of lamplight was the face of someone who has been standing outside a window in the snow for a very long time and has just been let in.



That night, Mau and Frieren slept in the real house for the first time.

The east room was larger than the Muslin's. It had the library bay, with the built-in shelves Wakana had made and Frieren had already half filled with grimoires. It had the reading chair, which Mau had said no to, and which Frieren had sat in for most of the afternoon. It had the closet in the inner corner, and above it the loft, where a small blue slime was already asleep on a pillow, having moved in at noon without asking, as everyone had known he would. It had a door in the north wall that opened into Ori's room. Through it, faintly, Milim could be heard snoring, about a foot above her bed.

And it had the east window.

Mau had carried the cars himself. One at a time. The red and white one first, then the red one, across the yard and up the stair and down the family corridor, held in both hands like something alive. Eren had carried the stands, and had set them on the new sill, and moved them, and moved them again, and measured the distance between them with his thumb, and moved them once more. Then Ori had come in through the door from her room with her small grey stone from the tidepool, and put it down between them, exactly in the middle, where it had always been.

Now the cars sat on the east windowsill of the real house, on Eren's stands, with Ori's stone between them, and the light of the snow outside came in through Senku's glass and ran along their sides like water.

And in the middle of the floor stood the shelf.

It was the old shelf. From the Muslin. The one that had never been placed. They had carried it across the yard together that afternoon, Mau at one end and Frieren at the other, and up the stair, and into the east room, and set it down in the middle of the floor. And then they had stood on either side of it, and looked at each other.

"Here," Frieren had said, pointing at the wall by the library bay.

"That blocks the bay."

"There," Mau had said, pointing at the wall by the closet.

"That blocks the loft ladder."

"By the window."

"*No.*"

So the shelf was still in the middle of the floor. *Temporarily,* Frieren had said. It had a grimoire on it, and Mau's comb, and a cup.

Mau lay in the new bed in the dark and looked at it. A dark shape in the middle of the room, in the snow-light from the window.

"There are walls," he said.

"There are," said Frieren, beside him, with her eyes closed.

"You said we'd argue about it when there were walls."

"We are arguing about it."

"We're not. We're lying here."

"This is how I argue," said Frieren. "Slowly. Over a long time." She turned over and put her face against his shoulder. "I have a lot of time."

Through the wall, very faintly, from the room two doors down, came the small dry sound of a panel sliding in its groove. Not open. Not much. About a hand's width.

Mau did not say anything about it. Neither did Frieren. But he felt her smile against his shoulder in the dark.

Downstairs, in the new common room, a lamp was still lit. Fern's ledger lay open on the long table beside it, at the page that said *Moving day*, underlined twice. Under it, in her small neat writing:

*Everyone moved. Everyone here.*
*The Muslin: guests.*
*The west wing: one square, pencil. For whoever comes.*

And under that, in a different hand, crooked and enormous, in charcoal, because Umaru had got up for a glass of water and found the ledger open:

*THE FORT IS BIG ENOUGH. (FOR NOW.)*

At the reset hour, Frieren's cold foot found his leg under the blanket, in the real house, for the first time.

"One," she said, into his shoulder, half asleep. "Still here."

"Still here," said Mau.

Outside, the eaves dripped, all night, a hundred bright threads of water falling past the walls into the soft snow, without touching them.
