*When: Day 95, evening into night. The real house has a roof now, and floors, and walls drying; the movie room is nearly finished. Moving day is five days off. The crackers table has met twice. Nazuna, as always now, woke an hour before dinner.*

Nazuna woke an hour before dinner, the way she always did now.

It had not been a decision. It had happened in the first winter, in the old inn, without anyone noticing. She slept through the days, the way she had always slept through the days, in the loft over Anko's bed with the shutter closed and a blanket over her head. And then one evening she had woken up at dusk, and come down, and found the whole house at the long table, and Milim crying about something and Senku explaining something else and Fern writing it all down. She had sat down at the end of the bench and eaten whatever was in front of her and listened. And it had been the most interesting hour of her week.

So the next day she had set herself to wake an hour earlier. And the day after that. Until she was sleeping from just after breakfast to just before dinner, like a cat in reverse, and nobody in the house could remember a dinner without her at the end of the bench in her coat with her hair everywhere, eating everybody's leftovers and asking inappropriate questions.

"Everything interesting happens at dinner," she told Anko, once, when Anko asked. "I'm not missing it. I've missed enough things."

Tonight she came down the loft ladder at five, yawning, and ate a cold potato from the kitchen, and sat down at the end of the long table in the Muslin, and looked round.

"Something's up," she said.

Anko, beside her, with her notebook, did not look up. "What makes you say that?"

"Yuta," said Nazuna.

Anko looked up.

Yuta was at his place, halfway down the bench, beside Maki. He was eating his soup. He was answering when people spoke to him. He was smiling, a little, at something Milim said. Nothing about him was different from any other evening.

"He's fine," said Anko.

"He's touching his pocket," said Nazuna. She bit the potato. "Every few minutes. Left side. Like he's checking something's still there." She chewed. "People don't check their pockets unless there's something in them they're thinking about."

Anko looked at Yuta for a long moment.

He touched his pocket. Left side. Quickly. Then went back to his soup.

Anko wrote something down.

"You notice things," she said, not looking up.

"I'm a vampire," said Nazuna. "Noticing people is half the job." She finished the potato. "The other half is bad for you."



The roof night was the seventh night, and it had been on the seventh night for as long as there had been a roof night. Since the Muslin's first weeks, when Yuta had explained to Mau and Rimuru that he and Maki had been *catching up, a whole life, and then this one*, and the three of them had climbed out onto a roof in the cold with a blanket and decided to do it again. Once a week. No business. And the rule: *if any of us starts carrying something he shouldn't, the other two say so.*

They had done it on the Muslin's roof, which was planks over poles, and creaked. They had done it on the bents of the real house, a week before, sitting on bare timber against the stars like three crows on a fence.

Tonight there was a roof.

A real one. Bark shingles over boards over rafters, laid by hand in the last five days, the whole house holding its breath while Wakana shouted *left, left, your left* from the yard. It was finished. It had a ridge. You could climb out of the loft window of the east end, the room that would be Mau and Frieren's in five days, and up a short slope of shingles, and sit on the ridge of the real house, the house they had built, and look at everything.

Mau went up first. Then Rimuru, in human shape, with the blanket. Then Yuta.

They sat on the ridge in a row, the way they always sat. Mau in the middle. The new shingles were rough and cold under them and smelled of bark. Below them, the yard. The ink tree, taller every day. The Muslin, small now, its windows glowing faintly from the lamps inside. The timber yard, and the red glow of the charcoal kilns at the far edge of it. And beyond everything, the north field going away in the starlight to the black hills.

Nobody said anything for a long time. That was how roof nights started.

"It's a good roof," said Rimuru, eventually.

"It's a very good roof," said Mau.

"Wakana's going to cry when he sees us on it," said Rimuru. "He's going to say we'll crack the shingles."

"We won't crack the shingles."

"I know. He's going to cry anyway."

Yuta did not say anything.

He was sitting on Mau's other side, with his knees drawn up and his arms round them. He was looking at the north field. And every few minutes, without seeming to know it, his left hand went to the pocket of his coat. Touched it. Came away.

Mau saw it.

He had seen it at dinner, too. He had not said anything. He looked at Rimuru, past Yuta's shoulder.

Rimuru had seen it too.

They looked at each other for a moment, in the starlight, over Yuta's head. Neither of them said anything. That was not how the rule worked. You did not jump. You waited. You gave him the chance to say it himself.

They waited.

Yuta's hand went to his pocket again.

"Yuta," said Rimuru, gently.

Yuta looked round.

"You're carrying something," said Rimuru.



Yuta did not answer for a long time.

He looked at Rimuru, and then at Mau, and then back at the north field. His hand had stopped halfway to his pocket. It stayed there, in the air, for a moment. Then it went the rest of the way, and went in, and came out with something in it.

A coin. Small. Warm. Of no metal anyone had a name for.

He held it on his open palm, in the starlight, between his knees.

"Since the bridge," said Yuta. "I've had it since the bridge. Like everyone." He looked at it. "I haven't decided. Everyone else has, or nearly. Nijika knows who. Rem knows who. Senku's *thinking*, at least. Coco's waiting for the centre of her lattice. Umaru's moved hers twenty-one times but she *knows*." He turned it over. "I don't know."

"Who would it be?" said Mau.

Yuta was quiet for a long moment.

"My teacher," he said.

Neither of them said anything.

"Satoru Gojo," said Yuta. "The strongest sorcerer in my world. In anyone's world, probably." He almost smiled. "He'd tell you so himself. Constantly." The smile went away. "He taught me. When nobody else would. When everyone wanted me dead because of what I carried. He took me in and taught me, and laughed at everything, and never once told me what he was really thinking." He looked at the coin. "And then he died."

The roof was very quiet.

"Sukuna killed him," said Yuta. "Cut him in half. In a fight that went on for hours. Everyone watched." His voice was very even. "I wasn't fast enough. I was there. Nearby. I wasn't fast enough to—" He stopped.

His free hand had gone up, without his seeming to notice, to his forehead. To the thin pale stitched line that ran across it, just under his hair. He touched it, the way you touch an old scar when you are thinking about how you got it.

Mau saw it.

He did not ask. None of them asked about the scar. Not Mau, not Rimuru, not anyone in the house. It had been there since Yuta came, and Yuta had never explained it, and the house had never asked, the way the house did not ask about a great many things. Mau looked at the stitched line under Yuta's fingers for a moment. Then he looked away, at the north field, and let it be Yuta's.

Yuta took his hand down.

"The Goddess said *some*," said Yuta. "Rem asked. About the dead. *Some.*" He turned the coin over. "So I could. Probably. He loved me. I think. He'd never say. But I think." He looked at the coin. "And I love him. So it'd work."

"So why haven't you?" said Rimuru.

Yuta did not answer at once.

"Because Sukuna's here," he said.

Mau went very still.

"In this world," said Yuta. "Out there. In the hills. Somewhere. You know. You fought him." He did not look at Mau. "If I bring Gojo-sensei here, he arrives in a world where Sukuna is. And he'll find out. Day one. Day two. And the first thing he'll want to do—" He stopped. "He'll want his rematch. That's who he is. He'll go looking. He'll go in front."

Nobody said anything.

"I'd be bringing him back to die again," said Yuta. Very quietly. "That's what I keep thinking. Every night. I hold the coin and I think about him walking out of Arrival House, laughing, and then walking out of the gate, and into the hills, and I'm standing at the line watching him go." He closed his hand round the coin. "I watched him die once. I'm not sure I can bring him here and watch it twice."

The roof was silent.

Below them, in the yard, someone crossed from the Muslin to the well with a lantern, and went back. The ink tree stood black. At the far edge of the timber yard, the charcoal kilns glowed red.

"And there's the other thing," said Yuta.

They waited.

"Everyone's bringing someone," said Yuta. "Or will. Nijika will bring her sister. Rem will bring hers. Senku will bring his father, he just won't admit it. Coco, her mother." He looked at his closed hand. "They're bringing people they lost. People they miss. To be *with* them." He paused. "I'd be bringing my teacher. The strongest in the world. And the first thing everyone would think is *good, now we've got the strongest in the world*. Now he can fight Sukuna for us." His voice did not change. "Now nobody else has to."

Mau looked at him.

"That's what everyone thought in my world," said Yuta. "About him. Every day. *Gojo will handle it.* *Gojo's the strongest.* *Let Gojo carry it.*" He was quiet. "Nobody ever asked him if he wanted to. Nobody ever stood next to him. We just let him go in front, because he was the strongest, and he was always laughing, so we thought he didn't mind." He looked at the coin. "He minded. I worked that out much later. Much too late."

He opened his hand.

"If I bring him," said Yuta, "I'm afraid I'll be doing it again. Bringing the strongest man in the world to a place with a monster in it. So he can carry it. So we don't have to."



Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Mau said:

"That's what I do."

Yuta looked at him.

"That's what I do," said Mau. "Go in front. Carry it. So nobody behind has to." He was looking at the north field. "You said it. On a roof. A long time ago. The Gojo lesson. Everyone let the strongest carry everything without asking. You said it to me and Rimuru, because that's what we both do." He paused. "And now you're doing it."

Yuta stared at him.

"You're carrying it by yourself," said Mau. "The coin. For eighty-five days. Every night. Not telling anyone. Not even Maki, I bet." He looked at Yuta at last. "You've been touching your pocket at dinner for a month. Nazuna noticed. *Nazuna*, Yuta. She only wakes up for dinner."

Rimuru made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

Yuta looked from one of them to the other.

"I was going to tell you," he said. "When I'd decided."

"That's the opposite of the rule," said Rimuru gently. "The rule's *before*. Not after. You don't come up on the roof and announce what you've decided. You come up on the roof and say *I'm carrying this*, and then we carry it together, and *then* you decide." He pulled the blanket a little further over all three of them. "You made the rule. You know the rule."

Yuta did not say anything for a long moment.

Then he laughed. Very quietly. At himself.

"I did make the rule," he said.

"You made the rule," said Mau.

Yuta looked down at the coin on his palm.

"So," said Rimuru. "We're carrying it. All three of us." He looked at the coin. "Here's what I think. You don't have to bring him now. You don't have to bring him at all. But if you do—" He paused. "If you do, he doesn't arrive into a world where everyone's waiting for the strongest to carry it. He arrives into this." He nodded down at the yard. At the Muslin with its lamps. At the ink tree. At the charcoal kilns. "Into a house where there's a roof, and a rule, and three idiots on it. Where nobody carries anything alone. Not even him."

"We'd stand next to him," said Mau.

Yuta looked at him.

"If he comes," said Mau. "And he wants his rematch. And he goes looking." He shrugged. "Then he doesn't go alone. We go with him. All of us. Or we sit him down at the long table and Fern says no, the way she says no to me." He almost smiled. "She's very good at it. She'd say no to the strongest man in the world without looking up from the ledger."

Yuta laughed. Properly, this time. Short and surprised.

"She would," he said. "She really would."

"So you don't have to decide whether he can carry it," said Rimuru. "He won't have to. That's what the house is *for*." He settled back against the ridge. "You just have to decide whether you miss him."

Yuta looked at the coin for a long time.

"I miss him," he said. Very quietly. "Every day."

Neither of them said anything.

"Then it's not *whether*," said Mau. "It's *when*."

Yuta closed his hand round the coin.

"When I'm ready," he said.

"When you're ready," said Rimuru. "And when you hold it, come up here first. Before. And tell us. And we'll sit with you while you do it."

Yuta looked at him. Then at Mau.

"On the roof," he said.

"On this roof," said Mau. "It's a very good roof."

Yuta put the coin back in his pocket. Left side.

He did not touch the pocket again for the rest of the night.



Someone was coming up the ladder.

They all turned round. A head came up over the edge of the loft window, below the slope of shingles. Then shoulders. Then a hand, holding something. Three somethings. Cups, steaming, held by their handles in one hand.

Kaneki.

He climbed up the slope of the roof carefully, one-handed, without spilling, and sat down on the ridge at the end, beside Yuta. Not close. He held out the cups. One. Two. Three.

"Coffee," said Kaneki.

They took them. Mau looked at his. It was black and thick and smelled like something burned a long time ago.

"I saw you from the kilns," said Kaneki. "Up here. It's cold." He did not take a cup for himself. "I thought you'd want something hot."

"Thank you," said Yuta.

Mau drank some. It was terrible. It was the worst coffee he had ever had.

"This is terrible," he said.

"I know," said Kaneki. "I make it like that."

Rimuru was looking at Kaneki over his cup. At the quiet young man sitting at the end of the ridge, who worked the night shift at the timber yard and the charcoal kilns, who did not sleep, who had come over the bridge alone and walked for days with Rem looking for Mau, and neither of them had called it trust.

"You heard," said Rimuru. "From the kilns. Didn't you."

Kaneki was quiet for a moment.

"Some of it," he said. "Sound carries. At night." He looked at the north field. "I wasn't listening. It just came down off the roof." He paused. "I'm sorry."

"It's all right," said Yuta.

Kaneki did not say anything for a long time. He sat at the end of the ridge with his hands empty on his knees, looking at the hills.

"You only get one," he said at last. Very quietly. "Coin."

Yuta looked at him.

"I've got one," said Kaneki. "Same as you. Since the bridge." He did not look round. "I need two. There are two people. A woman and a little girl. And I've got one coin. And I won't bring one without the other." He was quiet. "So I hold it. Every night. In the kiln light. And I don't use it."

Nobody on the roof said anything.

"I'm not telling you so you'll fix it," said Kaneki. "You can't fix it. Nobody can." He looked down at his empty hands. "I'm telling you because you're lucky. You've got one coin and one person. You miss him. He'd come." He paused. "Don't wait too long because you're afraid of what he'll do when he gets here. That's his to decide. Not yours." He stood up. "Make sure it's the one you can't not. That's all. And then use it."

He went back down the slope of the roof, carefully, and down the ladder through the loft window, and was gone.

The three of them sat on the ridge with their terrible coffee.

"I didn't know," said Mau, after a long time. "About the two."

"Nobody did," said Rimuru quietly. "I don't think he's told anyone." He looked at the ladder. "Except Eren, maybe."

Yuta was looking at the place where Kaneki had gone.

"He came up the ladder," he said, "with three cups. In the cold. And told us the worst thing he's carrying." He looked down at his own coffee. "And then he left before we could carry any of it."

"Next time," said Mau, "we bring four cups up."

Yuta looked at him.

"Next seventh night," said Mau. "We bring a fourth cup. And leave it on the end of the ridge." He looked at the empty place where Kaneki had sat. "In case."

Rimuru did not say anything. But he nodded.



At midnight, something flew over the roof.

They saw it go across the stars. A shape. Fast. Low. A coat, open, flapping. Hair everywhere. It went over the ridge of the real house, close enough that they could hear the coat, and banked round over the ink tree, and came back, and landed on the shingles beside Rimuru with a light thump.

Nazuna. Upside down, for a moment. Then right way up.

"Boys' club," she said. She sat down on the ridge, cross-legged, which should not have been possible on a ridge, and was possible for her. "Very exclusive. Very sad. Three boys on a roof with bad coffee." She sniffed. "Kaneki's coffee. I'd know it anywhere. It smells like a funeral."

"It's a good roof," said Rimuru.

"It's a great roof," said Nazuna. "I've been sitting on it every night since they put the shingles on. Watching the hills." She looked at Yuta. "You stopped touching your pocket."

Yuta stared at her.

"At dinner," said Nazuna. "Every few minutes. Left side. I noticed." She grinned. "Then you came up here, and now you've stopped. So somebody said something." She looked at the three of them. "Good. That's what roofs are for."

"How do you know what roofs are for?" said Mau.

Nazuna looked at him.

"I've spent my whole life on roofs," she said. "In a city full of night. Watching people through windows. Lonely people. People who couldn't sleep." She looked at the black hills. "The ones who were all right were always the ones who had someone on the roof with them." She stood up. "Anyway. I've got the north field. Anko's got the south. We swap at three."

"Still doing shifts?" said Yuta.

"I do *nights*," said Nazuna. "Night shifts are just nights with a reason." She stepped off the edge of the ridge, into the air, and did not fall. "Don't stay up too late, boys. It's bad for you."

She went away across the dark, toward the north field and the black hills.

The three of them watched her go.

"She only wakes up for dinner," said Yuta slowly. "And she noticed."

"She notices everything," said Rimuru. "She just never says."

Mau looked at the place where Nazuna had gone. Then at the end of the ridge, where Kaneki had sat, and left without taking a cup. Then at Yuta, beside him, not touching his pocket.

"There are a lot of people in this house," he said, "who notice things and never say."

"Yes," said Rimuru.

"We should get better at noticing them back," said Mau.

Nobody said anything to that. But on the next seventh night, when they climbed up onto the ridge of the real house with the blanket, Mau carried four cups. And at the end of the ridge, where Kaneki had sat, he put the fourth one down. Full. Hot.

It was gone in the morning. Empty. Rinsed. Upside down on the shingles.

Nobody had seen who took it.
