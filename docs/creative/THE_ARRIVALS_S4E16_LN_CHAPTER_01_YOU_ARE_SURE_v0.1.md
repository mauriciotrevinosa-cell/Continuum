## Chapter 25 — You Are Sure?

Nijika told Fern first, because that was the rule, and because Fern was the only person in the house who would not cry.

She found her on the hundred-and-first morning, in the new common room, very early, before anyone else was up, at the long table with the ledger open and a pen in her hand, writing nothing. It was the way Fern began every day now, since the twelfth. Mau said she was listening to the house wake up. Fern said she was doing the accounts.

Nijika sat down across from her.

Fern looked up. She looked at Nijika's face, and at Nijika's right hand, which was in the pocket of her jacket, closed round something. She put down the pen.

"Tonight," said Nijika.

Fern did not say anything for a moment.

"The roads are worse," she said at last. Not arguing. Only setting it down on the table between them, the way she set down everything, so that it could be looked at. "Anko's sightings page is full. The merchant's late again. The protection covers the settlement and not the road." She paused. "And she'd be the first. Since the window. We don't know how it works. Not really. Nobody's done it."

"I know," said Nijika.

"I'm not saying no."

"I know," said Nijika. "You wrote me first. On the list. On the fortieth." She looked at the ledger. "I waited for the house. I didn't want her to come to a house made of muslin. I didn't want her first night to be in a corridor where the walls come out." She smiled a little, crookedly. "She'd have said it was a squat."

"It *was* a squat," said Fern.

"It was a very nice squat."

Fern almost smiled.

"There's a square," said Nijika. "On the west wing. In pencil. You drew it yesterday." She looked at the plan on the wall by the door, where it hung in the early light. "For whoever comes."

"It's only dots," said Fern. "There's no wing. There's no room. Rimuru says the wings come in stages, when we need them."

"Then we'll need it," said Nijika. "Tomorrow. Or the day after. Whenever she says." She took her hand out of her pocket, and put it on the table, closed. "But the house is here now. That's what I was waiting for. The house." She swallowed. "I've waited a hundred days. I can't wait any longer, Fern. I can't. Every night I think, three days. For her it's been three days, or a day, or a year, and she's standing in the club with the lights off, and I'm *here*, in a house with a bath and a movie room, waiting for a *wing*."

Fern looked at her for a long time.

Then she picked up the pen again, and turned to the page of the ledger where the List was, the page from the fortieth day. And beside *1. Nijika: her sister. First.*, in the margin, very small, she wrote the date.

"Tonight," said Fern.



She told Kessoku second.

She did not need to. They had known since she sat down at the long table at breakfast and did not eat. Kita had looked at her once, across the bread, and then put down her spoon and stopped eating too. Ryo, who ate everything, ate everything, and then very quietly pushed Nijika's untouched bowl toward herself, and then, after a moment, pushed it back. Bocchi had not looked up from her porridge at all, and had been shaking very slightly since the first spoonful.

After breakfast, in the music room, with the door shut, Nijika said it out loud.

"Tonight."

Nobody said anything.

Then Kita came across the music room and hugged her, hard, the way Kita hugged, with her whole body, and did not let go for a long time. Ryo leaned on the wall with her bass across her knees and said, "Good," and then, after a pause, "I still owe her two thousand yen," and Nijika laughed so hard she had to sit down.

Bocchi did not move.

She sat on her stool in the corner by the private stair with her guitar across her lap and her hair over her face. Nijika looked at her. Everyone looked at her.

"Bocchi-chan," said Nijika gently.

"I'm *happy*," said Bocchi. Her voice came out very high and very fast. "I'm happy. I'm so happy. I'm happy for you. Seika-san is going to come. She'll be here. That's good. That's—" She stopped. Her hands had closed on the neck of the guitar so hard the knuckles were white. "That's good," she said, very small.

Nijika crossed the music room and crouched down in front of her stool.

"It's okay," said Nijika. "To be happy and something else. At the same time."

Bocchi did not say anything.

Under the stool, in the guitar case, in the small compartment under the neck, there was a coin. Everyone in the music room knew it was there. Bocchi had put it there on the tenth night, on the bridge, and closed the case, and not opened that compartment since. Her mother. Her father. Her little sister. The dog. Four, in another world, and one coin.

"I'm not going to," said Bocchi, into her hair. "I'm not going to use it. I said. I can't take one. If I take one I don't know what I'm leaving the others." She was shaking. "But when Seika-san comes. When she walks in. She'll have been *there*. Three days ago. In our world. She'll have seen—" She stopped.

"She might have," said Nijika quietly.

"I don't know if I want to know," said Bocchi. "What they're doing. If they're looking." She pressed her forehead against the neck of the guitar. "And I don't know if I can stand *not* knowing, when she's right there, and she *knows*."

Nijika did not tell her it would be all right. She did not know if it would.

She only put her hand on Bocchi's knee, and kept it there, and after a while Kita came and sat on the floor on Bocchi's other side, and Ryo, without getting up, played three notes on the bass, very low, the three notes from the snow.

Bocchi lifted her head.

"Tonight," said Bocchi. "Here. In the music room." She wiped her face on her sleeve. "We'll all be here. When you do it."

"Yeah," said Nijika. "That's what I wanted."



The house knew by lunch.

Nobody told it. Fern did not tell anyone, and Kessoku did not tell anyone, and Nijika certainly did not. But by lunch the whole house knew, the way the house always knew things, through the walls that came out and the walls that did not.

It did not make a fuss. That was the thing Mau noticed most, afterward. There was no announcement. Nobody came to Nijika and said *good luck*, or *are you sure*, or *it's the worst time*. Fern had already said that, and Nijika already knew.

Instead, things happened.

Momo made a second loaf of the sweet bread, the kind with the dried pears in it, and put it aside in the pantry with a cloth over it and a label in her round handwriting that said *FOR TOMORROW (OR WHENEVER)*. Rem went across the yard to the Muslin, which was the guest house now, and made up the best bed, the one in the old east room, with the warmest blankets, and lit the stove in there, and kept it lit. Wakana went to the plan on the wall by the door and looked at the small pencil square on the west wing for a long time, and then went to the timber yard and began, without saying anything to anyone, to sort through the leftover beams.

Umaru made a form.

It was a fort application form. She had made one for the cooper's daughter, in the winter, and kept it in the LATER drawer. This one she made at the long table at lunch, in front of everybody, very seriously, with her tongue between her teeth, in coloured chalk on a slate because the paper was all in the ledger. *APPLICANT: SEIKA (NIJIKA'S SISTER). STATUS: PENDING ARRIVAL. CUSHION: RESERVED (NEXT #1). NOTES:* And under notes, after a long pause, in very small letters: *will probably hate the fort. let her.*

She put it in the LATER drawer, on top of everything else.

And Mau watched Nijika, all afternoon, and saw that she was doing what Nijika always did, which was holding everything up. She rehearsed with Kessoku. She helped Momo in the kitchen. She played a game of something with the fort and lost on purpose. She laughed. She was steady, and bright, and exactly herself, and every so often, when she thought nobody was looking, her hand went to the pocket of her jacket, and stayed there.

At dinner, Frieren leaned over to Mau.

"She's frightened," said Frieren.

"Yeah."

"Not of the road." Frieren watched Nijika laugh at something Kita said, across the table. "Of the yes. Of hearing it." She was quiet for a moment. "She's been her sister's little sister her whole life. Tonight she's going to be the one who decides."



They did it in the music room at midnight.

It was not planned for midnight. It was only that it got late, and nobody wanted to say *now*, and then the bell in the tower over Arrival House rang twelve, far off, across the yard, and Nijika looked up and said, "Okay," and took her hand out of her pocket.

Kessoku were there. Kita on the floor by the soup-pot drum, with her knees up. Ryo on the low bench with her bass, not playing. Bocchi on her stool in the corner, by the private stair, holding her guitar like a shield. Nobody else. Mau had wanted to come, and had not asked, and Frieren had put her hand on his arm at the bottom of the stair and shaken her head, and he had understood. This was theirs.

So Mau knew what happened in the music room only afterward, from Kita, who told it at breakfast in a low, shaking voice to the whole long table, because Nijika could not.

Nijika sat down on the floor in the middle of the music room, cross-legged, with the coin in her palm.

It was a small coin. Warm. It had been warm since the tenth day, when every stayer had felt it appear in their hand on the bridge at sunset. It was not gold, and not silver, and not any metal anyone in the settlement had a name for. There was nothing on either face. It sat in the middle of Nijika's palm in the lamplight and was warm, the way a hand is warm.

"How do you do it?" said Kita, very quietly.

"You hold it," said Nijika. "And think of the person." She had asked Rimuru. Rimuru had asked Ciel. Ciel had said, *I do not know. Nobody has done it. But she made them simple. I think it is the simplest thing she has made.*

Nijika closed her hand round the coin.

She closed her eyes.

She thought of her sister.

Kita said afterward that you could see her doing it. That you could watch it go across her face. Not one memory. All of them, one after another, the way a whole song goes through you in the second before you start to play it. A tall woman with a cold face at the back of a basement club with her arms folded. A kitchen at night, after their mother, with only the two of them in it, and a pot on the stove, and her sister's back. Being carried home on someone's shoulders, very small, half asleep. A club built in a basement in a hurry, with secondhand amps and a stage made of pallets, *so you have somewhere to play.* Being told to practise. Being told she was too loud. Being told, very gruffly, without being looked at, on the night Kessoku played their first real show, *that wasn't bad.*

Her sister's face.

Nijika's hand was shaking.

And then, in the music room, at midnight, in the lamplight, a voice spoke.

Kita said she did not hear it. Neither did Ryo. Neither did Bocchi. They only saw Nijika's face change: go very still, and then very white, and then her eyes come open, wide, looking at nothing, at the air in front of her, listening.

It came to her, and to nowhere else. It was not loud. It was not soft. It was the voice that had spoken on the bridge on the tenth day, and in Senku's workshop, and on the forty-eighth night to the whole settlement, saying that the world was evolving.

Now it said something only to her.

*You are sure? Seika.*

Nijika sat in the middle of the music room with the coin in her fist and her eyes wide open.

She thought of everything Fern had said. The road. The sightings page. The protection that covered the settlement and not the road. The first, since the window. Nobody had done it. She thought of the club with the lights off, and the cold, and the snow, and the fort, and how her sister would hate all of it, and how she would stand at the back of every room in this house with her arms folded for a year.

She thought of the three days.

"Yes," said Nijika.

Her voice came out perfectly steady. Kita said it was the steadiest thing she had ever heard. Steadier than her drumming. Steadier than anything.

"Yes. I'm sure."

The coin went warm in her hand. Then warmer. Then, all at once, so warm that she gasped and opened her fingers.

Her palm was empty.

There was nothing there. No light. No sound. No sign at all that anything had happened, except that the coin was gone, and her palm was pink where it had been, and the lamps in the music room had not so much as flickered.

Nijika looked at her empty hand for a long time.

"When?" said Ryo, very quietly.

"She didn't say." Nijika's voice had started to shake now. Now that it was done. "She just said— and I said— and it's gone." She looked up at them. Her face had crumpled. "She didn't say *when*."

Then Kita was holding her, and Ryo was holding both of them, and Bocchi had come off her stool and put her guitar down on the floor, which Bocchi did not do, and was holding all three, and the four of them sat in a knot on the floor of the music room at midnight and did not say anything at all.



Nobody in the house slept much that night.

Mau knew, because he was awake, and he could hear them. Doors, very quietly, up and down both corridors. Footsteps on the stair. The kettle in the kitchen, at one, and at two, and again at three. Low voices on the south veranda. Somebody sitting on the bottom step of the main stair in the dark, for a long time, who turned out in the morning to have been Umaru, in her hood, with the fort application slate in her lap.

At two, he got up.

Frieren was already up. She was standing at the east window, by the cars, in her nightdress, with a shawl round her shoulders, looking out.

"Look," she said.

He looked.

Across the yard, past the ink tree, past the dark bulk of the Muslin with Rem's lamp still burning in the guest room, Arrival House stood at the edge of the plaza with all its windows black. And on its front step, in the cold, under the bell tower, in the starlight, sat four small figures in a row, wrapped in blankets.

Nobody had told them to wait there. Nobody knew if it would be there, or tonight, or tomorrow, or in a week. Arrival House, the voice had said once, long ago, to everyone. That was all.

Kessoku sat on the step of Arrival House in a row and waited.

Mau looked at them for a long moment.

Then he went down to the kitchen, and put the kettle on, and made five cups of tea, and carried them across the yard in the dark, with Frieren beside him carrying a sixth for herself and two more blankets.

They did not say anything when he got there. They took the tea. Bocchi took a blanket. Mau and Frieren sat down on the step beside them, at the end of the row, and the six of them sat there in the cold, under the bell tower, and watched the dark road and the dark plaza and waited.

At three, Anko came by on her rounds, with her lantern, and stopped, and looked at them, and sat down on the other end of the step. Nazuna came down out of the dark sky a little after and sat on the rail.

At four, the fort came. Not all of it. Umaru, with her slate. Suika. Ori, who was not in the fort and came anyway, in Milim's coat, with Milim floating half asleep a foot behind her. Coco and Agott, in one blanket.

At five, Yuta and Maki. Then Rimuru, in human shape, with a pillow, which he gave to Nijika. Then Fern and Stark, with more tea. Then Momo, with the pear bread, cut into slices, and a cloth over it. *FOR TOMORROW (OR WHENEVER).*

"It's tomorrow," said Momo, and handed it round.

By the time the sky began to go grey over the black hills, there were nearly forty people sitting on the step of Arrival House and on the ground in front of it, wrapped in blankets, holding cups, not saying much. Nobody had called them. Nobody had arranged it. The house had simply woken up, one by one, in the night, and looked out of its new windows, and seen four girls on a step in the cold, and come.

Nijika sat in the middle of the step, between Kita and Bocchi, with Rimuru's pillow in her lap, looking at the door of Arrival House behind her, and then at the road in front of her, and then at the door again.

"She's going to be *so* annoyed," she said, to nobody, in a small cracked voice, "that there's a *crowd*."

Nobody answered. But all along the step, in the grey light, forty people smiled.
