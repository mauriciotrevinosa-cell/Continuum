## Chapter 26 — Arrival House

Jinshi came across the plaza at the eighth bell with the key in his hand, as he had every morning since the building went up, and stopped dead at the edge of the step.

Forty people looked up at him from under blankets.

He looked at them for a long moment. At the empty cups, and the crumbs of Momo's pear bread, and Rimuru asleep sitting up against the wall with his mouth open, and Umaru asleep in her hood with the slate in her lap, and Nazuna asleep on the rail, and four girls in the middle of the step who were not asleep at all, and had not been, and were looking at him with their whole faces.

"Ah," said Jinshi.

He stepped between them, very carefully, and went up to the door. He put the key in the lock. He turned it with the same small flourish he always used, the one he did not know he did, which half the settlement imitated behind his back. Nobody imitated it this morning. Forty people watched him do it in total silence.

The door opened.

The intake hall was exactly as it always was. The long table, with the register on it. The waiting benches along the wall. The two terms rooms, their doors open. And at the back, the second witness room, which had not existed until the window, with its door half open and, inside, a table with nothing on it and a chair pushed back from it, as if someone had just stood up. Nobody had moved that chair since the eleventh day. Fern had said to leave it. Nobody had asked why.

It was empty. All of it. Grey morning light through the windows, and dust in the light, and nothing else.

The staff came in behind Jinshi: the four from the first wave of refuge, the two from the second community, and the girl from the river towns who kept the register. They took their places, as they always did. The girl at the register sat down and opened it to the page that said, in her small careful hand, *Day 101. Arrivals: 0.* She turned the page. She wrote *Day 102* at the top of the next one, and then stopped, with the pen in the air.

Nijika stood in the doorway.

"Can we—" she said.

"Arrival House opens at the eighth bell," said Jinshi. He stepped aside from the door. "It is the eighth bell."

Nijika came in. Kita came in behind her, and Ryo, and Bocchi, with a blanket still round her shoulders. Then, after a moment's hesitation, nobody else. The rest of the house stayed on the step and in the doorway and at the windows, looking in. Fern had said, a long time ago, that Arrival House was for strangers. It was not the house's. Nobody needed to be told.

Kessoku sat down on the waiting bench along the wall, in a row, and waited.



Nothing happened at the eighth bell.

Nothing happened at the ninth.

At the ninth, Momo came across from the kitchen of the real house with a tray of porridge and passed it in through the window, bowl by bowl, to the step and the doorway and the bench. Nijika did not eat hers. Kita ate half of hers and then put it down. Ryo ate hers, and Nijika's, very quietly, and then sat looking at the empty bowls with an expression of mild guilt.

At the tenth bell, some of the crowd on the step began to drift away. Not far. The fort went back to the fort, because Umaru had woken up and decreed that a watched pot never boils and that the Ministry would wait in an official capacity, from the toll booth, with the window open. Fern went to the common room to do the accounts, and did not do them, and sat at the long table looking out of the window at Arrival House. Rimuru woke up, looked round, said *not yet?* and went back to sleep against the wall.

Mau stayed on the step. Frieren stayed beside him. He could see Nijika through the open door, on the bench, with her hands in her lap and her eyes on the door of the second witness room, very straight, very still. Holding everything up.

At the eleventh bell, she fell asleep.

It happened all at once, the way it happens to people who have been awake all night holding something up. One moment she was sitting very straight on the bench, watching the door. The next, her head had tipped sideways onto Kita's shoulder, and her eyes were closed, and her mouth was a little open, and she was asleep. Kita froze. Then, very slowly, she put her arm round her, and looked at Ryo and Bocchi over Nijika's head, and none of them moved.

The intake hall was very quiet. Dust turned in the grey light. The register girl's pen hovered over *Day 102*.

And in the tower over Arrival House, the bell rang.

Once.

Nobody had pulled it. Mau knew this the way everyone on the step knew it, because they all looked up at once, at the tower, at the rope that hung down beside the door and had not moved. The bell rang once, by itself, a single round deep note that went out over the plaza and the yard and the fields and the real house, and hung in the air, and did not stop humming for a long time.

Nijika's eyes came open.

From the second witness room, at the back of the intake hall, came a sound.

A chair scraping on a wooden floor.



Jinshi reached the door first. He did not open it any further. He stood in it, very still, with his hand on the frame.

Then he stepped back, and turned, and looked across the intake hall at Nijika.

Nijika stood up. She did not seem to know she had stood up. The blanket fell off Bocchi's shoulders. Kita had both hands over her mouth.

Nijika walked across the intake hall. She walked the way people walk in dreams, as if the floor were a long way down. She reached the door of the second witness room, where Jinshi stood aside for her. And looked in.

Mau could not see, from the step. He saw only Nijika's back in the doorway, and her shoulders, and her hands, which had come up and stopped in the air in front of her and were not doing anything.

Then he heard a voice. A woman's voice, low and rough and flat, from inside the room. A voice that had not slept for a long time.

"*Nijika?*"

And then a sound like a great many pieces of paper falling at once.



Afterward, everyone remembered different things.

Kita remembered that Nijika did not run. That she stood in the doorway for what felt like a long time and was probably a second, with her hands in the air, and then she went in. Ryo remembered the papers. Bocchi remembered the sound Nijika made, which was not a word.

Mau remembered the papers too. He came in from the step by then, without deciding to, with half the house behind him in the doorway. And through the door of the second witness room he saw them all over the floor. Dozens of them. Sheets of thin white paper, printed, all the same, scattered across the boards and under the table and against the walls, where they had fallen out of someone's arms all at once.

On every sheet, four faces.

He did not know the writing. It was not any writing from this world. But he knew the faces. A girl with long pink hair, looking at the floor. A girl with red hair, smiling at the camera. A girl with blue hair and a blank expression. A girl with yellow hair and a side ponytail, grinning. Four photographs in a row across the top of every sheet. And underneath, in large black letters that he could not read but understood anyway, the way you understand a scream in a language you do not speak, one word.

And in the middle of the room, among the papers, the chair pushed back, the woman.

She was tall. That was the first thing. Taller than Nijika by a head. She had long yellow hair, the same yellow, tied back carelessly. She was wearing a black jacket over a T-shirt with something printed on it, and jeans, and the clothes looked as if she had been wearing them for three days, because she had. There were shadows under her eyes like bruises. In one hand she was still holding a roll of tape. On the table beside her lay a small black rectangle of glass, the thing they called a phone, with its screen dark.

She was holding Nijika.

She had her arms round Nijika so hard that Nijika's feet had almost left the floor, and her face pressed down into Nijika's hair, and she was not saying anything. Her shoulders were shaking. Her knuckles, on the back of Nijika's jacket, had gone white.

"You *idiot*," she said at last, into Nijika's hair. Very low. Very rough. "You *idiot*. Three days. *Three days.* Where were you. Where *were* you."

"Far," said Nijika, into her jacket. She was crying so hard the word barely came out. "Far away. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Onee-chan, I'm so—"

"Shut up." The woman's arms tightened. "Shut up. Don't. Don't apologize. You don't get to—" She stopped. She breathed. Mau watched her breathe, in, out, with her face hidden, the way a person breathes when they are holding something very heavy and cannot put it down yet. "I put up posters," she said. "Every station on the line. Every live house in Shimokitazawa. Every lamppost." Her voice cracked, very slightly, and closed again. "I didn't sleep. I sat on the stage with the lights off. I thought—"

She did not say what she thought.

She lifted her head.

She looked over the top of Nijika's head, at the doorway of the second witness room. At the girl with red hair standing in it with both hands over her mouth. At the girl with blue hair beside her, very still, with her blank face doing something it did not usually do. At the girl with pink hair a little behind them, holding the doorframe, white as paper, shaking.

The woman's face changed.

Mau would remember that change for a long time. He had seen a great many faces in this world. He had seen people come over the bridge and find out where they were. He had seen people find each other. He had never seen a face do quite what this one did, all at once, as it went from the girl with red hair to the girl with blue hair to the girl with pink hair, and understood.

"All of you," she said.

Her voice had gone very quiet.

"All four of you. *Here.*"

She took one arm away from Nijika. Not letting go. Only one arm. She held it out, toward the door, without a word, the way you hold out your arm to children in a crowd.

Kita ran.

She crossed the room in three steps, through the papers, and went into the open arm, and the woman closed it round her, hard. Ryo walked. She walked across the room at her own pace, unhurried, with her hands in her pockets, as if she happened to be going that way anyway. When she got there, she stood beside the three of them for a moment, looking at nothing. Then she leaned her forehead against the woman's shoulder, and stayed there.

Bocchi did not move.

She stood in the doorway holding the frame. Mau could see her shaking from across the room. Everyone could.

The woman looked at her over the other three heads.

"Bocchi-chan," she said.

Very gruff. Very short. The way you say the name of someone who is late for a shift.

Bocchi let go of the doorframe.

She came across the room the way she came across every room, as if she were walking into a strong wind. And she stopped in front of them, just out of reach, with her hands clenched at her sides and her hair over her face.

The woman reached out, past Kita, and put her hand on top of Bocchi's head. Not gently. Firmly. Like a lid.

She left it there.

Bocchi made a sound, very small, and stepped in.



The house stood in the doorway of Arrival House and did not say anything.

There was nothing to say. They stood in the doorway, and in the intake hall behind it, and on the step, and at the windows, forty of them and then more, as the fort came running from the toll booth with Umaru at the front, and Fern came running from the common room with the ledger still in her hand. They looked through the door of the second witness room at a tall tired woman in a black jacket holding four girls among a floor full of fallen posters, and nobody said anything at all.

Then the woman lifted her head again.

She looked past the four of them. At the doorway. At the crowd in it.

She looked at them for a long time.

At a small blue-haired boy rubbing his eyes, who was not quite a boy. At an elf in a shawl. At a man with dark hair and a strange quiet face beside her. At a small girl with pink twin-tails, floating a few inches off the floor. At a girl with blue hair and a maid's apron, holding a teapot. At a hooded figure with a slate. At a woman in black with milk-white hair. At a tall pale man in a butler's coat. At Jinshi, beautiful and composed, standing aside with the key in his hand. At forty people in blankets with porridge bowls, crowded into a doorway, all of them looking back at her, many of them crying.

Her face closed. It closed completely, the way a door closes. Mau watched it happen. Whatever had been open in it, a moment ago, holding four girls among the posters, was gone, put away, locked. What was left was cold and flat and extremely unimpressed.

"Nijika," she said.

"Mm," said Nijika, into her jacket.

"Who," said the woman, "are all these people."

Nijika lifted her face. It was red and wet and blotched and she was laughing, helplessly, in the middle of crying, the way you laugh when you have been holding something up all night and it has finally been taken off you.

"Family," said Nijika.

The woman looked at the doorway again. At the slime boy. At the elf. At the floating girl, who waved.

"*Hah?*" said the woman.



The register girl wrote it down. She had been sitting at the long table in the intake hall with the pen in the air since the bell, and when it was over, or as over as it was going to be, she got up very quietly and went to the door of the second witness room, with the register open in her hands, and cleared her throat.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I have to ask. For the register." She looked at the tall woman, who looked back at her with an expression that would have frozen a river. The register girl held her ground. "Your name?"

The woman looked at her for a long moment.

"Ijichi," she said. "Seika."

The register girl wrote it down, in her small careful hand, under *Day 102*. *Ijichi Seika.* And beside it, in the column she had ruled for it on her very first day and had not used since the window: *Arrived.*

Then, after a moment's thought, in the margin, very small: *by coin. The first.*

"Thank you," said the register girl, and went back to her table, and sat down, and put her face in her hands.



Later, much later, when the papers had been gathered up off the floor and the house had backed out of the doorway and gone to give the five of them the room, Mau went back into the intake hall to fetch Frieren's shawl, which she had left on the bench.

The door of the second witness room was half open. He did not mean to look. He looked.

They were sitting on the floor, the five of them, among the gathered posters, with their backs against the wall. Seika in the middle, with her long legs stretched out in front of her and her black jacket open, and Nijika under one arm and Kita under the other, and Ryo against Kita, and Bocchi on the end, holding one of the posters in her lap, looking at it. Seika's head was tipped back against the wall. Her eyes were closed. She looked like a person who had not slept for three days and was not going to start now.

"There was a voice," Seika was saying. Very low. Not opening her eyes. "At four in the morning. Last night. My last night." She paused. "I was sitting on the stage. The lights were off. I'd run out of posters." Another pause. "And a voice said— out of nowhere. Not loud. Not anything." She stopped.

"It said, *you are sure?*" said Nijika, under her arm, very quietly.

Seika opened her eyes. She looked down at her sister.

"It said your name," said Seika.

Nobody said anything.

"*You are sure? Nijika,*" said Seika. Her face was perfectly flat. "I didn't know what it was asking. Sure of what. I didn't know if I was dreaming. I didn't know anything." She looked at the ceiling of the second witness room. "Obviously I said yes."

"*Obviously?*" said Nijika, and her voice cracked.

"Idiot," said Seika. She tipped her head back against the wall and closed her eyes again. "It said your name."

Mau backed away from the door, very quietly, with Frieren's shawl in his hands, and went out of Arrival House into the grey morning, and stood on the step for a long time, breathing.
