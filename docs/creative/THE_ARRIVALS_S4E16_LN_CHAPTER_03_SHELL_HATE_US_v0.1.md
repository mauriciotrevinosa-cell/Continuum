## Chapter 27 — She'll Hate Us

Seika hated the cold first.

"It's freezing," she said, on the step of Arrival House, in the early afternoon, with her black jacket zipped to her chin and her hands jammed in its pockets.

"The snow's going," said Nijika. "It's much better than it was. You should have seen it a month ago."

"It's *freezing*."

"Momo has a coat for you."

"I don't need a coat."

Momo gave her a coat. It was a long brown wool one, from the refuge stores, with a hood lined with fleece. Seika looked at it for a long moment, held out at arm's length in Momo's two hands, with Momo beaming at her over the top of it.

"It's ugly," said Seika.

"It's *warm*," said Momo.

Seika put it on. She did not say anything else about the coat for the rest of the day, and she did not take it off either, and when she thought nobody was looking, she put her nose down into the fleece of the hood for a second and closed her eyes.



Nijika showed her everything.

That was the plan. Nijika had made the plan in the night, on the step of Arrival House, in a low voice to Kita, between the third cup of tea and the fourth: when she comes, I'll show her everything. The plaza. The bridge. The yard. The ink tree. The real house. The music room. The bath. The movie room. All of it, so she understands, so she sees it isn't a squat.

It did not go to plan, because the house came too.

It came in pieces, all afternoon. Wherever Nijika took her sister, someone was already there, by accident, very casually, not looking.

In the plaza, it was Himmel, who happened to be standing at the north end with his hands on his hips, looking at the road. He turned round as they came past and bowed, deeply, with a flourish, and said, "Welcome, Seika-san. I am Himmel the Hero." Seika looked at him for a long time and said, "Okay," and walked on, and Himmel watched her go with an expression of profound satisfaction, as if *okay* were the finest review he had ever received.

In the yard, under the ink tree, it was Coco and Agott and Tetia and Richeh, sitting on the bench round the trunk with their sketchbooks, who all looked up at once and stared. Coco said, "Your hair is the same as Nijika's," and Seika said, "Obviously," and Coco went pink to the ears and drew her for the rest of the afternoon from memory.

At the real house, it was Diablo, at the door, who bowed lower than Himmel, which Himmel saw from across the yard and took personally. In the common room, it was Seiko on her high seat, who looked down at Seika over her paper fan for a long, long time, while Seika looked back up at her with exactly the same expression, and the whole common room went quiet to watch.

"Hm," said Seiko at last.

"Hm," said Seika.

Seiko nodded once, slowly, and went back to her tea. Seika walked on. Okarun, at the long table, let out a breath he had been holding for half a minute.

"What was that?" whispered Kita.

"I think," said Ryo, "they recognized each other."



The fort processed her at three.

It was not optional. Umaru was waiting at the arch of FORT (EXTENSION) in her hood and her sash, with the slate under her arm, and the whole fort was arranged behind her on its cushions, thirty strong, in silence, watching.

Seika stopped in front of the arch.

She looked at the fort. At the frame Wakana had built without nails. At the hamster flag. At the toll booth on its platform, with Rem's cup on its hook. At thirty people of various ages and species sitting on cushions in a cushion fort in the corner of a common room, looking back at her with grave official faces.

"What is this," said Seika.

"This," said Umaru, "is the fort." She held up the slate. "You have an application."

She turned the slate round so Seika could read it. *APPLICANT: SEIKA (NIJIKA'S SISTER). STATUS: PENDING ARRIVAL.* Pending had been crossed out that morning, and *HERE* written above it, larger. *CUSHION: RESERVED (NEXT #1).* And at the bottom, in very small letters: *NOTES: will probably hate the fort. let her.*

Seika read it for a long time.

"Who wrote the notes?" she said.

"The Ministry," said Umaru.

Seika looked at the small hooded figure in front of her. At the sash, and the slate, and the very serious face under the hood, which was trying hard not to look hopeful.

"Accurate," said Seika.

She walked past Umaru into the fort, under the arch. She found the stack of NEXT cushions at the end of the bay, six of them, with a little card on the top one that said *#1*. She sat down on it. She sat on it for about five seconds, with her long legs folded and her hands in the pockets of the ugly coat and her face completely flat, looking round at the thirty citizens of the fort, who looked back.

Then she stood up.

"It's a terrible fort," said Seika.

She walked out under the arch again. Behind her, in the fort, nobody spoke. Then Umaru, at the arch, with the slate clutched to her chest, turned round to the thirty with her face shining.

"*Let her,*" Umaru whispered.

The fort, very quietly, cheered.



She met Mau at four, by accident, on the south veranda.

He had not meant to meet her at all. He had meant to stay out of the way, the way the house had been staying out of the way all day, appearing by accident and disappearing again. But he had come out onto the veranda with a cup of tea at the wrong moment, and she was there, alone, leaning on the rail, looking out at the yard and the ink tree and the white fields, in the ugly coat.

She turned her head and looked at him.

It was a long look. It went from his face to his hands to the cup of tea and back to his face. It was not unfriendly. It was the look of somebody who had spent a great many years standing at the back of a dark room, watching people come in through a door, and deciding very quickly which of them were going to be trouble.

"You're the one," said Seika.

Mau did not know what to say to that.

"All day," said Seika. "Everywhere she took me. Whenever anything happened, somebody did something, somebody said something stupid, they all looked at you. Just for a second. Then away." She turned back to the yard. "Not the elf. Not the slime. Not the old woman on the chair. You."

"I'm not in charge," said Mau.

"I didn't say in charge." Seika leaned on the rail. "I said they look at you." She was quiet for a moment. "I run a club. People look at the person they're scared for."

Mau stood very still.

Seika did not look round.

"She told me," said Seika. "Nijika. A bit. On the floor this morning. Not all of it. Enough." She was looking at the ink tree. "That you nearly died. That it was bad. That something's wrong with you that isn't your fault, and the whole house carries it with you."

Mau did not say anything.

"I'm not going to say anything about it," said Seika. "I don't know you." She pushed herself off the rail. "But she was scared for you. In the middle of telling me she was fine, she was scared for you." She walked past him to the door. "So don't do that again. Whatever it was."

She went in.

Mau stood on the veranda with his tea going cold and looked at the door she had gone through for a long time. Then, very slowly, he started to laugh.

Frieren came out a minute later and found him there, laughing into his cup, alone.

"What?" she said.

"Nijika's sister," said Mau, "just told me off."

Frieren looked at the door. The corner of her mouth moved.

"Good," she said.



Bocchi found her at five, or she found Bocchi. Afterward, neither of them could have said which.

It was in the music room. Kessoku had gone up to practise, because it was the hour they practised and because Nijika wanted her sister to hear them, and Seika had said she would come up in a minute and had not. Kita and Ryo and Nijika had gone back down to look for her. Bocchi had stayed. She was sitting on her stool in the corner by the private stair with the poster in her lap.

She had been holding it all day. The poster from the floor of the second witness room. She had picked it up in the morning, among the others, and had not put it down since. Four faces in a row across the top, and the big black word under them that half the house could read and nobody wanted to say. And in the bottom corner of the sheet, small, drawn in pink felt-tip pen, very badly, by a child, a dog.

There was a dog in the corner of every poster. Bocchi had checked them all.

Seika came in by the private stair, which was not the way anyone came in, and stopped when she saw Bocchi in the corner.

She did not say anything. She came across the music room and sat down on Nijika's empty stool, by the soup-pot drum, and folded her arms, and looked at the poster in Bocchi's lap.

"Your sister drew those," said Seika. "Futari. On every one. She wouldn't let us print them until she'd done the dog."

Bocchi's hands closed on the edges of the paper.

"Your mother came to the club," said Seika. "Every night. She sat at the bar and drank orange juice and didn't say anything. For three hours. Every night." She was looking at the soup-pot drum, not at Bocchi. "Your father drove. Up and down the line. All night, the first two nights. He came in at six in the morning and asked if anyone had seen you, and I said no, and he said thank you, and went back out." A pause. "The dog sat by the door."

Bocchi did not make a sound. She sat on the stool with the poster in her lap and her hair over her face and shook.

"They were looking," said Seika. "When I left. They're looking."

For a long time neither of them said anything.

"I have a coin," said Bocchi at last. Her voice was almost nothing. "Everyone who stayed has one. I could— one of them. I could bring one of them." She pressed her hands flat on the poster. "I'm not. I'm not going to. If I bring one, the others—" She stopped. "The others would be at the club every night instead. Looking for two of us." Her voice broke. "I'm *holding* it. I'm holding it. Nijika told you. I can't—"

"Good," said Seika.

Bocchi's head came up.

Seika was still not looking at her. She was looking at the soup-pot drum with her arms folded and her face flat.

"You're thinking about all four of them," said Seika. "Not just the one you miss most tonight." She paused. "That's right. That's what you do. You hold it until you can do it properly." She unfolded her arms, and leaned forward, and for the first time looked straight at Bocchi. "And in the meantime, you practise. So that when they get here, you're good enough that your mother doesn't have to pretend."

Bocchi stared at her.

"She pretends?" said Bocchi, horrified.

"Every show," said Seika. "She's been pretending since your first one. Your father too. They're very good at it." The corner of her mouth moved, very slightly. "Don't make them pretend. Practise."

Bocchi made a sound that was half a sob and half a laugh and entirely Bocchi, and put her face down on the poster in her lap, and Seika reached out and put her hand on her head again, like a lid, and left it there until the others came back up the stair.



They played for her at six.

Not on a stage. There was no stage in the music room, only the corner with the soup-pot drum and the stools. Seika stood at the back, by the door, with her arms folded, in the ugly coat. Exactly where Nijika had said she would stand, at the long table on the fortieth day. Exactly how.

They played the song from the snow. *Looked, Quiet*. A refuge child had named it, and Bocchi had written the first line: *Every morning, someone looks.* Kita sang. Ryo played the same low line she had been playing since the twenty-seventh day, and somewhere in the winter it had become the best thing in the song. Bocchi played with her eyes shut. Nijika drummed on a soup pot with a hide stretched over it and a pot lid for a cymbal, and kept time for all of them, steady, steady, the way she always did.

Seika did not move for the whole song.

At the end, there was silence. Kessoku looked at the back of the room. Kita was holding her breath. Nijika had her sticks crossed on the hide and was not looking at her sister, very deliberately, at all.

"Your timing dragged in the second verse," said Seika. "Nijika."

"I know," said Nijika.

"The pot lid's flat."

"I *know*."

Seika looked at the four of them for a long moment. At the soup pot. At the pot lid. At the guitars that had come over the bridge, and the bass, and the music room with no stage in a house in a world with no electricity, where four girls had been playing for each other, all winter, in the dark.

"That wasn't bad," said Seika.

Nijika's sticks clattered onto the floor.

She did not pick them up. She sat behind the soup pot with her hands over her face, and her shoulders went, and Kita put down her guitar and went to her. And Seika stood at the back of the music room with her arms folded and her face completely flat and did not come forward, and did not say anything else. But she did not leave either.



And then, because it was the hundred-and-second night, and nobody in the house could think of anything else to do with the size of what they were feeling, they took her to the movie room.

"It's a lantern show," Nijika explained, on the way, slightly frantic. "Senku made a projector. Like a magic lantern. Painted glass. And we play. Live. And Okarun narrates. It's—" She stopped. "You're going to say it's stupid."

Seika looked into the long low room with its three steps of cushions and its white wall and its lantern on the top step, and seventy people already crammed in, waiting, turning round to look at her.

"It's a stupid little lantern show," said Seika, "in a shed."

Nijika looked at Mau across the room.

Mau looked back.

They put out the lamps. Senku lit the wick. Nothing broke. The chromatrope opened on the white wall, red and gold and blue, turning, and seventy people breathed out, and Bocchi played, and Seika stood at the back by the door with her arms folded in the ugly coat and watched.

She watched the chromatrope. She watched Okarun's terrible film about aliens, all twelve slides, with Okarun narrating from the floor in a voice of total sincerity and Kita singing a love theme that was not in the film and Nijika hitting the pot lid every time the woman screamed. She watched the flying saucer rock across the wall on Wakana's lever, while seventy people screamed. She watched the alien go home.

She did not laugh once. Mau checked. Her face did not change at all, for the whole thing, in the dark, at the back, by the door.

At the end, when the last slide went up, the saucer rising into a sky of painted stars, and Nijika played a drum roll on the soup pot and Kita held one long note, there was the usual pause. Then the usual seventy people stood up in the dark and began to applaud.

And through the applause, from the back of the room, flat and clear and perfectly audible, came Seika's voice.

"Again," said Seika. "The alien one."

The movie room went completely silent.

Seventy people turned round in the dark and looked at the tall woman by the door with her arms folded in the ugly coat, whose face had not changed at all.

"What?" said Seika. "The narration was terrible. I want to see if it's terrible every time."

On the floor at the front, Okarun burst into tears.

Then the whole room was laughing, and shouting, and Senku was scrambling for the first slide of the alien film, and Umaru was standing on a cushion shrieking *THE MINISTRY GRANTS AN ENCORE*. And through all of it, in the light of the lantern, Mau saw Nijika at the front by the soup-pot drum with her sticks in her hand, turning round to look at her sister at the back of the room. She was laughing so hard she could not stand up.

*She'd stand at the back of this room with her arms folded,* Nijika had said, on the ninety-sixth night, *and say it was a stupid little lantern show in a shed.*

*And then she'd stay for all of it. And ask for the alien one again.*

They showed it four more times. Seika stayed for all of them.
