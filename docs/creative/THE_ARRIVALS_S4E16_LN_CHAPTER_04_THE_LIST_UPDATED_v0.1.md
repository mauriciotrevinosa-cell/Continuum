*When: The night of Day 102 into the morning of Day 103.*

Seika did not sleep in the Muslin.

Rem had made up the bed there, the best one, in the old east room, with the warmest blankets, and kept the stove lit for two days. At the end of the hundred-and-second night, when the alien film had been shown five times and the movie room had emptied in a long laughing line into the cold, Nijika took her sister across the yard to see it.

Seika stood in the doorway of the old east room of the Muslin and looked at the bed, and the stove, and the folded blankets, and the small jar of dried flowers Rem had put on the windowsill.

"It's across a yard," said Seika.

"It's the guest house now," said Nijika. "It's nice. Rem made it up. It's the best bed in—"

"It's across a yard," said Seika, "from you."

Nijika stopped.

Rem, who had come across with them carrying a lamp, looked at the tall woman in the doorway for a long moment. Then she smiled, very slightly, and went past her into the room, and opened the little door of the stove, and let the fire go out.

"There's a spare mattress," said Rem, "in the cellar. Under the main stair. Wakana keeps it for whoever comes." She closed the stove. "It will fit beside Nijika's bed. If Kita moves her guitar."

Seika looked at her.

"Thanks," she said, after a moment. Gruffly. Not looking at anyone. "For the—" She gestured at the room, the blankets, the flowers. "Thanks."

"It was nothing," said Rem.

"It was two days of stove."

"Yes," said Rem, and smiled properly, and carried the lamp out.

So Seika slept on a spare mattress on the floor of Kessoku's room in the real house, beside Nijika's bed, with Kita's guitar moved to the corner and Ryo asleep across the room with her mouth open and Bocchi through the wall with the door between them left open a crack.

Or she lay there. She did not sleep.



She had not slept for three days, and then for a fourth, and now that she was lying down on a mattress in a warm room with her sister breathing a foot away, she found that she could not.

She lay on her back and looked at the ceiling. Clay, washed white. A beam across it, pegged, no nails. Somewhere a long way off, a bell rang the hour, once. She did not know which hour. She did not know anything about this place. She did not know where she was, or how, or what the voice had been, or what *you are sure* had really meant, or whether there was any way back, or whether she wanted one.

She knew that Nijika was breathing a foot away. She turned her head and looked at her, in the dark. Nijika was asleep on her side with one hand hanging off the edge of the bed, toward the mattress, toward her, the way she had slept when she was six, and had had nightmares, and had come into Seika's room in the middle of the night and fallen asleep on the floor beside the bed with one hand on the blanket.

Seika looked at the hand for a long time.

"Seika-san."

It was barely a voice. It came through the door between the two rooms, the one that had been left open a crack, out of the dark on the other side.

"You're awake," said Seika.

"I don't sleep much." A pause. "Sorry."

Seika looked at the crack. Nothing showed in it. Only dark.

"The man on the veranda," she said. "This afternoon. I told him off."

Silence from the crack. Then, very small: "I know. Kita told me." Another pause. "Nijika laughed for ten minutes."

"Everybody looks at him," said Seika. "All day. Every time anything happens." She looked at the ceiling. "I want to know why."

The silence went on so long that Seika thought the girl on the other side had lost her nerve, the way she lost it at counters, and in doorways, and on every stage she had ever stood on.

Then Bocchi talked.

She talked the way she could only talk in the dark, to a door, with nobody looking at her face: all at once, too fast, without stopping, as if the words would go back inside if she let them rest.

"There was a man called Sukuna," she said. "A long time ago. Before the Muslin. Before any of this. He wasn't a man. He looked like one. He cut a whole street in half and didn't touch it." Her breath went in, shaky. "And he came at us. The four of us. We were right there in the street, and I couldn't move, I couldn't— none of us could move." Her voice went very thin. "And Mau-san went in front."

"In front," said Seika.

"Of us. Of me." Something rustled in the dark, as if she had put her hand flat on her own chest. "There were stronger people there. He wasn't the strongest. He just went first. Because we were the closest." A pause. "Its arm went through him. Here. He didn't wake up for three days. We sat outside his door the whole time. Nobody thought he would."

Seika lay very still.

"And something came with it," said Bocchi. "Into his shadow. From Sukuna. It isn't him. It's the thing people are scared of, when they're scared of him. He's the one holding it." She stopped, and started again. "And after that there was a Trial. I don't know what happened in it. Nobody does. He doesn't talk about it." A pause. "He came out, and he went in front again. He always goes in front. Every time. Even when it isn't his turn. Even when it hurts him."

She was quiet for a moment.

"He's like a brother," said Bocchi. "Everybody's. Even mine, a bit, and I can't even talk to him." Her voice wobbled. "They don't look at him because of what he can do. They look at him because he's already done all of it. For all of us. Over and over. They look to see if he's all right."

The room was very quiet. Ryo turned over in her sleep. Nijika's hand hung off the edge of the bed, toward the mattress.

"I told him not to do it again," said Seika at last.

"He will," said Bocchi.

"I know." Seika looked at the dark crack in the door. "I could tell. Standing there with his tea." She was quiet for a moment. "Go to sleep."

"You too."

Bocchi's breathing on the other side of the door went slow, at last, a long time later. Seika lay and listened to it.

Then she got up, very quietly, and put on the ugly coat, and went out into the corridor.



The corridor was dark. There were small glyph lights set into the wall at long intervals, Coco's work, shadowless and very faint, just enough to see the doors by. Seika walked along it in her socks, slowly, with her hands in the pockets of the coat, looking at the doors.

Each door had a slip of card beside it, at eye height, with a name. In ink. *BOCCHI*, on the door with the thick wall. *MAI.* *YUTA / MAKI.* At the very end, the east end, a door with *MAU / FRIEREN* beside it and, under that, in Fern's hand, smaller: *Do not knock after dark unless something is on fire.* She stood in front of that one a little longer than the others. She went round the end, into the other corridor. *ORI / MILIM.* *FERN.* *STARK*, with a dotted line drawn very lightly between his card and Fern's. *UMARU / SUIKA.* A door. *COCO / TETIA / AGOTT / (RICHEH).* *QIFREY.* *ANKO / NAZUNA.* *OKARUN.* And beneath it, on a tiny separate card, at knee height, by the windowsill, *TURBO GRANNY (separate address).* She stopped and looked at that one for some time.

At the turn of the corridor, a narrow door. The slip beside it said *REM.* In pencil. Every other name she had passed was in ink. This one was in pencil, in a different, rounder hand, and somebody had very carefully not gone over it.

She did not know why that was. She looked at it, and then she walked on.

*KANEKI.* *AIRA.* The cellar stair, going down into the dark.

And at the very end of the corridor, a door with no slip beside it at all.

It was a plain door, like the others. But there was a lamp hanging on a hook beside it, a small brass one, lit, turned low. And under the door, along the floor, there was a line of light, warm and yellow, much brighter than the lamp.

Seika stood in front of it for a long time.

Then she put her hand on the latch, and opened it.



It was a library.

That was the first thing. The second thing was that it was too big. The door had been in the end wall of a corridor on the upper floor of a timber house, and the room beyond it should have been a small room, the size of the others, with a window. It was not. It went back. Shelves, floor to ceiling, dark wood, crammed with books, going back and back into a soft yellow light that did not come from any lamp she could see, much further than the house went, much further than anything went. The air smelled of paper and dust and, very faintly, tea.

Just inside the door, there was a chair. A deep old armchair, worn soft, with a blanket folded over one arm. Beside it, a small table, with a lamp on it, lit. And on the table, a single book, closed, waiting.

In a second chair, further in, across a little rug, a woman in black with milk-white hair was reading.

She did not look up at once. She finished her page. Then she looked up, over the top of the book, at Seika in the doorway in her socks and the ugly coat, with that sharp, total interest that had made a goddess's coin feel, for a moment, very small.

"Ah," said Echidna. "You are the first."

Seika did not move.

"The first what?"

"The first stranger," said Echidna, "who is not a stranger." She closed her book on one finger. "Who cannot sleep. Who walks down a corridor in a strange house at night, looking for something." She tilted her head. "I said there would be a door at the end of it. With a lamp. And a chair." She glanced at the armchair. "There is."

"I'm not a stranger," said Seika. "I'm her sister."

"Yes," said Echidna. "That is what I meant."

Seika looked at her for a long time. At the black robe, and the milk-white hair, and the eyes, which were very old, and very curious, and not unkind. Then past her, at the shelves going back and back into the soft yellow light, much further than the house went.

"This shouldn't fit," said Seika.

"No," Echidna agreed.

"I run a live house in Shimokitazawa," said Seika. "I've seen a lot of things at four in the morning." She paused. "This is the first one that shouldn't fit."

The faint smile came at the corner of Echidna's mouth.

"Sit," she said. "Or do not. It is your chair. I only keep the books."

Seika stood in the doorway a moment longer. Then she came in, and sat down in the deep old armchair, slowly, the way you sit down in a chair you are expecting to be uncomfortable, and found that it was not. She put her hands on the arms. She looked at the book on the little table beside her.

"Is that for me?"

"It is for whoever sits there," said Echidna. "Tonight, that is you."

Seika picked it up. It was not large. The cover was plain dark cloth, with nothing written on it. She opened it.

It was music.

Not a book about music. Music. Pages and pages of it, written by hand, in a notation she did not recognize at first and then, slowly, did. Staves. Clefs. Notes. Not from her world. The shapes were wrong, older, stranger, but they were notes. Somebody, somewhere, a very long time ago, in some other place, had written songs down, by hand, carefully, and this was the book.

Seika turned the pages slowly.

"Where's it from?"

"A very long way away," said Echidna. "From someone who is not anywhere anymore." She went back to her own book. "I read it once. I keep everything I read." A pause. "I could not hear it. I do not play. I have always wondered what it sounded like."

Seika looked at the page in front of her. She read the first line of notes, slowly, with her eyes, and her fingers moved on the arm of the chair, very slightly, the way a guitarist's fingers move when she is reading. She had not played in years. Not since the club. Not since she had put down her guitar, a long time ago, and built a basement stage out of pallets so that her little sister would have somewhere to play.

Her fingers moved on the arm of the chair.

"It's in a minor key," she said quietly. "It's sad. Then the middle goes up." Her fingers moved. "It's a love song, I think. Somebody waiting for somebody."

Echidna looked up from her book.

She did not say anything. She only looked at the tall tired woman in the armchair with the book of a dead person's songs open in her lap, whose fingers were moving on the arm of the chair, and something came into her old curious face that was very close to hunger and very close to something else.

"Go on," said Echidna softly.

Seika read. Her fingers moved. She told Echidna, very quietly, in the soft yellow light, what each song sounded like, as well as she could. A song for a harvest. A song for a child. A song that was obviously for drinking, and was very rude, which made Echidna laugh, once, a short surprised sound that she seemed not to have expected from herself. A song for someone who had died.

Somewhere around the seventh song, Seika's voice slowed down. Somewhere around the eighth, it stopped.

Echidna looked up.

The tall woman was asleep in the armchair, with her head tipped against the wing of it and the book open on her chest, under one hand, and her mouth a little open, and the shadows under her eyes very dark. Her fingers had stopped on the arm of the chair, in the middle of a chord.

Echidna looked at her for a long time.

Then she got up, which she did not often do, and crossed the little rug, and took the folded blanket off the arm of the chair, and shook it out, and laid it over Seika, up to her chin, very carefully, so as not to move the book.

She went back to her own chair. She did not pick up her own book. She sat and looked at the sleeping woman, and the dead person's songs on her chest, and the lamp, for the rest of the night, with the soft yellow light going back and back behind her into a room that should not fit.

"Mine," she said, very quietly, to nobody. She did not mean the book.



Nijika woke at dawn and found the mattress empty.

The house heard her. Everyone agreed on that afterward. It was not a scream, exactly. It was the sound of someone who has spent a very long time in a world without her sister, and one day with her, and has woken up to find the place on the floor beside her bed empty. And who, for one second, before she is properly awake, believes that the day was the dream.

She was in the corridor before anyone else had opened a door. Kita was behind her. Ryo was behind Kita, with her hair on end. Bocchi was in her own doorway, white. Doors were opening all down the corridor. Mau was out of the east room with Frieren behind him, and Ori in her doorway, and Milim floating out over Ori's head, half awake, saying *what what what*.

Nijika ran.

She did not know where she was running. She ran down the corridor and round the turn, past Rem's pencil door, past Kaneki and Aira, past the cellar stair, to the end of the corridor, where a small brass lamp still hung, lit, beside a door with no name. The door was open a hand's width. Soft yellow light came out of it across the floor.

Nijika stopped in front of it, breathing hard.

Then she pushed it open.

Seika was asleep in the armchair, just inside the door, under a blanket, with a book open on her chest and her mouth open and her head tipped against the wing of the chair. She was snoring, very slightly.

Across the little rug, Echidna looked up from her book.

"She could not sleep," said Echidna. "So she came to the end of the corridor." A pause. "As I said."

Nijika stood in the doorway. Behind her, half the corridor had come running and stopped, in a crowd, in nightclothes, looking over her shoulders into a library that should not fit.

Nijika did not say anything. She went in, and knelt down on the floor beside the armchair, and put her head down on the arm of it, next to her sister's hand, and closed her eyes.

Seika, without waking, moved her hand off the book and put it on top of Nijika's head. Like a lid.

In the doorway, Kita began, very quietly, to cry. Behind her, Ryo put her hand over Kita's mouth, gently, so as not to wake anyone. Behind them, the whole corridor backed away, one person at a time, in total silence, and went back to bed.



At breakfast, Rimuru said *looked, quiet*, and the table breathed out, and Senku's chalk squeaked on the board.

Then Fern opened the ledger.

She read the day, the way she read every day. "Day one hundred and two. Arrival House opened at the eighth bell. At the eleventh bell—" She stopped. She looked down at the page. She started again. "Intake, one. By coin. Terms, to follow." A pause. "Arrivals."

She stopped again.

The common room waited.

"Arrivals, one," said Fern.

She did not say anything else for a moment. Nobody did. Down the long table, Seika was sitting between Nijika and Kita in the ugly coat, eating porridge, with a crease on one cheek from the wing of an armchair, looking at her bowl. She did not look up.

Then Fern turned the ledger to the page from the fortieth day.

*THE LIST.*

*1. Nijika: her sister. First.*

*2. When we're ready.*

She dipped her pen. Beside the first line, after *First*, she wrote one word, in ink. Then she turned the ledger round on the table so that everyone could see it.

*1. Nijika: her sister. First. HERE.*

Nijika put her spoon down.

Seika looked at the ledger. At the line. At her own name, which was not on it, because the line did not need it. She looked at it for a long time with her face completely flat. Then she looked at her porridge again.

"Your handwriting's very neat," she said to Fern.

"Thank you," said Fern.

"Hers is terrible."

"I know," said Fern.

Nijika, beside her sister, laughed, and put her face in her hands.



There was the second line.

*2. When we're ready.*

Fern looked at it. Everyone looked at it. The house had been looking at it since the fortieth day: *when we're ready*, under the first line, which was all anyone had been willing to write.

At the far end of the table, by the teapot, Rem put the teapot down.

She did it very carefully, the way she did everything. She set it on its stand. She turned the handle so it pointed toward Momo, who would want it next. She folded her hands in her lap.

"Ram," said Rem.

The common room went quiet.

She had said the name before. At the table, on the fortieth day, in front of everyone. She did not say anything else now either. She only sat very straight at the end of the long table with her hands folded, and looked at the ledger, and waited.

Fern looked at her for a long moment.

Then she dipped the pen, and under *2.*, after *When we're ready*, she wrote.

*2. Rem: her sister. When we're ready.*

She did not turn the ledger round this time. She did not need to.

Down the table, Seika had looked up from her porridge. She was looking at Rem. At the girl with blue hair and the maid's apron and the folded hands, who had made up the best bed in the guest house and kept the stove lit for two days and let it go out.

"Another sister," said Seika.

"Yes," said Rem.

"Older or younger?"

"Older," said Rem. "By a little."

Seika considered this.

"Let me guess," she said. "She'll hate it here."

Rem thought about it seriously.

"She will say," said Rem, "that the house is crude, and the food is barely edible, and the fort is a disgrace, and everyone in it is an idiot." She paused. "Except me."

Seika looked at her for a long moment.

"I like her already," said Seika.

Rem smiled. It was not her usual small smile. It was much larger than that, and it went all the way up into her eyes, and she looked down at her folded hands so that nobody would see it, and everybody saw it.



That afternoon, in the hall of Arrival House, the register girl came out from behind her table and went to the board.

It hung on its hooks on the wall by the door, where it had hung since the fifty-ninth day, the new side out. *MATERIAL. WHERE. HOW MUCH. WHO. BACK.* Stone, Okarun. Clay, Aira and Momo. Lime, Mikasa and Eren. Timber, crossed through, *DONE*, in Wakana's hand, with a small drawing of a ridge beam.

She took it down. It was heavy, and she was not tall. One of the refuge Searchers came over from the door and took the other end without being asked. A woman who had come in through Arrival House herself the week after Last Defense, found by a team that had gone out looking, one of the very few of the refuge who had stayed when the window closed.

Together they turned the board round.

The old side. *ROUTE. TEAM. OUT. BACK. FOUND.* The white paint under *FOUND* worn through to the wood, rubbed out and written over so many times by so many hands that the grain showed. The last name in it, faint, half erased, from before the voice.

The refuge woman took the chalk from the ledge.

She looked at the columns for a long moment. *ROUTE.* She wrote, after a pause, *COIN.* *TEAM.* She wrote *NIJIKA.* *OUT.* She wrote *DAY 101, MIDNIGHT.* *BACK.* *DAY 102, ELEVENTH BELL.*

Then *FOUND.*

Her hand stopped over the worn wood. She was crying, a little, without making any sound, the way the people who had been found always cried at that column.

She wrote: *SEIKA.*

They hung the board back on its hooks. Old side out. Just for the day, the register girl said. Just for today.

Mau saw it that evening, crossing the plaza on his way back from the bath, with his hair wet and a towel round his neck. He stopped in the open door of Arrival House and looked at the board on the wall in the lamplight. At the old columns. At the one fresh line of chalk across the worn white paint. At the name under *FOUND.*

He stood there for a long time.

The last time he had stood in front of a board and felt his throat go tight like this, on the fortieth day, in front of the first line of the List, he had not known why.

He knew now.

"I told her a bit," said Nijika, behind him.

He had not heard her come. She was standing in the doorway of Arrival House with her hands in the pockets of her coat and her nose pink from the cold, looking past him at the board. At the line in chalk. At *COIN* and *NIJIKA* and *DAY 101, MIDNIGHT*. At her sister's name.

"Yesterday," said Nijika. "On the floor of the witness room. I was telling her everything at once. The bridge. The house. Everyone." She came and stood beside him, in front of the board. "And she'd been here an hour, and she'd already noticed that whenever anything happens, everybody looks at you." She laughed, not very well. "That's Onee-chan. She reads the room before she's through the door."

Mau did not say anything.

"So I told her," said Nijika. "That there was a Trial. That you nearly didn't come back from it. That something's wrong with you that isn't your fault, and we all carry it." She looked at the chalk. "And then I stopped."

"Why?"

Nijika did not answer at once.

"Because the next thing was the coin," she said. "Where it came from. What it was the end of." She was quiet for a moment. "I opened my mouth to tell her, and I thought: she's going to do the sum. She does sums. That's how she kept a club open for ten years. She's going to look at you across the table at breakfast and do the sum, every morning, for the rest of her life." Her hands had closed in her pockets. "So I stopped. And then I didn't know if I was allowed to stop. So I came to ask."

Mau looked at the board for a long time. At *FOUND*. At the white paint worn through to the grain by all the hands that had written in that column before the voice, and rubbed it out, and written again.

"Tell her about the Trial," he said. "If she asks. Tell her the coins came out of it. That they were what was waiting at the end." He paused. "That's true. That's enough."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is mine." He did not look away from the board. "I don't want her doing sums. I don't want anyone who comes through that door to sit down at the table owing something. Or feeling bad for being here." He was quiet for a moment. "She's not a debt. She's your sister. She came. That's all of it."

Nijika looked at him sideways for a long time, with her pink nose and her hands in her pockets.

"She'll work it out," she said. "Someday. She works everything out."

"Then she works it out," said Mau. "But not from you."

Nijika nodded, slowly.

"Bocchi told her about Sukuna," she said. "Last night. Through the door, in the dark. Kita heard them. The street, and you going in front." She almost smiled. "Bocchi. Talking. For ten minutes without stopping."

Mau did not answer at once. Something in his chest had gone tight and warm at the same time.

"That one's hers," he said. "She was there. She can tell it."

Then Nijika did a thing she had never done before. She leaned sideways and bumped her shoulder against his arm, once, hard, the way she bumped Bocchi on the way to the corner where they played, and said "Okay," in a voice that was not quite steady. Then she ran back across the plaza toward the lamps, because Kita was calling her name from the veranda.

Mau stood in front of the board a while longer. Then he went home. Across the plaza and the yard, past the ink tree, under the dripping eaves of the real house, through the door, into the long warm noisy common room where the whole house was arguing about something at the table. Seika was at the back, by the fort, with her arms folded in the ugly coat, pretending not to listen. Fern was writing in the ledger. Nijika was laughing.

Mau sat down in his place beside Frieren.

"Well?" said Frieren.

"She's on the board," said Mau. "Under *found*."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment.

Then she leaned her shoulder against his, the way she did, and did not say anything. And they sat at the long table in the real house together, in the noise, with the List on the page in front of Fern, two lines long, one of them done.

Down the table, Seiko had put her painted tin out on the board, which she had never once done in the Muslin, and taken the lid off. Nazuna, who had woken an hour before dinner the way she always did now, was eating her third cracker out of it and being glared at. Maki and Mai were arguing about nothing across it, the way they always did. And Kaneki, in from the timber yard for once at a reasonable hour, was sitting at the end of the bench beside Eren with a cracker in his hand that he was not eating, listening to the noise as if it were something he wanted to remember.



Nijika told the others over the next few days. Not at the long table. One at a time, the way the house passed on the things that mattered. Rem, at the teapot, whose name was on the next line. Yuta, on the roof. Umaru, in the toll booth, with the shutter down. Nobody asked her why. Everybody in the house had been there on the tenth day, when the coins came, and knew what they had been the end of.

Fern heard it last, late one night, at the head of the long table, with the ledger open in front of her. She listened. Then she put her pen down, and did not write it.

It was the only rule in the house that never went into the ledger.
