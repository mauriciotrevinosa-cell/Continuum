*When: Day 104, two days after Seika came. Rem's name sits second on the List. The weekly tea, the bath, the movie nights and the washing court mornings all go on, and the snow in the north field has not started to melt.*

Mau had carried the coin for ninety-four days, and he had never once held it the way you were supposed to.

He knew how. Everyone did now. Nijika had sat on the floor of the music room with it closed in her fist and thought of her sister, and her sister had come. You held it. You thought of the person. That was all. Ciel had said it was the simplest thing the Goddess had ever made.

He kept it in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left. He had put it there on the tenth night, on the bridge, when every stayer had felt one appear in their palm at sunset. He had felt it there every day since, small and warm against his ribs, through the cold days and the ugly days and the snow, the way you feel a stone in your shoe. He took it out sometimes, at night, and looked at it. Then he put it back.

He had never closed his hand round it and thought of someone. He had told himself it was because the house came first. One at a time, Fern said, and Nijika was first, and he was not going to jump the List.

That was not the reason. He knew it was not the reason.



On the hundred-and-fourth evening, he went out to the ink tree alone.

The day had been loud. Seika had spent it being shown the house again, more slowly this time, by Nijika, and by Kita, and by the fort in a body, and by Himmel, who had given her a tour of his two vermilion columns that lasted forty minutes. At dinner she had sat between Nijika and Bocchi in the ugly coat, eating Momo's stew in silence, and said at the end of it, to nobody, "It's not bad," and Momo had had to leave the room.

It had been a good day. He had watched it all day from a little distance, smiling, and somewhere in the afternoon, without deciding to, he had put his hand on the inside pocket of his coat and left it there.

The ink tree stood in its circle at the middle of the yard, in the dusk, with its great silver-green crown moving very slightly in a wind he could not feel. Under it, the snow had gone, and the ground was dark and soft. Somebody had put a bench round the trunk, a ring of planks on short legs, the week after the tree came. Wakana, probably. No nails.

Mau sat down on it, with his back to the trunk, and took out the coin.

It lay in his palm, warm, the way a hand is warm. There was nothing on either face. He had looked at it a hundred times in ninety-four days, and it had never once looked any different. It did not look different now.

He closed his hand round it.

He closed his eyes.

He tried to think of someone from before.



He had done this before, without the coin. Of course he had. Everyone who had come over the bridge did it, sooner or later, at night, lying awake: tried to go back, in their head, to before. To a kitchen. A street. A face. Most of them could. They lay awake and cried for faces they could see perfectly.

Mau had never been able to.

His life began in a clearing. It had always begun there. He had woken on his back in long grass with the sky above him, and turned his head, and there had been a face, and he had known it before he knew his own name. Pale hair. Green eyes. Pointed ears. A voice asking him, very calmly, whether he was alive.

Before that, nothing.

Not darkness. Not a door that would not open. Just nothing, the way there is nothing before the first page of a book.

He had a hoodie. Orange and dark blue, with the name of a racing team on it, from a year he could not place, in a world he could not remember. He had a red shirt. He knew how to make the old movie machine work, and how to do long division, and the shape of a car that went faster than anything, without knowing how he knew. Somebody had given him the hoodie, probably. Somebody had taught him long division. There must have been someone. There was always someone.

He sat under the ink tree with the coin in his fist and his eyes shut and reached for them.

A kitchen. A street. A face.

He reached, and reached, and there was nothing there to reach. No kitchen. No street. Only the clearing, and the long grass, and the sky, and her face turning toward him, the first thing.

The coin was warm in his hand.

Nothing happened.

He sat there for a long time. The dusk went blue, and then darker. The lamps came on in the real house, one by one, behind him. Somebody laughed in the common room. Somebody was playing a guitar, slowly, the song from the snow. He sat under the tree with his eyes shut and his hand closed and kept reaching, past the clearing, into nothing, until his hand ached and his eyes stung and he understood, finally, the thing he had been not understanding for ninety-four days.

There was nobody to call.

Not because they were gone. Not because they would say no. There was simply nobody on the other side of the clearing for the coin to find. Whoever had given him the hoodie, whoever had taught him long division, whoever there must have been, he had nothing of them. Not a name. Not a face. Not the shape of a hand. You could not call someone you could not think of.

He opened his eyes.

He opened his hand.

The coin lay on his palm, warm.

"Okay," he said, out loud, to nobody, to the tree. His voice came out rough. "Okay."



He did not hear her come. He never did.

"You're sitting in the dark," said Frieren.

She was standing at the edge of the root circle, in her shawl, with two cups. She did not come closer. She looked at him, and at the coin in his open hand, and at his face, which he had not had time to do anything about.

"I was trying," said Mau.

Frieren did not say anything. She came across the root circle and sat down beside him on the bench round the trunk, and gave him one of the cups, and held the other in both hands, and looked at the coin.

"There's nobody," said Mau. "I tried. I thought, maybe if I held it. Maybe the coin would know something I don't. Maybe it would find someone even if I couldn't." He looked at it. "It didn't. There's nobody there. Before the clearing, there's nobody."

"There's you," said Frieren.

"There's not even me." He laughed, a little. It did not come out well. "I don't know who I was. I don't know who gave me the hoodie. I don't know if anyone's looking. If there's someone sitting in a dark room somewhere, three days after, putting up posters." He stopped. "And I can't even think of them. I can't even miss them properly. I don't know what to miss."

Frieren did not tell him it was all right. She did not tell him it was not his fault. She had learned, a long time ago, in the corridor, in the snow, in a great many places, that those were not the things he needed.

She leaned her shoulder against his and drank her tea.

They sat there for a long time.

"I've watched a lot of people," said Frieren at last, "lose the people from before. Over a thousand years." She looked up at the crown of the ink tree, silver-green against the dark. "Most of them lost them slowly. Faces going. Voices going. They'd wake up one day and not remember what their mother's laugh sounded like, and grieve that too." She paused. "You lost yours all at once. Before you ever knew you had them." She was quiet for a moment. "That's not less. It's only different."

Mau looked down at the coin.

"I've lost teachers," said Frieren. "And friends." She turned her cup in her hands. "With some of them, I didn't understand what I'd lost until a very long time after." She paused. "It's all right not to know what to miss. Sometimes you only find out later." She looked at the coin in his hand. "Sometimes you find out you were missing it all along, and it was right next to you."

Mau did not say anything.

He closed his hand round the coin again. Not trying, this time. Only holding it.



And then, from no direction, very quietly, a voice spoke.

He knew it. He had heard it a great many times. On the bridge. In the Trial, four hundred and thirty-seven times, before it said *settlement protection withdrawn*. On the forty-eighth night, telling the whole settlement that the world was evolving. It was not loud and it was not soft. It came to him under the ink tree and to nowhere else.

Frieren did not move. She did not hear it. But she felt him go still, and she put her hand over his, over the coin.

*As you cannot bring a loved one,* said the voice, *I will give something that benefits you in the long run.*

Then nothing.

The wind moved in the crown of the ink tree. In the common room, the guitar played on, slowly. A door closed somewhere in the north wing.

Mau sat very still.

"What did she say?" said Frieren quietly.

He told her. Word for word. He did not know what else to do with it.

Frieren was quiet for a long time.

"*In the long run,*" she said at last, thoughtfully, the way she said things she was going to think about for a hundred years.

Then she looked down at their hands. At his fist, closed round the coin, with her hand over it.

"Open it," she said.

He opened it.

The coin was still there.

It lay on his palm in the dark under the ink tree, exactly as it had been. Small. Faceless. And warm. Not cold. Not dull. Warm, the way a hand is warm, the way it had been every day for ninety-four days against his ribs.

Mau stared at it.

"She didn't take it," he said.

"No," said Frieren.

"She said— she gave something. She said *I will give.* She didn't take the coin."

"No." Frieren looked at it. "It's still warm."

"Then what did she give?"

Frieren did not answer. She did not know. Neither of them knew. They sat under the ink tree in the dark with the coin on his palm between them, warm, and looked at it.

"Then it's still yours," said Frieren at last. "To keep. Or to give." She closed his fingers over it with her own, gently. "Someday, maybe, to someone who has more than one person on the other side of the clearing, and only one coin."

Mau looked at her.

She did not say a name. She did not need to. There were people in the house who had more than one person on the other side and only one coin. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it.

He put the coin back in the inside pocket of his coat, on the left, against his ribs. It was warm there. It had always been warm there.

"I thought it would hurt more," said Mau. "Finding out there's nobody."

"Does it hurt?"

"Yes." He thought about it. "But not the way I thought." He looked across the dark yard at the lit windows of the real house. At the shapes moving behind them. At the common room, where somebody was laughing, and somebody was playing the song from the snow, and Seika was probably standing at the back with her arms folded. "I kept thinking, if I used it, who would I call. And I couldn't think of anyone." He paused. "And then I thought, if I could call anyone, anyone at all, from anywhere, who would I want here." He looked at the windows. "And they're already here. All of them. Every one."

Frieren did not say anything.

"Maybe that's what she meant," said Mau. "*As you cannot bring a loved one.*" He swallowed. "Maybe it's not that I can't remember them. Maybe it's that they're all already in the house."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment in the dark.

Then she leaned over and kissed him, very lightly, on the temple, which she did not often do where anyone might see, and there was nobody to see.

"Idiot," she said softly.

"Yeah."

"Drink your tea. It's cold."

He drank it. It was cold.

They went in together. Across the dark yard, under the dripping eaves, into the noise of the common room, where Seika was indeed standing at the back by the fort with her arms folded, and the song from the snow was on its third verse, and nobody asked where they had been.



That night, Anko walked the edge three times, as she always did.

On the third round, a little before dawn, past the last cottage, at the place in the north field where the huge tracks had once turned back forty paces from the line, she stopped.

She stood there for a long time with her lantern raised.

Then she took out her notebook, and wrote, under that night's date, in her small precise hand:

*North field. Just past the line. Something new.*

She underlined *new*, and went to wake Rimuru.
