# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 23
## Chapter 2 — The Gate

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 23  
**Reading order:** 2  
**Continuity state:** Day 105 | a blue gate forty paces past Anko's red stake, outside the protection on purpose | Ciel: a built door, a space larger than the settlement, 'has teeth' | Mau: what his coin bought, without spending it | Frieren: 'Dungeons... chests... grimoires'; one step past the stake | Senku's pole: limestone, cave air | Fern: nobody goes in yet | Umaru's sign: DOES NOT AUTHORIZE ENTRY (YET.)

*When: Day 105, the next dawn. Seika's first week in the house goes on around everything that follows.*

Diablo let Anko in without a word, which told Rimuru everything before she opened her mouth.

It was not yet dawn. The office beside the common room was dark except for the coals in the little stove. Rimuru was asleep in his chair at the desk with his cheek on a ledger page, in human shape, because he had been drawing the west wing until midnight and had not made it to the loft. He woke when the door opened and sat up with the page stuck to his face.

Anko stood in the doorway with her lantern and her notebook.

"North field," she said. "Just past the line. Forty paces." She held out the notebook, open, so he could read it by the lantern. *Something new.* "You need to see it. Now. Before the house wakes up."

Rimuru peeled the page off his cheek.

"Ciel?"

*I felt it at two,* said Ciel. *I have been looking since. I did not wake you.* A pause. *I wanted to be sure what it was first.*

"And?"

*I am not sure,* said Ciel. *That is why you need to see it.*



They did not manage to go before the house woke up.

It was not possible. Rimuru woke Mau, because Ciel said to, and Mau woke Frieren, because she was already awake and had been watching him sit up in bed and pull his boots on. Frieren's moving woke Ori through the door, and Ori woke Milim, who was floating about a foot above her own bed, and Milim, waking, said *WHAT* at a volume that woke the rest of the east corridor. By the time they came down the main stair, Mikasa was already standing at the bottom of it, dressed, with her blades on, and Eren behind her pulling on his coat. Yuta and Maki came out of their room. Senku came up from his workroom with chalk on his hands. Kaneki, coming in from the timber yard with sawdust on his sleeves, turned round in the doorway and followed them.

And Fern was in the common room in the grey dark, at the long table, with the ledger, because Fern was always in the common room at this hour. She looked up at all of them coming down the stair in a body, armed and half dressed, and closed the ledger, and stood up.

"Where?" she said.

"North field," said Anko.

Fern picked up the ledger and came.



The north field lay white and grey in the first light, the snow there still deep and crusted, because the thaw had not reached it yet. The path the night shift walked ran out across it from the last cottage, a dark trodden line, to the place where the huge tracks had once come down from the hills in the winter and stopped, forty paces from the line, and turned away. Everyone in the house knew that place now. Anko had planted a stake there, with a strip of red cloth tied to it, so the night shift would always know where the protection ended.

Past the stake, forty paces out, there was a light.

It was blue.

That was what Mau saw first, coming up the path behind Anko's lantern in the grey half-dark: a blue light in the field, ahead, standing up out of the snow, where nothing had been the day before. Not a fire. Not a lantern. A tall, narrow oval of blue light, taller than two men, standing on its end in the middle of the field. Its lower edge hung a hand's breadth above the snow, and the snow beneath it had melted in a perfect ring, down to the black earth.

They stopped at the red stake. Nobody had told them to. Nobody wanted to go past it.

Up close, the light moved. Its edges rippled, slowly, the way the surface of deep water ripples when something moves far beneath it. Its centre was darker, a deep shifting blue that seemed to go back a long way, much further than the field, much further than anything. It made a sound. Not loud. A low, steady hum, almost below hearing, that Mau felt in his teeth and in the soles of his feet and in the place behind his breastbone where the seal was.

Cold air came out of it. Not the cold of the field. A different cold, damp and still, that smelled of stone and water and very old dark places.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"Ciel," said Rimuru quietly.

*Looking,* said Ciel.

The house waited.

*It is not a seam,* said Ciel at last. *It is not like the window, or the bridge, or the Trial. It is not one of hers that I know.* A pause. *It is a door. Built. With a space behind it. The space is larger than this field. Larger than the settlement.* Another pause, longer. *And it is arranged. Inside. Rooms, and passages, and things that move. I cannot see them clearly. The door does not let me look far.*

"Hostile?" said Mikasa. She had not taken her eyes off it. Her hands were on her blades.

*Not the door,* said Ciel. *The door is only a door.* A pause. *What is behind it, I think, has teeth.*

Mikasa did not move her hands.

Milim, at the back, made a small involuntary sound of delight and was trodden on by Eren.

*There is one more thing,* said Ciel. *It is outside the line. Exactly. Forty paces past the place where her protection ends.* A pause. *That is not chance. Somebody put it here. On purpose. Just far enough out.*



Mau had not said anything since the stake.

He was standing at the front with Frieren beside him, looking at the blue light. The cold air from it moved his hair. The hum went through his teeth. And somewhere under the hum, in the place behind his breastbone where the seal was, something very small and very old had lifted its head and was listening, the way it listened to things it wanted.

He put his hand on the inside pocket of his coat. The coin was there. Warm.

"I know what it is," he said.

Everyone turned.

He told them. All of it. The ink tree, the evening before. The coin in his fist. Reaching, past the clearing, into nothing. Nobody there. And then the voice, from no direction, under the tree, to him and nowhere else.

*As you cannot bring a loved one, I will give something that benefits you in the long run.*

He took the coin out of his pocket and held it up, on his palm, in the blue light. Small. Faceless. Warm.

"She didn't take it," he said. "She gave this instead." He looked at the gate. "This is what my coin bought. Without spending it."

The north field was very quiet.

Fern was writing. She was standing at the back with the ledger open on her arm and her pen moving, the way she wrote down everything, so that it could be looked at later. She wrote the words of the voice, exactly, and underlined *in the long run*.

"A gift," said Eren slowly. He was looking at the gate with an expression Mau could not read. "Outside the line."

"On purpose," said Rimuru.

"Why outside?"

Rimuru did not answer at once. He was looking at the gate the way he had looked at the stone footings on the seventieth morning, the way a builder looks at ground.

"Because it's not safe," he said at last. "Because she knows what's inside it has teeth. And she wouldn't put teeth inside the line." He paused. "She protects the settlement. Not the land around it. Everything she's given us that's dangerous, she's put just outside." He looked at the red stake. "The road. The hills. Now this."

"Then why give it at all?" said Maki.

"*In the long run,*" said Frieren.

Everyone looked at her.

Frieren had not taken her eyes off the gate. She was standing very straight beside Mau in her shawl, and her face, in the blue light, was doing something Mau had seen it do only a few times. When she had first seen the ink tree in leaf. When Himmel walked into the common room for the first time. When she opened a grimoire she had never read.

"I know what that is," said Frieren.

"You *know*—"

"Not this one." She took a step forward, past the red stake, before anyone could stop her. One step. Then she stopped, and looked at it, from closer, with her head on one side. "But I know what it is. In my world there were places like this. Old places. Under mountains. Built by nobody knew who, full of rooms and passages and things that moved." Her voice had changed. "Dungeons."

"*Dungeons,*" said Milim, in a voice of total rapture.

"They were dangerous," said Frieren. "Very. People died in them all the time." She tilted her head the other way. "And at the bottom, or in the rooms along the way, there were always chests."

"Chests," said Mau.

"With things in them," said Frieren. "Treasure. Weapons. Old coins. Potions." She paused. "Grimoires."

There was a silence.

Fern, at the back, closed her eyes.

"Frieren-sama," said Fern.

"I'm only *looking*."

"You're past the stake."

Frieren looked down. She was one step past the red stake, outside the line, in the snow, in the blue light. She looked at her own feet, and then back at the gate, and then, very slowly, with enormous reluctance, she took one step back.

"I was only looking," she said again, with dignity.

Stark, beside Fern, was trying very hard not to laugh, and failing, and being glared at.



Senku stuck a pole in it.

Nobody stopped him. Nobody had thought to. He went back to the last cottage and came back with a long ash pole, the kind they used for knocking snow off the eaves, and walked out past the stake while everyone was still talking. Mikasa went after him at once, blades half drawn, and he held up a hand without turning round.

"I'm not going in," said Senku. "I'm doing *science*."

He held the pole out at arm's length and pushed the tip of it, very slowly, into the blue light.

It went in. Smoothly, without resistance, without a sound. The tip disappeared into the rippling blue as if into water, and then a foot of it, and then two feet. Senku held it there for the count of ten, with his lips moving. Then he pulled it out.

The end that had gone in was wet. Not with snow. With dew, fine and cold, beaded all along the wood. And there was something on the very tip, a grey smear, which Senku held up close to his eyes and then, before Fern could say his name, smelled.

"Stone dust," he said. "Wet stone. Limestone, maybe." He looked at the tip. "And it's colder in there than out here. A cave temperature. Constant." He looked at the gate with blazing eyes. "There's air in there. Breathable, probably. It's a real space. Physical." He turned round to the house. "It's ten billion percent a cave."

"It's a *dungeon*," said Frieren.

"It's a cave with *architecture*," said Senku. "Which is a dungeon, fine." He looked at the pole again. "I want to know who built it."

*So do I,* said Ciel.



Fern decided. Of course she did.

"Nobody goes in today," she said, at the red stake, with the ledger closed on her arm. Her voice was perfectly calm. "Nobody goes in tomorrow. Nobody goes in until we know what's in there, and how to get out again, and who goes, and what happens if they don't come back." She looked round at all of them. At Milim, vibrating. At Frieren, looking at the gate. At Senku with his wet pole. At Mikasa with her hands on her blades. "It's not going anywhere. She didn't put it here to disappear tomorrow. *In the long run,* she said." She paused. "So we take the long run."

Nobody argued. Even Milim did not argue. She looked at Fern, and then at the gate, and then at Rimuru, and said, in a small voice, "But *soon*?"

"Soon," said Rimuru. "Not today."

"Mikasa and I will take the first watch," said Eren. He had already taken off his coat and was spreading it on the snow at the stake, to sit on. "Here. At the line. Nothing comes out, nobody goes in."

"In shifts," said Fern.

"In shifts," Eren agreed.

Mikasa sat down beside him on the coat, in the snow, at the red stake, facing the blue light, with her blades across her knees. She did not say anything. But Mau saw her shoulder touch Eren's as she sat down, and stay there.

By the time the sun was fully up, the whole house had come out to the north field to look. The fort came in a body, in hoods. Kessoku came, with Seika in the ugly coat, who looked at the blue light for a long time with her arms folded and then said, flatly, "Of course," and nothing else. The witches came. Echidna stood at the red stake and looked at the gate with an expression of such pure, starving interest that Satella had to take her hand. Daphne looked at it and said, "There will be things to eat in there," and nobody could tell if she meant it hopefully. Sekhmet was carried out by Minerva and laid on the snow at the stake to look, and looked, and said, "Large," and went back to sleep.

Umaru made a sign.

She made it on a board from the timber yard, in red paint, very large, and planted it in the snow beside Anko's red stake, facing the gate. It said:

*THE MINISTRY OF FORT AFFAIRS DOES NOT AUTHORIZE ENTRY.*
*(YET.)*

Then she stood in front of it with her arms folded, beside Mikasa and Eren, for most of the morning, glaring at the gate, until Seiko sent Momo to bring her in for lunch.



That night, Mau stood at the east window of the real house, by the cars and Ori's stone, and looked out.

You could see it from there. Past the yard and the ink tree and the dark roofs of the last cottages, past the white of the north field, a small blue light, steady, standing up out of the snow. Like a candle someone had left in a window a long way off. Two smaller lights beside it, warm and yellow: the lantern at the red stake, where the second watch was sitting, Yuta and Maki tonight, on Eren's coat.

Frieren came and stood beside him.

"You're thinking about the coin," she said.

"I'm thinking about *in the long run*." He looked at the blue light. "She gave me something. Instead of someone. Something that helps, she said. Later." He paused. "And it's a hole in a field with teeth in it."

"Most good things are," said Frieren.

He looked at her.

She was looking at the blue light too. In the dark window, her face was reflected over it, faintly, the light sitting just where her eyes were. He could see her trying to keep her face perfectly composed. He could see her failing.

"You want to go in," said Mau.

"I'm a thousand years old," said Frieren. "I've been in a great many dungeons."

"You want to go in so badly."

There was a pause.

"There are going to be chests," said Frieren, very quietly, to the window, with an expression of perfect, serene, ominous innocence.

Mau looked at her for a long moment.

Then he started to laugh. He laughed until he had to sit down on the edge of the bed, with his face in his hands, while Frieren stood at the window with her arms folded and her ears going pink at the tips and told him it was not funny.

It was, a little.
