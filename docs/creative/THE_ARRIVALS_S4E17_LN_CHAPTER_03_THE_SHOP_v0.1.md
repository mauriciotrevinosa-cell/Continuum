# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 23
## Chapter 3 — The Shop

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 23  
**Reading order:** 3

*When: Day 106. Mikasa and Eren had kept the watch at the red stake all night, in shifts with Yuta and Maki, as they would every night from now on.*

The window appeared to Mikasa first, at dawn, when she stood up off Eren's coat to stretch her legs and took two steps toward the red stake.

She stopped dead.

Eren, still sitting, saw her stop. He saw her hands go to her blades. He was on his feet beside her before he had decided to stand, and then he stopped too, because it had appeared to him as well.

It hung in the air in front of them, at the height of a face, a little way past the stake. A flat rectangle of pale blue light, thin as paper, faintly glowing at the edges, with nothing holding it up. It had not been there a moment ago. There was writing on it.

Mikasa read it. Then she read it again. Then she said, without turning her head, in a perfectly level voice, "Eren. Go and get Fern."



By the time the house came, there were forty of them standing at the red stake in the dawn, and every one of them could see it.

That was the first thing they learned: anyone who came near enough saw it. Not at the cottages. Not at the start of the path. But somewhere on the path out across the north field, about twenty paces short of the stake, each of them, one by one, stopped walking and said *oh*. If you stepped back past that point, the window was gone. Step forward, and there it was, hanging in the air in front of your face, exactly as it had hung in front of Mikasa's. Everyone saw the same window, in the same place. Mau walked round it. From behind, it was only a faint blue haze, with nothing written on it at all.

The second thing they learned was that everyone could read it.

"It's in Japanese," said Okarun.

"It's not," said Himmel. "It's in the common script of the northern kingdoms. Very old-fashioned. Rather beautiful."

Mau looked at it, and could read every word, and could not have said what language it was in. Only that it was his.

They looked at each other.

"It's translating," said Senku, very softly. He had his face about six inches from the window, and his eyes were enormous. "It's translating for each of us. Individually. In real time." He reached out a finger and stopped just short of touching it. "That's not a sign. That's an *interface*. Somebody built an interface."

The third thing they learned was what it said.

At the top, in larger letters, one word: *SHOP.*

Under it, a row of smaller words, each in its own little box: *WEAPONS. ARMOUR. CONSUMABLES. MATERIALS. SPECIAL.*

Under that, a list, which changed when you looked at a different box. Under *WEAPONS*, it was a list of names, each with a small picture beside it, drawn in pale blue lines, and a number. *Hunter's Dagger, 120. Shortsword (Gate-forged), 300. Hand Axe (Gate-forged), 340. Spear, 280.* And on, down the window, further than it seemed to go, as if the window were deeper than it was tall.

And at the very bottom, in a separate box with a border round it:

*GATE: WEEK 1. GRADE: NORMAL. CHANGES IN: 6 DAYS.*

*SETTLEMENT BALANCE: 0 CRYSTALS.*

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Umaru, who had come out in her hood at the back of the fort, pushed her way through the crowd to the front, stood in front of the window, looked at it for about three seconds, and said, in a voice of utter, total recognition:

"*It's a shop menu.*"



It turned out that about a third of the house knew exactly what it was, and the other two thirds had no idea at all.

The third who knew were from worlds with games. Umaru, first and above all. Then Okarun, and Momo, and Marin, and Kita, and Hakari and Karane, who had very different opinions about which games, and Nano, who had already worked out the exchange rate, and Yuta, who had played a little, and Senku, who had played a great deal more than he admitted, and Seika, who looked at it with her arms folded and said, "It's a game menu," in the voice of someone who had seen a great many teenagers waste a great many hours, and Nijika, who said "*Onee-chan*," and was ignored, and Turbo Granny, from Okarun's shoulder, who said every shop was a scam and this one more than most.

The two thirds who did not know were everyone else. Frieren. Fern. Stark. Himmel. Mikasa and Eren, who had found it and still did not know what it was. Rimuru knew, a little, from before the slime, but had spent so long in a world where things like this were *real* that he looked at it the way Mikasa did, with his hands not quite at his sides. The Witch Hat girls. Maomao and Jinshi. The witches, who had come at the back and were looking at it with seven very different expressions.

"Explain," said Fern.

Umaru explained.

She stood at the front, beside the window, in her hood and her sash, and explained, with the absolute confident authority of someone who had spent ten thousand hours of her previous life doing exactly this on a couch with a bag of chips. A shop menu, she said, was where you spent the things you found in the dungeon. You went into the dungeon. You fought the monsters. The monsters dropped things. Usually crystals, or coins, or bits of themselves. You came out. You came to the shop. You bought better weapons. Better armour. Potions. You went back in, deeper, with the better weapons, and fought harder monsters, which dropped more crystals, and you came out and bought even better weapons.

"And then?" said Fern.

"And then you go back in," said Umaru.

"And then?"

"And then you go back in *again*," said Umaru, with enormous patience. "That's the game, Fern."

Fern looked at the window.

"That's not a game," she said. "That's an economy."

Umaru opened her mouth. Then she closed it. Then, slowly, she looked at Fern with an expression of dawning respect.

"...Yeah," said Umaru. "Yeah, it's both."



They spent the whole morning at the window, taking turns.

Senku took the longest turns. He went through every box. He made Suika write down everything, every name and every number, in the notebook she carried for him. Under *CONSUMABLES*: *Stamina Draught, 80. Antidote (Common), 60. Ration (Gate Issue), 15. Rope (50 ft), 20.* Under *ARMOUR*: things with names like *Leather Vest (Reinforced)* and *Bracers (Gate-forged)*. Under *MATERIALS*: *Gate Iron (ingot), 200.* Senku stared at that one for a full minute. Under *SPECIAL*, almost everything was greyed out, faint and unreadable, with a small padlock drawn beside it.

"Locked," said Umaru. "You unlock those later. When you've cleared enough. Or found a key. Or something." She squinted. "Or when the grade goes up."

"*Gate Iron*," said Senku, still looking at the materials. "Two hundred crystals an ingot. Iron. From a *shop*." He turned to Suika. "Write down that the iron's called something special. Gate-forged. It's not normal iron. There's a reason it's not normal iron."

"Why?" said Suika.

"I don't know yet," said Senku happily. "That's the best part."

Umaru looked for snacks.

She went through every box, very thoroughly, twice. She found *Ration (Gate Issue)*, and looked at its little blue picture for some time. It was a grey brick.

"That's not a snack," said Umaru, betrayed.

"It's food," said Momo.

"It's a *brick*."

"It's fifteen crystals," said Fern, writing. "It's the cheapest thing on the list." She looked up. "Which means it's the thing we'll buy most of, if anyone ever goes in."

Umaru looked at the grey brick for a long time with an expression of profound personal grief, and then went to sit on the board of her own sign in the snow with her chin in her hands.

Then Maomao found the potions.

She found them under *CONSUMABLES*, near the bottom, where they had been hiding. Three lines, side by side, each with a little blue drawing of a small round bottle beside it. *Healing Draught (Minor). Healing Draught (Lesser). Healing Draught.*

All three were greyed out. Beside each one, where the price should have been, the same small line of text:

*NOT SOLD. OBTAIN FROM: BOSS.*

Maomao looked at it for a long time.

"Not sold," she said.

"You have to kill a boss," said Umaru, from her sign, without looking up. "A big monster. At the end of a floor. Or the end of the dungeon. They drop the good stuff." She paused. "Sometimes. Not always. Usually not."

Maomao looked at the three grey lines.

"And how strong," she said slowly, "is a *minor* healing draught?"

Nobody knew. The window did not say. Maomao reached out and, before anyone could stop her, touched the line with one finger.

A smaller window opened beside it, pale blue, with a few lines of text.

*HEALING DRAUGHT (MINOR). Restores a small amount of vitality. Closes shallow wounds. Will not mend bone. Will not restore what is lost.*

Maomao read it twice.

"A scratch," she said. "Maybe a cut." She stepped back from the window. "It's a scratch." Her face had gone very still, in the way it went still when she was thinking about something that mattered. "So nobody's going to walk in there and come out with a cure for anything. Good." She folded her hands in her sleeves. "Good. I'd hate to be put out of work."

But Mau saw her look at the three grey lines again, before she turned away. At *Healing Draught*, the last of the three, the one with no *minor* or *lesser* in front of it. He thought about her winter clinic, and the chilblains, and the cough the refuge children had had, and the bean.

He thought she would very much like to see one.



Frieren was disappointed.

She did not say so. She stood at the window for a long time, reading every box, with her head on one side and her face perfectly composed. Then she stepped back, past the point where it vanished, and came and stood beside Mau in the snow with her hands in the sleeves of her shawl.

"In my world," she said, "dungeons didn't have shops."

"No?"

"You went in. You found what you found. You came out." She looked at the place where the window was, which she could no longer see. "If you wanted a better weapon, you found one in a chest. Or you didn't." She paused. "It takes the fun out of it, a bit. Knowing what's for sale."

"There's no grimoires on the list," said Mau.

Frieren was quiet for a moment.

"No," she said. "There aren't." She looked at the gate, blue and humming, past the stake. "Which means if there are any," she said slowly, "they're in the chests."

Mau looked at her face, and decided not to say anything at all.



At noon, Fern closed the ledger.

She had filled four pages. Every name, every number, every box, as far as the window would let anyone scroll. *Settlement balance: 0 crystals.* She had underlined *settlement*.

"It's one purse," she said. "Not one each. One for all of us. Everything anyone brings out goes into one balance, and anyone near the window can see it, and anyone near the window can spend it." She looked round. "Can they?"

"Probably," said Umaru, from the sign. "In co-op mode. Yeah."

"Then nobody spends anything," said Fern, "without the ledger." She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. "Not one crystal. Not a ration. Not a rope. Every crystal that comes out of that gate goes in this book, and every crystal that goes into that window goes in this book. Anko registers what comes out. I record what goes in." She looked at Anko.

Anko, at the stake, had already taken out a fresh notebook. On the first page, in her small precise capitals, she had written: *GATE. LOOT REGISTER. VOL. 1.*

"Already started," said Anko.

"And the potions," said Fern. "If anyone ever gets one. They come to me. And Maomao. And we decide." She looked at the window, which she could see from where she stood. "They're rare. The window says so. We don't drink them because we can. We keep them for when we have to."

Maomao, beside her, nodded once.

"And nobody," said Fern, "goes in. Not yet." She looked at Milim, who had been edging toward the stake for twenty minutes. "Not yet."

Milim stopped edging.

"Then how do we get crystals?" said Okarun. "If nobody goes in?"

Fern looked at the window. At *SETTLEMENT BALANCE: 0 CRYSTALS.* At *CHANGES IN: 6 DAYS*, which had become *5 DAYS* sometime during the morning, while nobody was looking.

"Somebody goes in," she said. "Soon." She closed the ledger. "But first we decide who. And how. And what happens if they don't come out."

She turned and walked back down the path toward the settlement, with the ledger under her arm, and the house followed her, in twos and threes, looking back over their shoulders at the blue light and the window none of them could see any more.

Umaru stayed on her sign a while longer. Then she got up, and turned the sign round, and added a line under *(YET.)*, in smaller letters, in red:

*NO SNACKS IN THE SHOP. BRING YOUR OWN.*
