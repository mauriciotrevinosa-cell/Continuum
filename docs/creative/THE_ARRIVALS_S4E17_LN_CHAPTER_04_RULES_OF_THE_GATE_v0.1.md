## Chapter 4 — Rules of the Gate

*When: Day 107. The watch at the stake goes on day and night, in shifts. Seika's first week and the washing court mornings carry on around the long table.*

The rules took a whole day, because the house argued about every one of them, which was how Mau knew they would hold.

They argued at the long table, all morning, with the ledger open in the middle and Fern at the head of it and the plan of the real house on the wall behind her, and Senku's tally on the board, and the window out in the north field that none of them could see from here. Seiko presided from the high seat with her fan. The witches sat at their end and listened. Seika stood at the back by the fort with her arms folded, which by now everyone understood to mean she was paying very close attention.

Fern wrote the first rule herself, before anyone else could speak.

*1. Nobody goes in alone.*

Nobody argued with that one.



Then it got harder.

"Who goes?" said Fern.

Everyone started talking at once.

Milim wanted to go. Milim wanted to go so badly that she had been vibrating gently on the bench since breakfast and had broken a cup by holding it. Frieren wanted to go, and said so, calmly, three times, with her hands folded on the table, as if repetition alone would settle it. Stark wanted to go wherever Fern went. Himmel wanted to go because a hero did not let other people walk into a dungeon in front of him, and said so at some length, with a hand on his chest. Okarun wanted to go and was told by Momo, without looking at him, that he was not going.

"Not the first time," said Rimuru.

The table went quiet.

He was sitting by the cold hearth in his place, with his chin on his hand, the way he sat when he had been thinking about something all night. He had a sheet of paper in front of him, covered in his cheerful untidy handwriting.

"The first time isn't for crystals," said Rimuru. "The first time is for learning. What's in there. How it's built. What the monsters are. What hurts them and what doesn't. Whether you can get out when you want to." He looked round the table. "The first party goes in, looks, and comes out. That's all. Small. Careful. People who can fight, and people who can stop, and people who'll come back when they're told."

"*I'll* come back when I'm told," said Milim.

Rimuru looked at her.

"I will!"

"You threw a handful of rice cakes over the Muslin's roof," said Wakana, from the far end, "when Rimuru said to throw them *for luck*."

"That *was* for luck. *Very far away.*"

Rimuru waited until the laughter had gone round.

"Milim goes," he said.

Milim's mouth fell open.

"As Guardian," said Rimuru. "Not as the one who fights. The fort made you Guardian. The Guardian goes where her people go, and stands behind them, and if something comes that they can't handle, she handles it." He held her eyes. "Gently. And only then. You don't go first. You don't go alone. You don't touch anything that isn't attacking someone. You stand at the back and watch. That's the job."

Milim looked at him for a long moment, with her mouth still open.

Then she closed it. She sat up very straight on the bench, with her hands in her lap.

"Guardian," she said. "Okay." She swallowed. "Okay. At the *back*." Then, very fast: "But if something comes—"

"Then it's yours."

Milim nodded, very seriously, and did not vibrate again for the rest of the morning.

The rest of the first party took another hour. In the end, it was five. Yuta and Maki, because Yuta's cursed energy and Maki's cursed tools were the surest things in the house against things nobody knew. Mikasa, because she had found the window first, and because she was the best of any of them at going into a place she did not know and coming out again. Eren, because Mikasa was going. And Milim, at the back, as Guardian.

"Not me," said Mau.

He had not argued once all morning. Everyone turned.

"Not the first time," said Mau. He looked at Rimuru. "Ciel told me. Last night."



She had told him at the east window, after Frieren had gone to sleep.

*You felt it,* said Ciel. *At the stake. When you first saw the gate.*

He had. He had felt it the way he felt things he did not want to feel. Something small and old, in the place behind his breastbone where the seal was, lifting its head. Listening.

*The wheel wants it,* said Ciel. *Not the way Milim wants it. The way it wanted the plaza.* A pause. *A place full of things that fight. Things it has never met. Things it could turn toward.* Another pause. *I do not know what it would do in there. Neither do you. That is the problem.*

*So I don't go.*

*Not the first time,* said Ciel. *Let me see inside first. Through the others. Let me learn the shape of it.* A pause, gentler. *Then we will know what the wheel wants. And then you can go in knowing.*

He had not argued with her either.



"Then how do we see inside?" said Fern. "If the gate won't let Ciel look."

Rimuru held up the sheet of paper.

"It won't let her look in," he said. "From out here. It's a door, and it keeps its own shape, and Ciel can't see past it." He turned the paper round. On it, in his untidy hand, was a drawing of five small figures with lines running from their feet. "But it can't stop me going in. *With* them."

He held out his hand, palm up, on the table.

Something came out of his shadow.

It was very small. A thread of darkness, thinner than a hair, that rose out of the shadow his hand cast on the table and curled in the air like smoke, and then, as everyone watched, drifted across the table to Yuta, and sank into Yuta's shadow on the bench, and was gone.

Yuta looked down at his own shadow.

"It's in there," said Rimuru. "A piece of mine. In yours. It goes where you go." He looked at the five of them. "One for each of you. And one at the stake, in the shadow of Anko's marker, where it can see the gate." He paused. "Everything your shadow sees, mine sees. Everything mine sees, Ciel sees. If you get hurt, I'll know. If you get lost, I'll know where. If you need out—" He closed his hand. "I can pull."

*A relay,* said Ciel, with deep satisfaction. *Shadow to shadow. Like a line of people passing buckets.* A pause. *No cameras. No wires. Nothing she will need to break.*

Senku, at the far end of the table, had gone very still.

"*No wires,*" he said, softly. He looked at Yuta's shadow on the bench with an expression of profound, envious love. "That's a network. That's a *distributed sensor network*. With no hardware." He put his face in his hands. "She let him have *that*, and she won't let me have a *light bulb*."

"It's not hardware," said Rimuru gently. "It's me."

"I know," said Senku, muffled. "That's what makes it worse."



Then the contract.

That was Mau's. He had asked for it, at the end of the morning, and the table had looked at him, and then at Fern, and Fern had nodded.

The contracts in this world were real. Everyone at the table knew it. The ones you signed with the witches, the one Mau had signed with the Goddess, the ones from long ago that none of the newer arrivals had seen. Paper, ink, a name. And if you broke one, something happened to you, and it was not small. Mau had never broken one. Not once. He had been very careful, for a very long time, to keep to the exact letter of every contract he had ever made. The house knew that too.

He wrote it on a clean sheet at the long table, slowly, in his own hand, with the five of them watching. Fern checked every line.

*THE GATE.*

*Whoever enters agrees:*

*1. Never to enter alone.*

*2. To come out when the relay says come out. At once. Without argument.*

*3. To go no deeper than the party agreed before entering.*

*4. To bring everything found to the ledger.*

*5. To leave no one inside.*

He looked at the last line for a long time before he wrote it.

"*To leave no one inside,*" said Maki, reading it upside down. "What if we can't?"

"Then you don't come out either," said Mau. "Not until you've tried everything." He put the pen down. "And then the relay pulls you. And then we go back in. All of us." He looked at her. "Nobody gets left in a hole. Not in this house."

Maki looked at him for a long moment. Then she took the pen, and signed. *Zen'in Maki.* The ink flared, very faintly, gold, for an instant, and went back to black.

Yuta signed under her. Then Mikasa, in small neat letters. Then Eren, who looked at rule two for a long time before he signed it. Then Milim, in enormous capitals that took up half the remaining space, with a small drawing of a dragon after her name, which the contract appeared to accept.

"What happens," said Okarun, from down the table, "if someone breaks it?"

"Nobody's going to break it," said Mau.

"But if they did."

Mau thought about it.

"It wouldn't be good," he said honestly. "So don't."



The afternoon was for the rest.

Umaru wrote a strategy guide. Nobody had asked her to. She wrote it at the toll booth in the fort, on six pages, in coloured chalk, and presented it to the first party at the long table in the late afternoon with enormous ceremony. Its title was *THE MINISTRY OF FORT AFFAIRS GUIDE TO DUNGEONS (FIRST EDITION).*

Maki read it out, because Umaru asked her to.

"*One. Check your corners.*" Maki turned the page. "*Two. Don't split the party.* Three. *If there's a lever, don't pull it until you know what it does.* Four. *Bosses have patterns. Watch first, hit second.* Five. *Save before the boss.*"

"We can't save," said Yuta.

"I *know* we can't save," said Umaru. "I'm saying it so you *feel* how bad it is that we can't save."

Maki turned the page.

"*Six,*" she read, and paused. "*Never, ever open a chest without poking it first. With a long stick. From as far away as possible. Some chests are not chests. Some chests are MIMICS. Mimics eat you.*"

She lowered the page.

Across the table, Frieren, who had been listening to all of this with her chin on her hand and her face perfectly composed, said: "Hm."

"*Hm?*" said Umaru.

"Nothing," said Frieren.

"Frieren-sama," said Fern, very quietly, without looking up from the ledger.

"I said *nothing*."

Umaru looked at Frieren for a long moment, narrowly. Then she took back the guide, and added a line at the bottom of page six, in red chalk, underlined twice: *THIS MEANS YOU.* She did not say who *you* was. She did not need to.



At dusk, Mau walked out to the red stake on his own.

Mikasa and Eren were on the watch, on Eren's coat, with the lantern between them. They did not say anything when he came. Mikasa shifted over on the coat, and he sat down beside them, and the three of them looked at the blue light standing up out of the snow forty paces away, humming.

He could feel it from here. The wheel. Small and old, behind his breastbone, with its head up, listening.

He put his hand flat on his chest and left it there.

"You're not going," said Eren. It was not a question.

"Not the first time."

"Good," said Eren.

Mau looked at him.

Eren was looking at the gate, with his elbows on his knees and his hands loose between them. In the blue light his face was very calm.

"Exits before rooms," said Eren. "That's what I said about the tunnels. That's what Rimuru's doing with the shadows. That's what your contract is." He nodded at the gate. "We go in and find the exits. Then you go in." He paused. "That's the right order. That's the only order."

Mikasa, on his other side, said nothing. But in the light of the lantern Mau saw her look at Eren for a long moment, the way you look at someone who has said a thing you did not know they knew.

They sat at the stake until the second watch came. Then Mau walked back down the path to the settlement alone, with his hand still on his chest, and the hum fading behind him into the dark, and the coin warm against his ribs, and something under the coin, very quietly, still listening.
