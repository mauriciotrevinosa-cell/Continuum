*When: Days 109 to 111, the same days as the first runs. While the party went in twice more, Stark split wood, and the window counted down toward the change.*

Stark did not say anything about it for two days, which was how Fern knew it was bad.

The first party went in again on the hundred-and-tenth morning, and again that afternoon. The same five, under the same contract, with the same threads in their shadows. They went one room deeper each time, as the contract allowed and Fern agreed beforehand: the round room and the left-hand passage in the morning, then the middle passage and the room past it in the afternoon, where there were more wolves, and once a thing like a wolf but larger, with a crust of crystals along its spine, which took Yuta and Maki together to bring down and left a crystal the size of a walnut.

They came out each time when Rimuru said. They brought out what they found. Anko wrote it down. *Day 110, morning. Crystals: 14 (small). Injuries: Eren, bruised ribs (wolf, blades crossed again, Maomao says stop doing that). Steel: useless.* *Day 110, afternoon. Crystals: 2 (small), 1 (medium). Injuries: none. Steel: still useless.*

Each time, Fern took the crystals to the window, and the number at the bottom went up.

And each time, at the red stake, Stark stood with the house and watched the five of them go into the blue, and said nothing at all.



Fern found him that evening, behind the timber yard, splitting wood.

He split wood when he was thinking. Everyone knew it. He had split most of the winter's firewood for the Muslin on the night before Fern moved her bed a handspan across the chalk line, and nobody had ever needed to ask why. Now he was splitting deadfall oak by lamplight, behind the timber yard, alone, with his own axe. The one he had carried across a continent in another world, and over the bridge in this one. The one that had never once failed him.

Fern stood at the corner of the timber yard with her ledger under her arm and watched him for a while.

He brought the axe down. The log split clean in two. He set up another. He brought the axe down. It split.

"It works on wood," said Fern.

Stark did not turn round.

"It works on wood," he agreed. He set up another log. "Works on wood. Works on bandits. Works on demons, in our world, if you hit them hard enough and you don't stop." He brought it down. The log split. "Worked on a dragon, once. You were there."

"I was there."

"Doesn't work on a wolf." He set up another log. He did not bring the axe down. He stood with it resting on his shoulder and looked at the log. "Mikasa's the best fighter in this house who isn't Milim or the ones with the powers. She hit that thing at the neck with everything she had, and it didn't even notice. And Eren crossed his blades and held it off with *steel*, and it nearly bit through." He looked at the axe on his shoulder. "If I went in there with this, I'd be the guy at the back holding the lantern."

Fern did not say anything.

"You're going to go in," said Stark. "Eventually. You and Frieren. With your magic. Zoltraak goes through anything." He set the axe down against the chopping block, very carefully, the way he always set it down. "And I'm going to be standing at the red stake. Watching you walk in." He looked at the ground. "That's the thing. Not being useless. I've been useless before. I was useless for years, in a village, too scared to go outside." He was quiet for a moment. "It's watching *you* walk in. And not being able to walk in after you."

Fern looked at him for a long time in the lamplight.

Then she opened the ledger, and turned it round, and held it out so that he could see the page.

It was the page she had started on the day of the window. *GATE. SHOP. PRICES.* Every name, every number, in her small neat hand. And beside one line, halfway down, under *WEAPONS*, there was a small mark in the margin. A tick, in ink, very small, made at some point in the last two days when nobody was looking.

*Hand Axe (Gate-forged), 340.*

Stark looked at it.

"The balance is three hundred and thirty," said Fern. "As of tonight." She closed the ledger. "Tomorrow they go in again. If they bring out one more crystal, it's three hundred and forty."

"Fern—"

"I'm not deciding," said Fern quickly. "The table decides. I'm only the ledger." She looked away, at the stacked timber. "I only put a mark in the margin. That's not deciding. That's bookkeeping."

Stark looked at her. At the ledger under her arm, and the small ink tick he could no longer see, and her face, which had gone faintly pink in the lamplight and was looking very hard at a stack of oak.

"That's deciding," said Stark.

"It's *bookkeeping*."

He picked up his own axe again, and set up the next log, and split it, clean, in one stroke. Then he stood with the axe on his shoulder, grinning at the ground, and did not say anything else, and neither did she, and after a while she sat down on the chopping block beside the woodpile and watched him split wood until the lamp burned down.



The table decided the next morning.

It was not a long argument. Fern laid out the ledger at breakfast, the way she laid out everything: *Balance: 330. The window. The list.* What did the settlement buy first, with its first three hundred and thirty crystals, from a shop in a field?

Momo said rations, and was right, and everyone knew she was right. Senku said *Gate Iron*, one ingot, to study, and was also right, in a different way. Umaru said, very seriously, that in every game she had ever played the first purchase was always a better weapon for the party member who couldn't do damage, because otherwise you were carrying dead weight and the whole run slowed down, and then went bright red and said she didn't mean Stark was dead weight, she meant in *games*.

"She's right," said Maki.

Everyone looked at her.

"She's right," said Maki again. She was sitting at the long table with her spear across her knees, as she had sat every morning since the first run. "There are three people in this house who can hurt what's in there. Me, Yuta and Milim. And Mikasa and Eren, if they get the right blades. Everyone else is the lantern." She looked down the table. "Frieren and Fern can, with magic. Probably. We haven't tested it." She looked at Stark. "He can't. Not with what he's got. And he's the one who goes in front of Fern." She shrugged. "Buy him something that cuts."

The table was quiet.

"Mikasa's blades first," said Stark. "She's been in there three times. With a nicked edge."

"No," said Mikasa.

Everyone turned.

Mikasa was sitting beside Eren with her two blades in front of her on the table, the nicked one and the good one. She did not look up from them.

"I can go in with Maki and Yuta and Milim in front," said Mikasa, "and watch, and learn, and wait." She touched the nick in the edge with one finger. "You're going in with Fern and Frieren and Mau. Next week. When it changes." She looked up, at last, at Stark. "In front."

Stark did not say anything.

"The axe," said Mikasa. "Then mine."

The table voted. It was not close. Even Momo voted for the axe, and then said *rations next*, firmly, and Fern wrote it down.



They bought it at noon.

Everyone came out to the red stake to see. It was the first thing anyone in the settlement had bought from a window in a field, and nobody quite knew what would happen.

The first party had come out at the eleventh bell with nine more crystals. Fern had fed eight of them to the window and kept one back, for a reason she would explain later, and the number at the bottom had said *SETTLEMENT BALANCE: 410 CRYSTALS*. Now Fern stood in front of the window with the ledger open on her arm and Stark beside her, and looked at the list.

*Hand Axe (Gate-forged), 340.*

"How do I—" said Fern.

"Touch it," said Umaru, from the back. "The name. Then it asks if you're sure."

Fern touched it.

A smaller window opened beside it, pale blue.

*HAND AXE (GATE-FORGED). A plain axe of gate iron. Holds an edge against gate creatures. Will not break on them. Purchase for 340 crystals?*

And under it, two small boxes. *YES. NO.*

Fern looked at the boxes for a long time.

"It asks if you're sure," she said quietly. "Everything here asks if you're sure."

She touched *YES*.

The number at the bottom changed. *SETTLEMENT BALANCE: 70 CRYSTALS.* And in the air in front of the window, at the height of a man's chest, something began to take shape. Lines first, pale blue, drawing themselves in the air like the little pictures on the list. Then the lines filled in. Then they were not lines. They were wood, and iron, and weight, and the weight fell, all at once, out of the air, into the snow at Stark's feet, with a soft heavy *thump*.

It was an axe.

It was a plain axe. That was the first thing anyone could have said about it. A hand axe, a little longer than Stark's own, with a haft of some dark close-grained wood and a head of grey iron, unornamented, with an edge that caught the winter light strangely, the way the wolves' hides had caught the crystal light. Dully. Like wet slate.

Stark bent down and picked it up.

He held it for a long moment. He turned it over in his hands. He tested the weight, the balance, the grip, the way he had tested every weapon he had ever held. His face did not change.

"Well?" said Fern.

"It's an axe," said Stark.

"*Stark.*"

"It's a good axe." He looked at the edge. "It's a really good axe." He swung it once, gently, through the air, and it made a sound none of them had heard an axe make. A low hum, very faint, like the gate's. "It's heavier than it looks. And lighter." He frowned. "That doesn't make sense."

"*Gate iron*," said Senku, who had pushed his way to the front and was staring at the axe head with his nose about three inches from it. "It's carrying something. In the metal. It's not just iron." He looked up, blazing. "It's like Maki's spear. Like a cursed tool. But it's not cursed. It's—" He stopped. "I don't know what it is. I need an ingot."

"Rations next," said Momo, firmly, from the back.

Then Stark did the thing everyone had been waiting for and nobody had said out loud.

He turned round, with the new axe in his hand, and walked back down the path, past the red stake, past the twenty paces where the window vanished, past the last cottage, into the settlement. Inside the line.

He stopped in the plaza and held up the axe.

It was still there. Solid. Heavy. Real. In his hand, in the settlement, in the thin winter sun, a long way from the window and the gate.

"It stays," said Stark.

"It stays," said Umaru, very quietly, with something like awe. "In the games, it doesn't always stay. Sometimes it only works in the dungeon." She looked at the axe. "This one stays."

Fern opened the ledger and wrote: *Day 111. Out: 340. Hand Axe (Gate-forged). Stark. Balance: 70.* And then, after a moment, in the margin, very small, beside the tick she had made two days before, a second tick.



Senku got the crystal Fern had kept back, that evening, after a long negotiation in which Fern conceded that one small crystal was a reasonable research budget and Senku conceded that he would not ask for another until the gate changed.

He took it to the workshop, and set it on the bench beside the box marked NOT YET, and looked at it all evening.

It glowed. Faintly, blue, steadily, the way the crystals glowed in the walls of the corridor. It was warm to the touch. Not as warm as a coin. But warm. Like something that held a little of something in it.

Late that night, very carefully, he wrapped two copper wires round it, one at each end, and touched the far ends of the wires to a tiny glass bulb.

Nothing happened.

He waited. He watched the crystal. He watched the bulb.

Then the crystal flickered. Once. And went out.

Not cracked. Not shattered. Only out. Dark. A small grey stone on the bench, cold, with two copper wires round it, like a stone from the riverbank.

Senku looked at it for a long time.

He did not hear a voice. He did not need to.

He unwound the wires, very carefully. He picked up the small grey stone. He opened the box marked NOT YET, where the pinch of glittering dust from the seventh bulb lay at the bottom, and put the stone in beside it, and closed the lid.

Then he took the label off, and wrote a new one, in small square capitals, and stuck it on.

*NOT YET. (AGAIN.)*

He sat in the dark workshop for a while after that, looking at the box. And then, slowly, he started to laugh, very quietly, so as not to wake anybody.

"Ten billion percent," he said, to the box, "you're going to let me eventually."

The box did not answer.

Out in the north field, past the red stake, the blue light of the gate stood up out of the snow, humming. The window hung beside it, where nobody could see it from the settlement, with a new line at the bottom that had not been there that morning.
