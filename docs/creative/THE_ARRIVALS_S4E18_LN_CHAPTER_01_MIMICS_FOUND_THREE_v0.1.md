*When: Day 112, the dawn the gate changed, the start of its second week. The watch at the stake, Seika on her mattress and the washing court mornings all carry on.*

The gate changed in the night, and Frieren was standing at the red stake when it did.

Nobody had asked her to keep watch. It was Yuta and Maki's shift, on Eren's coat, with the lantern. But at some point after midnight, Maki looked up and found the elf standing beside the stake in her shawl, with her hands folded in her sleeves, looking at the blue light. She had not heard her come. Nobody ever did.

"It changes at dawn," said Maki. "Probably. The window says *zero days*."

"I know," said Frieren.

"You could wait inside. It's cold."

"I know," said Frieren, and did not move.

So she was there, at the first grey of the hundred-and-twelfth morning, when the gate flickered. Not much. The blue light stuttered, once, like a candle in a draught, and for an instant, through it, instead of the stone corridor and the crystal glow, there was nothing at all. A darkness with no depth. Then the light steadied, and hummed, and was the gate again. But the colour was a shade deeper. And the cold that came out of it smelled different. Less like wet stone. More like dust, and old iron, and something dry.

The window, hanging beside it, changed too.

*GATE: WEEK 2. GRADE: NORMAL. CHANGES IN: 7 DAYS.*

Frieren looked at it for a long time.

"The chest," she said.

Maki did not say anything. Yuta, beside her on the coat, looked at the ground.

"It's gone," said Frieren. It was not a question. "The first room. The corner. All of it. New rooms. New chests." She stood very still. "The old one's gone."

"Probably," said Maki gently.

Frieren closed her eyes. She stood at the red stake in the grey dawn, in her shawl, and mourned, for a long moment, a chest she had never seen, in a room that no longer existed, which might have held a grimoire she would now never read.

Then she opened her eyes again.

"*New chests,*" she said.

And went to wake Mau.



"Now," said Frieren.

"It's dawn."

"It changed. It's new. Nobody's been in it." She was standing over the bed in the grey light with her shawl still on and snow on the hem of her skirt. "Ciel said after you'd seen the shape of it. You've seen the shape of it. Three times. Through the shadows." She paused. "Ask her."

Mau, half asleep, asked her.

*She is right,* said Ciel, after a moment, with what sounded like great reluctance. *I have seen it through five shadows, three times. I know how it is built now. Rooms. Passages. Things that fight. Things that drop crystals.* A pause. *And I know what the wheel does when it is near. It listens. It does not turn. Not yet.* Another pause. *You may go. If you go carefully. If you do not fight unless you must. And if you come out when Rimuru says.*

"She says yes," said Mau.

"I know," said Frieren. "I heard your face."



It was the four of them. The table had agreed it on the evening of the axe, before anyone had known the gate would change: the second party would be Frieren, and Fern, and Stark in front with the new axe, and Mau. Not Milim, this time. *You've been in,* Rimuru told her. *Let someone else have a turn at being amazed.* Milim had sulked for an hour and then gone to stand at the red stake with her arms folded to watch, as Guardian of the entrance, which she had decided was a separate and equally important job.

Mau signed the contract under the five names that were already there. The ink flared gold, faintly, and went black. Frieren signed under him, in small neat runes. Fern signed in her small neat hand. Stark signed, and then looked at his name for a while, under Fern's, before he gave the pen back.

Rimuru put the threads in their shadows. Mau felt his go in, a small cool thread curling once at his heel and then gone.

*I see you,* said Rimuru's voice, in the back of his head. *All four. Go slow.* A pause. *And Frieren—*

"I know," said Frieren.

*Rule six,* said Rimuru's voice. *Umaru made me say it.*

Frieren did not answer that.

They walked past the red stake together, across forty paces of snow, into the blue.



It was not stone this time. Or it was, but older. The corridor was wider, and the walls were not rough rock but dressed blocks, fitted, very old, with the edges worn soft. There were pillars, here and there, carved with patterns nobody could read. The crystals still grew from the walls and lit everything blue. But between them there were empty sockets in the stone, as if something had once been mounted there and taken away.

"It's a different dungeon," said Fern.

"It's a *ruin*," said Frieren. She had stopped just inside the gate and was looking round at the pillars with her whole face. "Someone built this. Lived here. A long time ago." She put her hand flat on the nearest pillar. "It's very well made."

"Frieren-sama."

"I'm only *looking*."

Stark went first. That was the agreement. He walked down the corridor ahead of them with the new axe held low in both hands, the way he had walked ahead of Frieren and Fern for years across another continent, and Mau watched his back and thought that he had never seen Stark walk quite like that. Not braver. Lighter.

The first thing that came out of the dark at them was a skeleton.

It was not a person's. It was too tall for that, and too many-jointed, and it walked on four limbs, like a dog that had once been something else, with a long skull and empty eyes that glowed the same pale blue as the crystals. It came out of a side passage at a scuttling run, jaw open, straight at Stark.

Stark did not think. Mau saw him not think. He saw him plant his feet and bring the new axe round in one flat, short, brutal arc, the stroke he had used on bandits and demons and once, years ago, on a dragon. The grey edge hit the skeleton across the ribs.

It went through.

It went through bone the way Maki's spear had gone through the wolf: like a knife through bread. The skeleton came apart in mid-stride, in a burst of blue sparks that filled the corridor and fell, softly, like snow. A small crystal clinked on the stone at Stark's feet.

Stark stood very still, with the axe at the end of its arc.

Then he looked down at it. At the dull grey edge, not even marked. At the crystal on the stone.

He turned round.

He did not say anything. He did not need to. His face said all of it. He was grinning so widely, so helplessly, at Fern, that he looked about fourteen, and Fern, behind Mau, made a small sound and put her hand over her mouth, and her eyes over the top of it were very bright.

"It works," said Stark.

"I saw," said Fern.

"It *works*."

"I *saw*, Stark." She lowered her hand. Her face was perfectly composed again, almost. "Pick up the crystal. Anko will want it."

Stark picked up the crystal. He was still grinning when he put it in the bag.



They went on. Slowly. As agreed.

There were more of the skeletons, in ones and twos. Stark took most of them. When three came at once, from two sides, Fern lifted one hand, almost lazily, and a line of white light went out from her fingertips and through two of them, and they came apart. Zoltraak. It worked. Of course it worked. Frieren took the third without looking up from the carving on a pillar she was reading.

Mau did very little. Ciel had asked him not to, and he could feel why. Every time something came out of the dark, the small old thing behind his breastbone lifted its head and watched it, with an attention he did not like. It did not turn. It only watched. He kept his hands in his pockets and his eyes on Stark's back, and carried the bag, and let the others fight.

*Good,* said Ciel, very quietly. *Like that.*

And then, in a room off the main corridor, a square room with a fallen pillar across one corner, there was a chest.

It sat against the far wall, in the blue light. Wooden, bound in dark metal. About the size of a travelling trunk. Its lid was closed.

Frieren stopped dead in the doorway.

Fern, behind her, closed her eyes.

Stark, beside Fern, looked at the ceiling, with the patient expression of a man who has seen this show before, many times, in many dungeons, on another continent, and knows exactly how it ends.

Mau did not know how it ended.

"Is that—" said Mau.

"A chest," said Frieren. She was already walking toward it.

"Umaru said—"

"I know what Umaru said." She knelt down in front of it. She held out one hand over the lid, palm down, not touching. Her lips moved. A faint, cool light came from her palm, and passed over the chest, and went out.

"What was that?" said Mau.

"A spell," said Frieren, without turning round. "To detect mimics. It's very reliable." She looked at the chest. "It's right ninety-nine times in a hundred."

"And what does it say?"

"It says it's a mimic," said Frieren.

There was a pause.

"So you're not going to open it," said Mau.

Frieren did not answer. She put both hands on the lid.

"*Frieren*—"

She opened it.

The chest opened its mouth.

That was the only way Mau could describe it, afterward. The lid came up, and under the lid there was not a hollow box but a mouth, wide, wet, red, ringed with teeth like a shark's, and a long dark tongue, and the mouth lunged forward and closed, very neatly, over Frieren's head and shoulders, and most of her upper body, so that all that was left of her outside the chest was her lower half, kneeling on the stone, with her legs kicking.

From inside the chest, muffled, came a sound.

"*It's dark! It's dark! I'm scared!*"

Mau stood frozen.

Fern sighed. It was the longest, most patient, most bone-deep sigh Mau had ever heard a human being produce. Then she walked forward, took hold of one of Frieren's ankles, and nodded to Stark, who took hold of the other. Stark set his feet. Fern set hers.

"One," said Fern. "Two."

They pulled.

Frieren came out of the mimic with a wet sucking sound, all at once, and fell backward onto the stone, and Stark brought the axe down on the chest's lid before it could close, and it came apart in blue sparks. A crystal clinked on the floor.

Frieren sat up. Her hair was soaked. Her shawl was soaked. There was something on her face Mau did not want to think about.

She looked at the place where the mimic had been. It was empty. No grimoire. Nothing but a crystal.

"It was a mimic," said Frieren.

Mau began to laugh.

He could not help it. He had not known he was going to. It came up out of him all at once, helpless, the way it had come up on the roof in the snow, and he laughed until he had to lean on the fallen pillar, while Frieren sat on the floor of the square room, soaked to the waist, glaring at him.

"Never," said Frieren, with enormous dignity, wringing out her hair, "underestimate the knowledge of someone older than you."

Mau stopped laughing long enough to look at her.

"You forget," he said, "I'm older than you now."

It was true. Everyone in the house knew it, though nobody said it often. Four hundred and thirty-seven scenarios. Ciel had counted them. Millennia, inside the Trial, though outside it had been days. He had lived longer in there than Frieren had lived in her whole life. He did not remember most of it. But he had lived it.

Frieren looked at him for a long moment, soaked, on the floor.

Then she ignored him completely, stood up, wrung out her shawl, and walked out of the square room into the corridor, toward the next one, with her chin up.

Fern looked at Mau. Her face said, very clearly, without a single word, *it's always like this*.

Stark patted Mau on the shoulder as he went past, with the air of a man welcoming another man into a very old and very exclusive club.

"First one's the worst," said Stark. "You get used to it."

"Does she *ever*—"

"No," said Stark and Fern together.



There was a second chest, two rooms further on.

Frieren cast the spell. It said mimic. She opened it. It ate her. *It's dark! It's dark!* Fern and Stark pulled her out. Stark killed it. A crystal. No grimoire.

Mau laughed so hard he had to sit down.

There was a third, at the end of a long gallery of pillars, on a raised step, under a carving of something with wings.

Frieren cast the spell. It said mimic. She looked at Mau, very steadily, daring him. Then she opened it.

It ate her.

This time Mau helped pull. He took hold of her belt, with Fern on one ankle and Stark on the other, and they pulled together, and she came out of the third mimic with the same wet sound and fell back on top of all three of them in a heap, and Stark killed the chest from underneath her. A crystal rolled down the step.

No grimoire.

They lay in a pile on the floor of the gallery for a moment, the four of them, in the blue light, Frieren soaked on top, nobody moving.

"Frieren," said Mau, from somewhere underneath her.

"Mm."

"That spell. That's right ninety-nine times out of a hundred."

"Yes."

"How many times has it been right today?"

There was a pause.

"Three," said Frieren.

"Out of three."

"Yes."

"And you opened all three."

"There's always the one in a hundred," said Frieren, from on top of him, with total conviction. "Someday, it'll say mimic, and I'll open it, and it won't be. And there'll be a grimoire inside." She paused. "If I stop opening them, I'll never find that one."

Mau lay on the floor of the gallery underneath a soaked elf and thought about that for a long time.

Then he started to laugh again. Not at her, this time. He did not know what at. At all of it. At a thousand years of opening chests that you knew were mimics, because of the one in a hundred.

Frieren got up off him, wrung out her hair for the third time, took a small notebook out of her pocket, the one she kept for spells, and wrote in it, in her small neat runes, by the blue light of the crystals:

*Week 2. Mimics found: 3. Mimics opened: 3.*

She looked at it for a moment. Then she added, underneath:

*Grimoires: 0. (So far.)*

She put the notebook away.

"Next room," said Frieren.
