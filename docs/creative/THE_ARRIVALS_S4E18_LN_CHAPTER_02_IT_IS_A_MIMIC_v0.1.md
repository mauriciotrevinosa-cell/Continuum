## Chapter 8 — It Is a Mimic

*When: Day 112, the same morning, in the deepest room.*

The fourth chest was in the last room, the deepest one the table had agreed to.

It was a small round room at the end of the gallery of pillars, with a domed ceiling so thick with crystals that the whole room glowed like the inside of a lamp. There was nothing in it but the chest. It sat in the exact centre of the floor, on a low stone dais, under the brightest part of the ceiling, as if someone had put it there to be looked at.

Frieren stopped in the doorway.

So did everyone else.

"That's the last room," said Fern quietly. "The contract says this far. Then back."

"I know," said Frieren.

She did not take her eyes off the chest. Nobody else did either. It sat on its dais in the blue light, closed, wooden, bound in dark metal, larger than the other three. There was a carving on its lid. A sort of seal, very worn, of something with wings.

*Rimuru,* Mau thought.

*I see it,* said Rimuru's voice, in the back of his head. A pause. *Ciel says she can't tell. Again.* A longer pause. *Ciel also says she would like to formally register that she has given up.*

Frieren walked across the round room, stepped up onto the dais, and knelt in front of the chest.

She held out her hand over the lid, palm down. Her lips moved. The faint cool light passed over the wood and the iron and the worn winged seal, and went out.

She knelt very still.

"Well?" said Mau.

"Mimic," said Frieren.

Nobody said anything. Fern did not even sigh this time. She simply walked over to the dais, and stood at Frieren's left side, and Stark came and stood at her right, and they both looked down at the backs of Frieren's ankles with the calm, practised expressions of two people taking up positions they have taken up many hundreds of times before.

Mau came and stood behind her, without being asked, and put his hands in his pockets, and waited.

Frieren put both hands on the lid.

"There's always the one in a hundred," she said, to nobody, very quietly.

She opened it.

It was a mimic.

The lid came up and the mouth was there, wider than the others, redder, with more teeth, and it lunged, and closed over Frieren's head and shoulders and arms and half her body in one wet gulp, and her legs kicked on the dais, and from inside, muffled, came:

"*It's dark! It's dark!*"

Fern had her left ankle before the second *dark*. Stark had her right. Mau took her belt. Nobody counted to three this time. They pulled.

She came out harder than the others. It held on. Mau felt it hold on, felt the whole chest lean forward on its dais after her, its iron bands creaking, as if it did not want to let go of something. Then, with a long, horrible, sucking sound, it let go, and Frieren came out all at once and fell backward off the dais on top of all three of them, and Stark, from underneath, got the axe up and brought it down, and the mimic came apart in a great burst of blue sparks that filled the whole round room and fell over them like snow.

They lay in a heap on the floor, the four of them, under the falling sparks.

Mau was already laughing. He could feel it starting, the way it had started three times before, rising up out of him at the sight of her, soaked again, on top of all of them again, in a dungeon, for the fourth time in one morning. He opened his mouth to say something about the one in a hundred.

Then he saw her face.

She was not looking at any of them. She was sitting up, on top of Stark's legs, soaked to the waist, with her hair plastered to her head and something dripping off her chin. And she was holding something against her chest, in both arms, very tightly, the way a child holds something it has been told it cannot keep.

It was a book.

It was small, and old, and bound in something dark that might once have been leather. It was soaked, like her. And she was holding it against herself, and looking down at it, and her face was doing a thing Mau had never seen it do. Not in the clearing. Not on the night of the snow. Not under the ink tree in the rain of its leaves.

He did not laugh.

"It was inside," said Frieren. Her voice was very small. "It was inside the mimic. Not the chest. The *mimic*. It must have eaten someone, once, a long time ago. Someone who had this." She looked down at it. "And it kept it. All this time."

Nobody said anything.

Frieren opened the book.

The pages were damp but not ruined. The writing on them was small and faded and in a script Mau did not recognize. Frieren did. He watched her eyes move across the first page, and stop, and go back to the start, and move across it again, slower. Her lips were moving. Her ears, at the tips, had gone pink.

"It's a grimoire," she said.

She was not speaking to any of them. She sat on the floor of the round room, on top of Stark's legs, in the falling blue sparks, and turned the page, and began to read.



They waited.

Stark, underneath, did not move his legs. Fern sat up slowly beside them and did not say *Frieren-sama*. Mau sat on the floor of the round room with his back against the dais and watched her read.

She read for a long time. The sparks finished falling and went out. The crystals in the dome glowed steadily overhead. Somewhere far back down the gallery of pillars, something skittered on stone, and Stark's hand moved to the axe, and stopped there, and nothing came.

She turned a page. And another. Her face did not change. It was the face, still, that he had never seen. Not happy, exactly. Something past happy. Something very old and very quiet. The face of someone who has opened chests for a thousand years because of the one in a hundred, and has just, at last, on the floor of a round room in a dungeon in another world, found it.

*Rimuru says,* said Ciel, gently, at last, *the relay has been quiet for a long time. He would like to know if everyone is alive.*

*Tell him yes,* thought Mau. *Tell him she found one.*

A pause.

*He says he is very happy for her,* said Ciel. *He says also that it is time to come out.*

Mau got up.

"Frieren," he said. "Let's finish the dungeon first. Then you can read it. At home. In the reading chair." He held out his hand to her. "Rimuru says it's time."

Frieren looked up from the book.

She looked at him. She did not say anything. Her face was perfectly composed. She closed the grimoire, very gently, and put it inside her soaked shawl, against her chest, and held it there with one arm. Then she took his hand with the other, and let him pull her up.

She said nothing at all.

Fern got up. Stark got up, and picked up the bag, and the axe, and shook out his legs. They turned to go, the four of them, toward the doorway and the long gallery of pillars and the way back out.

Mau had taken two steps toward the door when something landed on his back.

It landed hard. It landed all at once, with no warning at all, with arms round his neck and knees at his sides and a wet shawl and a small hard book pressed between his shoulder blades. He staggered, and caught himself, and caught her, by reflex, under the knees, the way you catch someone who has jumped on your back.

"*You owe me,*" said Frieren, in his ear.

"*What?*"

"You owe me." She settled herself more comfortably, with her chin on his shoulder, and her arms round his neck, and the grimoire between them. "Count this as your punishment for breaking your promise."

"What promise—"

"A hundred years," said Frieren. "The tree. *Both of us. A hundred years.* That was the promise. And then the tree came in one breath, on the seventieth day, ninety-nine years and some months early." She paused. "You broke it."

"*Rimuru* broke it! *Ciel* broke it! I didn't even—"

"You were holding him up," said Frieren. "When he did it. Your hand was on his back." She tightened her arms round his neck. "That counts."

Mau opened his mouth. He closed it again.

It was the most unfair thing he had ever heard. It was pure Frieren logic, from start to finish. It was not even slightly his fault, and she knew it, and she knew that he knew it, and she did not care.

"I declare," said Frieren, in his ear, very clearly, in the voice of someone reading out a proclamation, "that tonight and tomorrow, you have to treat me as queen." A pause. "And do as I say."

Mau went very quiet.

The round room was very quiet.

Fern, two steps ahead, had turned round. She was looking at Mau over Stark's shoulder. And her face, for once in her life, said only one thing, very clearly, without a single word, with every muscle in it: *Please. Please don't say anything. Please don't make this worse. Please.*

Stark, beside her, had his hand over his mouth and was looking at the ceiling.

Mau stood in the middle of the round room with a soaked elf on his back, her arms round his neck, her chin on his shoulder, and a grimoire from inside a mimic pressed between them, and thought about it. About a hundred years. About a tree that had come too early. About a thousand years of opening chests because of the one in a hundred.

"If that," said Mau, "is my punishment—"

Fern closed her eyes.

"—I will happily oblige."

There was a silence.

Then, very softly, in his ear, Frieren made a sound. It was small, and short, and he had heard it only a few times in his whole life, and he would have crossed a great many dungeons to hear it again. It was a laugh.

"Good," said Frieren. "Walk."

He walked.



They went back up the gallery of pillars, through the square rooms, down the long corridor of dressed stone, toward the gate, in the order the contract required. Stark in front, with the axe. Fern behind him. Mau at the back, carrying the queen.

The queen gave directions.

"Left," said Frieren, at a junction where there was only one way to go.

"There's only *left*."

"Left, subject."

He went left.

"Slower," said Frieren, a little further on.

"Rimuru said to come out."

"Rimuru isn't queen."

"*Frieren*—"

"Your Majesty," said Frieren.

There was a pause.

"*Your Majesty,*" said Mau, through his teeth, "Rimuru said to come out."

"Then walk slower *and* come out," said Frieren, with perfect serenity, and put her chin back on his shoulder.

Ahead of them, Fern walked very straight, with her shoulders very square, and did not turn round once, the whole way back. Stark's shoulders, in front of her, were shaking.

*Rimuru wishes to know,* said Ciel, in the back of Mau's head, very carefully, *why the relay is reporting that you are now carrying Frieren.*

*Tell him later,* thought Mau.

*He says he can see it,* said Ciel. *Through Stark's shadow. He says he would like to be told now.* A pause. *He says, also, that half the house is at the red stake. And that Milim is there. And that Umaru is there.*

Mau stopped walking.

"What?" said Frieren.

"Half the house," said Mau, "is at the stake."

Frieren considered this.

"Good," she said. "Walk."



They came out of the blue light one after another, into the white morning and the snow and the north field. Stark first, with the axe and the bag. Then Fern, very straight. Then Mau, with Frieren on his back, soaked, with her chin on his shoulder, her arms round his neck, and her shawl dripping onto his coat.

Half the house was at the red stake. More than half. Milim, at the front, as Guardian of the entrance, with her arms folded. The fort, in hoods, with Umaru beside her sign. Rimuru, kneeling at the marker with his hand on it, looking up at them with an expression of profound, delighted disbelief. Yuta and Maki. Mikasa and Eren. The five from the big room, with Nano holding a schedule nobody had asked for. Mai at the edge. Kaneki, off the night shift, with his collar up. Seika, at the back, with her arms folded in the ugly coat.

Everyone stared.

Mau walked across forty paces of snow toward them with Frieren on his back. Nobody said anything. He reached the red stake, and stopped, and stood there.

Frieren lifted her chin off his shoulder.

She looked down at all of them from her place on his back. At Milim, and Umaru, and Rimuru, and the whole staring house. She was soaked to the waist. There was mimic on her shawl. Her hair was plastered to her head. Her ears were pink.

And she said, very clearly, very proudly, in the voice of someone announcing the single greatest achievement of a thousand-year life:

"I have a subject."
