*When: Day 112, afternoon into night: the first day of the reign.*

The first decree was issued at the red stake, from Mau's back, before anyone had recovered enough to speak.

"Home," said Frieren. "Subject."

Mau carried her home.

He carried her down the path across the north field, past the last cottage, through the plaza, across the yard, past the ink tree, with the whole house walking behind them in total silence, as if in a procession. Nobody had decided it would be a procession. It simply was one. Milim walked at the front, beside Mau, as Guardian, with her chin up. Umaru walked behind her, holding her sign, which still said *THE MINISTRY OF FORT AFFAIRS DOES NOT AUTHORIZE ENTRY (YET.)*, because she had picked it up without thinking and nobody had told her to put it down.

At the door of the real house, Diablo opened it for them, and bowed, very low, to Frieren specifically.

"Your Majesty," said Diablo.

Frieren inclined her head, graciously, from Mau's back.

Behind them, in the yard, Rimuru sat down on the bench round the ink tree, put his face in his hands, and laughed until he could not breathe.



The second decree was the bath.

"I'm covered in mimic," said Frieren, in the hall, still on his back. "Four mimics."

"I know," said Mau. "I can feel it. On my neck."

"Take me to the bath."

"I can't take you into the—"

"The small one," said Frieren. "The one with the slate." She paused. "Write *queen* on the slate."

He carried her across the yard to the north side, to the little bathhouse with its own small door. The slate by the door already had three names on it for the afternoon, in three different hands. Mau stood in front of it, with Frieren on his back, and looked at the chalk.

Then he rubbed out the first name, which was *Senku: temperature study (2)*, and wrote, very small: *Q.*

Frieren, on his back, looked at the *Q* for a long moment.

"Bigger," she said.

He made it bigger.

She slid off his back at the door of the little bathhouse, at last, after nearly an hour, and stood on her own feet in the snow, soaked and dripping and composed. She held the grimoire against her chest. She looked up at him.

"Wait here," she said. "Subject."

"In the snow?"

"On the bench. By the door." She considered. "You can sit."

"Thank you, Your Majesty."

"You're welcome," said Frieren, and went in, and latched the door.

He sat on the bench by the door of the little bathhouse in the snow for an hour, while steam came out under the eaves and the small window of Senku's glass went white. Twice, people came round the corner of the north wing, saw him sitting there, and turned round and went away again without a word. The second time it was Senku, with a thermometer, who looked at the slate, and at the large *Q*, and at Mau on the bench, and closed his eyes, and turned round, and went back to the workshop.



The third decree was the hoodie.

She came out of the little bathhouse pink to the ears, with her hair wrapped up in a towel and the grimoire, dried now, under her arm, and looked at him on the bench.

"Your hoodie," she said.

"What about it?"

"I want to wear it."

He looked at her. "It's in the closet."

"I know where it is," said Frieren. "Get it."

So he went and got it. Up the main stair, down the family corridor, into the east room, past the shelf standing in the middle of the floor, to the closet in the inner corner, under Rimuru's loft, where it hung on its own hook, by itself, where it had always hung. Orange and dark blue, with the name of a racing team on it, from a year he could not place, in a world he could not remember. The thing he had woken up in, in the clearing, the first morning of his life.

He brought it down to her. She put it on, over her dress, in the hall.

It was enormous on her. The hem came to her knees. The sleeves came past her hands by a long way, so that she had to push them back, and they fell down again at once, and she pushed them back again. The hood, when she put it up over her damp hair, came down over her eyes, and she had to tip her head back to see out from under it.

She looked at herself in the dark glass of the hall window for a long time.

Then she turned round and looked at him, from under the hood, with the sleeves over her hands.

"It's too big," said Frieren.

"It's my size."

"I know," she said. "That's why."

She did not take it off. She wore it for the rest of the day, and all of the next, and Mau did not see it again on its hook for some time after that.



The crown was not a decree. Frieren had not asked for a crown. She said so, several times, at length, in the common room that afternoon, with considerable dignity, while it was being made.

"I don't need a crown," said Frieren. "Queens in my world didn't always wear them. Some of them wore circlets. Some of them wore nothing at all. It's not the crown that makes a queen."

"It's a little bit the crown," said Marin.

Marin was at the long table with Wakana and Coco and a circle of oak, and paint, and a basket of dried flowers from Daphne's barn that Daphne had handed over with a long-suffering face. Wakana had cut the circle in under an hour, from a curl of deadfall oak, with no nails and no joins, so that it was one piece all the way round. Marin was painting it gold. Coco was not allowed to paint anything on it, because Coco's hands sometimes drew shapes without asking, and Qifrey was sitting beside her to make sure. So Coco was choosing the flowers instead, very seriously, one at a time.

Fern was against the crown.

"Don't encourage her," said Fern. "She'll be impossible."

"She's already impossible," said Marin, painting. "This way she'll be impossible *and* gold."

Agott, who had been reading in the corner and pretending not to care, came over in the middle of the afternoon without a word, took the circle from Marin, and lettered something on the inside of it, very small, in ordinary ink, in her precise hand. Then she gave it back and returned to her book. Marin looked at what she had written and burst out laughing.

It said: *QUEEN (ONE DAY). VALID FOR: MAU ONLY.*

Umaru made a pass to go with it. Of course she did. She made it at the toll booth, on a card of stiff paper with the Ministry's seal, and brought it to the long table with enormous ceremony. It said: *ROYAL DECREE PASS. HOLDER: FRIEREN (QUEEN). SUBJECTS: ONE (MAU). EXPIRES: TOMORROW, MIDNIGHT. NOT VALID ON THE FORT.*

"*Not valid on the fort?*" said Frieren.

"The fort," said Umaru, "is a sovereign nation."

Frieren looked at her from under the enormous hood for a long moment. Then she inclined her head, as one sovereign to another, and accepted the pass.

They crowned her before dinner. Nobody planned it. Marin simply finished the last petal, and held the crown up, gold, with Coco's dried flowers round it, red and yellow and blue, and the whole common room went quiet. Frieren was sitting in the reading chair by the window, which Diablo had carried down from the east room at her request, in the orange hoodie, with the grimoire on her knees.

Marin walked across the common room and put the crown on her head, over the hood.

It slid down slightly. The hood was too big. Frieren pushed it back up with one sleeve-covered hand, and it stayed, a little crooked, gold and flowered, on top of the orange hood.

She did not say anything. She did not need to. She sat in the reading chair in the hoodie and the crooked crown with the grimoire on her knees and looked out at the whole common room, at all of them looking back at her, and her face did the thing again. The thing from the round room. Very old and very quiet and past happy.

Then she looked at Mau, standing by the long table.

"Dinner," said Frieren. "Carry me."



At dinner, Eren made the mistake.

He did not mean to. He was sitting across from the reading chair, which had been carried to the end of the long table, where Seiko's high seat looked down on it with an expression of grudging professional respect. He watched Mau bring Frieren her bowl, and set it down, and then, on instruction, cut her bread into small pieces. He watched Frieren eat one piece and say *more butter, subject*. He watched Mau put more butter on it. And Eren, who had been a soldier, and had stood in a great many lines and taken a great many orders, and had strong feelings about crowns, opened his mouth.

"Is this," said Eren, "going to be a thing? Crowns? Because I'm not—"

"Eren," said Umaru.

She was sitting beside him, on the bench, in her hood, with the Ministry's seal on the pass in front of her. She did not look up from her soup.

"Don't bother the lovebirds," said Umaru. "It's a crown that only commands Mau. For one day." She took a spoonful of soup. "It's in the pass. *Subjects: one.*"

Eren looked at her. Then at Frieren, in the crooked crown and the orange hoodie, being fed buttered bread. Then at Mau, buttering.

"As long as she doesn't tell me what to do," said Eren slowly, "I won't complain."

"Eren," said Frieren, from the reading chair.

Eren froze.

The whole long table froze.

Frieren looked at him across the table, from under the hood and the crooked crown, with her face perfectly serene.

"Pass the salt," said Frieren.

There was a very long silence.

Eren looked at the salt. It was beside his elbow. He looked at Frieren. He looked at Umaru, beside him, who had put down her spoon and was watching with enormous interest. He looked at Mikasa, on his other side, whose face was perfectly still and whose eyes, very slightly, were laughing.

Slowly, Eren picked up the salt, and passed it down the table.

Frieren took it.

"That," she said, "was a request." She salted her soup. "Not a decree." She handed the salt back to Mau, who put it down. "Thank you, Eren."

Eren sat back on the bench, very slowly, and did not say anything for the rest of the meal. Beside him, Mikasa, for the first time anyone at the table could remember, laughed out loud at dinner. It was short, and surprised, and over almost before it started. Eren turned and stared at her. She looked straight ahead at her bowl, pink to the ears, and did not do it again. But Eren kept looking at her, for a long time, after that.



That night, in the east room, Frieren read the grimoire in the reading chair by the window, in the hoodie, with the crown on the shelf in the middle of the floor where she had put it. The fourth decree of the day had been that Mau hold the lamp.

He held the lamp. He sat on the floor beside the reading chair with the lamp in his hand and his back against the wall, and held it at the angle she said, while she read. Every so often she said *higher*, or *left*, or *don't move*. He did not move.

"What does it do?" he said, at last, much later. "The spell."

Frieren did not answer at once. She turned back to a page near the beginning, the one she had read first, on the floor of the round room, soaked, while blue sparks fell around her. She read it again.

Then she put the grimoire down on her knees, and stood up, in the enormous hoodie, and went to the east window.

It was cold outside. Colder than it had been for a week. The glass of the window had frost on it, at the corners, in the dark, in thin white feathers.

Frieren put one finger against the glass, very lightly, in the middle of the frost. Her lips moved.

The frost moved.

It did not melt. It moved. The thin white feathers of it began to grow, and curve, and branch, across the dark glass, slowly, the way frost grows on a cold morning, but faster, and with purpose. Lines. Shapes. A trunk. Branches spreading out from it, wide, a great crown of them, drawn in frost on the window. And under the branches, two small figures. Sitting. Side by side. With their shoulders touching.

Frieren looked at it.

Then she reached up, quickly, with the sleeve of the hoodie, and wiped it away.

"It draws what you're thinking of," she said, to the window, not turning round. "In frost. That's all. On a cold window." She paused. "It doesn't do anything. It's completely useless."

Mau sat on the floor with the lamp in his hand and did not say anything at all.

Frieren stood at the window for a moment longer, in the hoodie, with her back to him. Then she came back, and sat down in the reading chair, and picked up the grimoire again, and opened it.

"Higher," she said. "Subject."

He held the lamp higher.

Some time after midnight, he noticed that she had stopped turning pages. She was asleep, in the reading chair, in the orange hoodie, with the hood fallen back and the grimoire open on her chest under one hand.

The fifth decree, issued an hour earlier, had been: *don't leave until I'm asleep*.

He put the lamp down. He did not leave. He sat on the floor beside the reading chair in the dark, with his back against the wall, in the light of the snow through the window, where there was no frost any more. Only, in the very corner of the glass, low down, where her sleeve had not quite reached, a single small white shape. A leaf.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he fell asleep too, sitting up, on the floor, where he was.
