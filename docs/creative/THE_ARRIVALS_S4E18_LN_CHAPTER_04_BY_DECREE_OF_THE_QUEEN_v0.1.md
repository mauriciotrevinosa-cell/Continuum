*When: Day 113, the second day of the reign. While the queen gave decrees, the leftover beams were still stacked in the timber yard, waiting for a reason.*

The queen woke at dawn, in the reading chair, in the orange hoodie, and found her subject asleep on the floor beside her with his back against the wall and his mouth open.

She looked at him for some time.

Then she nudged him with her foot.

"Breakfast," said Frieren. "Subject."

Mau woke, and looked up at her, and understood at once that it was still the second day.



The decrees came all morning.

Breakfast in the reading chair, which Diablo carried back down to the end of the long table without being asked. Tea, poured from the left, not the right. The good honey, the jar Momo kept on the high shelf for birthdays, which Momo handed over with a face that said she was keeping a ledger of her own. A walk round the yard, on Mau's back, twice, past the ink tree, while the whole house pretended not to watch from the windows. A dried pear, peeled. A second dried pear, not peeled, because she had changed her mind.

"By decree of the queen," said Frieren, at mid-morning, in the east room, standing in the middle of the floor in the hoodie and the crooked crown, "the shelf goes by the window."

Mau looked at the shelf. It was standing where it had stood since moving day, in the middle of the floor, with a grimoire on it, and his comb, and a cup, and now, as of last night, a gold oak crown with dried flowers.

"You said by the window *blocks the light*," said Mau.

"That was before I was queen."

"Does the light change when you're queen?"

"Yes," said Frieren.

He moved the shelf to the window. It took a while, because she changed her mind about exactly where by the window four times. In the end it stood under the east sill, beside the cars and Ori's stone, a little to the left. It blocked the bottom third of the light. Frieren looked at it for a long time with her head on one side.

"Good," she said.

"Good?"

"For today," said Frieren.



She issued the decree about the loft at lunch.

It was not, strictly, a decree. Agott's lettering on the inside of the crown was perfectly clear, and so was the Ministry's pass, which Frieren had propped against her cup: *SUBJECTS: ONE (MAU). NOT VALID ON THE FORT.* But Frieren was in the reading chair at the end of the long table, in the orange hoodie, with the crown on, and Rimuru was at the cold-hearth end, in his place, eating, and she looked at him across the whole length of the table for a long, thoughtful moment, and the table went quiet, the way it did when Frieren looked at someone like that.

"Rimuru," said Frieren.

Rimuru looked up. He had a piece of bread halfway to his mouth.

"Do you want," said Frieren, "to still have a loft?"

The common room went completely silent.

Rimuru put the bread down.

Everyone in the house knew about the loft. The loft over the closet in the inner corner of the east room, which Wakana had built in the Muslin, *small apartments had them*, and rebuilt in the real house exactly the same, because Rimuru slept there. Rimuru, who sleep-travelled, and had once been found asleep as a slime on Frieren's stomach, in the one room in the house nobody knocked on after dark. Rimuru, who had kept his own room downstairs as a daytime office, with Diablo outside the door, and climbed up the little ladder to the loft above Mau and Frieren's closet every night, as a slime, and slept there, and had slept there for a very long time.

"By decree of the queen," said Frieren, serenely, "there shall be changes."

"*Frieren-sama!*"

Fern was on her feet at the long table. Her face had gone red.

"You can't decree *Rimuru*," said Fern. "It's on the pass. It's on the crown. *Agott wrote it inside the crown.* It only commands Mau. You *can't*—"

"I'm only asking," said Frieren.

"You said *by decree*!"

"I said *there shall be changes*," said Frieren. "I didn't say what changes." She looked at Rimuru again, over her cup. "I'm only asking if he wants to still have a loft."

Fern opened her mouth. Then she closed it. Then she sat down, very slowly, and put her face in her hands, and said something into them that nobody could hear and that Stark, beside her, later refused to repeat.

Rimuru had not said anything.

He was sitting very still at the cold-hearth end of the table, looking at Frieren. Not offended. Not hurt. Looking at her the way he had looked at the stone footings on the seventieth morning. The way he had looked at the gate in the north field. The way a builder looks at a problem he has just seen for the first time, and has realised has been there all along.

"Not by decree," said Rimuru slowly. "Not because you're queen."

Frieren waited.

"But you gave me an idea," said Rimuru.



He explained it on the floor of the common room, after lunch, with the plan of the real house unpinned from the wall and laid out on the boards, the way Senku had once laid out his enormous drawing. The whole house gathered round. Frieren watched from the reading chair, in the crown.

"We built this house," said Rimuru, "for the people in it. On the hundredth day. Everyone in ink. Rem in pencil. Every room full." He put his finger on the plan. "And two days later, Seika came. And she's sleeping on a mattress on the floor of Kessoku's room, because there was nowhere else."

At the back, by the fort, Seika, in the ugly coat, did not say anything. She did not look as if she minded the mattress.

"And Rem's coin is next," said Rimuru. "On the List. Second line." He looked at Rem, at the far end, by the teapot. "When Ram comes, where does she sleep?"

Rem did not answer. She looked at the plan.

"And after Ram," said Rimuru, "someone else. And someone else. The coins don't stop. Not for years, maybe." He looked down at the plan. "Every time someone comes, we're going to find they're sleeping on a floor. And then we're going to build them a room. *After.*" He shook his head. "That's backwards. That's what the Muslin was for. That's how you build when you don't know what's coming." He looked up. "We know what's coming. People. One at a time. For years."

He picked up a pencil.

"So we stop building after," said Rimuru. "We build *before*." He leaned over the plan, to the west side, where Wakana had drawn the dotted wing, *not promises, places*, and where Fern had drawn one small square in pencil on the hundredth day, *for whoever comes*. "The west wing. Now. Not when we need it. Now. In stages. A frame like the main house, smaller. A few rooms at a time." He drew, quickly, in his cheerful untidy lines. "Seika's. Next to Kessoku's wall, if we turn the corridor here." He drew. "Ram's. Next to Rem's turn, if Rem wants." He looked at Rem. Rem, very slowly, nodded. He drew. "And one more."

He drew one more small square. He did not write anything in it.

"One empty room," said Rimuru. "Always. Built, and finished, and ready, with a bed in it and the stove laid. Before anyone needs it." He put the pencil down. "And when someone comes and takes it, we build the next empty one. So there's always one. So nobody who comes to this house ever sleeps on a floor again." He looked round the circle. "That's the change."

Nobody said anything.

Then Wakana, kneeling at the edge of the plan, very quietly, said: "I've been sorting the beams."

Everyone looked at him.

"Since the day before Seika came," said Wakana. "The ones left over from the frame. I didn't know what for." He looked at the plan. "That's enough for the west wing's first bent. Maybe the first three rooms."

Rimuru looked at him for a long moment.

Then he looked across the common room at the reading chair.

"Your Majesty," said Rimuru, "I'm keeping the loft."

"I know," said Frieren.

"I'm keeping it because I like it," said Rimuru. "Not because I'm allowed."

"I know," said Frieren.

"But I'm putting a door on the ladder," said Rimuru. "A small one. That latches from your side." He paused. "So that if you ever want the room to yourselves, you can just close it."

Frieren looked at him for a long time from under the crown.

"That," she said, "is an excellent idea."

"I know," said Rimuru. "You gave it to me."



Umaru wanted a lounge.

She did not wait for the plan to be pinned back up. She stood up on the bench in her hood and her sash and the Ministry's seal, before Rimuru had even finished, and pointed at the west wing.

"*A LOUNGE*," said Umaru.

"Umaru—"

"A *gigantic* lounge. In the west wing. The size of the common room. *Bigger.* With a sunken floor. And cushions all the way round. And the kotatsu built in. *Four* kotatsu. And a shelf for every game anyone ever remembers how to make. And a door that only the Ministry has the key to." She drew herself up. "The Ministry of Fort Affairs formally requests a Ministry Lounge."

"No," said Fern.

"*Fern*—"

"No," said Wakana.

"*Wakana*—"

"No," said Rimuru, gently.

Umaru looked round the circle. At Fern, with the ledger. At Wakana, kneeling by the plan, who had just finished counting how many rooms the leftover beams would make. At Rimuru, who had just said *one empty room, always*. At the small square in pencil with nothing written in it.

She sat down on the bench, slowly.

"...It was worth asking," said Umaru.

"It's always worth asking," said Rimuru.

Umaru did not say anything. She sat on the bench in her hood, looking at the plan on the floor. At the west wing. At the small empty square.

She looked at it for a long time.

Mau, watching her from beside the reading chair, saw her face change. Not much. Just a little. The way it had changed on the night of the forty-first, in the toll booth, with the shutter down, when she had told him about the coin she had moved twenty-one times.

She did not say anything else. Not then.



At midnight, the reign ended.

It ended in the east room, by the light of the snow. Frieren was standing in the middle of the floor in the orange hoodie, with the crown in her hands, looking at the shelf under the window. Mau was sitting on the bed, watching her.

Somewhere far off, across the yard, the bell in the tower over Arrival House rang twelve.

Frieren listened to all twelve. Then she put the crown down on the bed, very carefully, beside him.

"It's over," she said.

"Yeah."

"You were an adequate subject."

"Thank you, Your Majesty."

"I'm not Your Majesty any more." She looked at the shelf under the window. At the bottom third of the light it blocked. At the cars, and Ori's stone, beside it.

Then she went over to it, put both hands on the side of it, and began, slowly, by herself, to push it back toward the middle of the floor.

Mau watched her.

"The decree's expired," said Frieren, pushing. "So the shelf goes back to where it was." She got it to the middle of the floor, where the boards were slightly paler from where it had stood for thirteen days, and lined it up exactly with the pale patch, and stepped back. "Temporarily."

"You moved it *yourself*."

"I'm not queen any more," said Frieren. "I have to do things myself now." She looked at the shelf, back in the middle of the floor, with the grimoire on it, and his comb, and the cup. "We'll argue about it."

"When?"

"Slowly," said Frieren. "Over a long time."

She came and sat down beside him on the bed, in the orange hoodie, with the hood fallen back. She did not take it off.

"You can keep the hoodie," said Mau.

"I know," said Frieren. "I already decided."

"That's not a decree any more."

"No," she agreed. She leaned her shoulder against his. "It's just mine now."

They sat like that for a while. The snow light lay on the floor, and the shelf stood in the middle of it, where it belonged, temporarily.

"Frieren."

"Mm."

"It's over," said Mau. "The decree. The crown. All of it." He looked at the crown on the bed beside him, Wakana's oak and Marin's gold and Coco's flowers going soft at the edges. "You're still my queen."

Frieren did not answer at once.

Then she said, "Idiot," very quietly, and turned, and pushed him over onto his back on the bed with one hand flat on his chest, and lay down on top of him. All of her. The whole small weight of her, in the orange hoodie, with her chin on his breastbone and her hair everywhere and her sleeves over her hands, the way a cat lies down on the one warm thing in a room and dares anyone to move it.

He did not move it.

"Idiot," she said again, into the hoodie. It came out muffled. It did not sound like a decree.

He put one hand on her back. Up in the loft above the closet, nothing stirred.

After a while he said, "Tell me how you got it."

She lifted her head. "Got what?"

"The grimoire."

"You were there."

"I know," said Mau. "Tell me anyway."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment, down the length of her own nose, from very close. Then she sat up. Not off him. Just up, cross-legged on his stomach, in the hoodie with the sleeves over her hands, and he saw her face change into the face she wore when she was going to tell something properly, from the beginning, and nobody was going to hurry her.

"It started," said Frieren, "a long time ago."

"How long?"

"Long enough that Himmel was the one pulling." She held up one sleeve. "The first one was in a ruin in the north. It said mimic. I opened it. It was dark." She paused, for weight. "Himmel pulled me out by the boots. He laughed for a week. Eisen didn't laugh. Eisen just held the other boot."

"How many?"

"I stopped counting." The sleeve came down; the other came up. "There was one in a tower that had three of them in a row, and they were *all* mimics, and I knew they were all mimics, and I opened all three, because the fourth one might not have been, and there *wasn't* a fourth one, Mau. There was a wall." Her eyes had gone very wide and very indignant. "Who builds a tower with three mimics and no fourth chest?"

"A monster."

"A *monster*," she agreed, with feeling. "There was one that spat me out because I had too many grimoires in my bag. There was one in a swamp that was so old it had moss on its teeth. Heiter said that one was a sign from the goddess and I should stop. I didn't stop. Fern has pulled me out of," she counted on the sleeve, gave up, "*several.* Stark has pulled me out of fewer, because Stark is scared of them, which is fair, because they bite."

He was laughing. Very quietly. Under her. She ignored it, the way she ignored the weather.

"And none of them," said Frieren, "*none of them*, in all that time, ever had anything inside but teeth." She leaned down over him, until her nose was an inch from his. "Every person I have ever travelled with has told me to stop opening them. Every single one. For centuries. *It's always a mimic, Frieren.* *The spell is ninety-nine percent, Frieren.*" She sat back up, triumphant, in the dark. "And on the hundred-and-twelfth day, in a round room, under a winged seal, the one in a hundred was a mimic *and* it had a grimoire. Both. At once." She folded her sleeves. "So they were all wrong. For centuries. And I was right."

"You were eaten."

"I was *right*," said Frieren, "and *eaten*. Those aren't opposites." She considered. "That's the whole story."

"What's in it?"

"That part," said Frieren, "is a secret."

She lay back down on top of him, as if she had never got up, chin on his breastbone, satisfied with the whole history of the world. Her breathing went long and slow almost at once.

Mau lay under her in the dark and looked at the ceiling and did not stop smiling for a long time.



Up in the loft above the closet, Rimuru lay very flat on his pillow and very still, and did not say anything, because there was nothing in the world that needed saying, and because he had heard every word and was not going to admit it.

*Rimuru.*

Ciel. Only to him. Very quietly.

*The Noise has started to press again.*

He did not move.

*Softly,* said Ciel. *Not at anything. Not at the gate. Not at the walls. It is not reaching. It is leaning.* A pause. *As if it knows something is coming. And not yet.*

Rimuru lay in the dark and listened, below him, to an elf asleep on top of a man who was nearly asleep, and to the man's breathing slowing to match hers.

*Later,* he told her.

*Rimuru—*

*Tomorrow,* said Rimuru. *Tell me tomorrow. Not tonight.*

Ciel was quiet for a moment.

*Tomorrow,* she agreed.

Below him, the last of the reign went out of the room like a candle, and the hoodie stayed.
