*When: Days 114 and 115: the night after the reign, and the morning the west wing's first bent went up.*

Umaru came to the long table on the hundred-and-fourteenth night without her hood.

It was late. The common room was nearly empty. Fern was at the head of the table with the ledger, as she was every night, writing up the day: *west wing: footings checked, Wakana; bent one cut, Wakana and the cooper; pegs, the cooper; tomorrow, raise.* Rimuru was at the cold-hearth end with the west wing plan, drawing. Mau was beside him, holding the lamp, because Rimuru had asked and because his arms had got used to holding lamps.

Umaru came in from the fort by the arch. She came very quietly, which was not how Umaru came anywhere. She came across the common room in her socks and her sash and stopped at the end of the long table.

She was not wearing her hood.

Mau had seen her without it before. A few times. On the night of the forty-first, in the toll booth with the shutter down. In the two weeks he had been in bed, when she had come in with the fort's baskets. Once, a long time ago, in another house, on a night he did not like to think about. Without the hood, she looked older, and smaller, and much more like someone who had once been two different people, in another world, and had stopped needing to be.

Fern put down her pen.

Rimuru put down his pencil.

"I have a request," said Umaru. "For the west wing."

Nobody said *lounge*. Nobody said anything.

"Not from the Ministry," said Umaru. "From me."



She did not sit down. She stood at the end of the long table with her hands at her sides, the way you stand when you have rehearsed something many times and are afraid that if you sit down you will lose it.

"I want a room," said Umaru. "Not for me. I have a room. With Suika. With a door." She swallowed. "A temporary one. In the west wing. For someone."

"For whoever comes," said Rimuru gently. "That's the empty one. That's what it's for."

"No," said Umaru. "Not whoever. *Someone.*" She looked at the plan on the table, at the west wing, at the small square Rimuru had drawn and left empty. "The empty room is for anyone. That's good. That's right. But this—" She stopped. "This is for someone. And I haven't decided."

The common room was very quiet.

"You haven't decided," said Fern carefully, "who."

"I know who," said Umaru.

She did not say his name. She did not need to. Mau knew. Fern knew. Rimuru knew. Everyone who had been at the long table on the fortieth day knew, and everyone who had seen the drawer marked *LATER* in the toll booth knew, and everyone who had heard, through the walls that came out, what Umaru had said in the toll booth on the night of the forty-first, with the shutter down, to Mau.

*He already gave up so much for me.*

*If he comes,* Mau had said, *you'll have two older brothers.*

*But not yet,* she had said. *Not now. Not this winter.*

*Ready and safe.*

"I know who," said Umaru again. "I haven't decided *whether*." She looked down at her socks. "Every day, I don't decide. Every day I open the drawer, and look at the coin, and close the drawer. And every day, it's not a *no*. It's not a *yes*. It's just—" She stopped. "It's just *not yet*."

"That's allowed," said Fern. "It's your coin. *Not yet* is allowed for as long as you want."

"I know," said Umaru. "But there's nowhere."

She looked up.

"If I decided yes," she said. "Tomorrow. If I opened the drawer and held the coin and thought of him. And the voice said *you are sure?* And I said yes." Her voice had started to shake. "Where would he *go*? He'd come to Arrival House, and then he'd come here, to the house, and there'd be no room. He'd sleep on a mattress on the floor of my room, with Suika. Like Seika. Except Seika *wanted* the floor, to be near Nijika." She swallowed. "He'd come into a house where nobody had made any room for him. Where there was no place that was his. Because I hadn't decided. Because I'd kept saying *not yet* until the yes came all at once." Her hands had closed at her sides. "That's not *ready*. That's not *safe*. That's just— that's just me deciding no, every day, by not having anywhere to put the yes."

Nobody said anything.

"So I want a room," said Umaru. "For him. Temporary. Not with his name on it. Not *for* anything. Just— there. Built. With a bed. And a window." Her voice cracked. "So that if I decide, there's somewhere. So that it's a real choice. So that *not yet* is a choice, and not just because there's nowhere."

She stood at the end of the long table, without her hood, in her socks, and shook.

"I want the room to exist," said Umaru, "so that the choice exists."



Fern looked at her for a long time.

Then she picked up her pen, and opened the ledger to the page where the plan of the west wing was copied, small, in her neat hand, the way she copied everything. And next to the small empty square Rimuru had drawn, *one empty room, always*, she drew a second small square.

In pencil.

Inside it, very small, she wrote: *(temporary). For someone.*

And under that, after a pause, one letter: *U.*

She turned the ledger round so that Umaru could see.

Umaru looked at it. She looked at it for a long time, standing at the end of the table, with her hands at her sides.

"You didn't put his name," she said.

"No," said Fern.

"Good," said Umaru. Her voice was almost nothing. "Don't. Not yet."

"I won't."

Rimuru had picked up his pencil again. Without saying anything, he leaned over the west wing plan on the table, and beside the small empty square he had drawn that afternoon, he drew a second one, exactly the same size, with a window on the south side, toward the yard and the ink tree, and a door that opened onto the corridor two rooms down from where Umaru's door would be, if she ever wanted it to be two rooms down.

"There," he said. "Next to the empty one. With a south window." He did not look up from the plan. "Wakana can cut it in the second bent. It'll be up in a week."

Umaru looked at the drawing.

"It's not a lounge," she said, in a small cracked voice.

"No," said Rimuru. "It's better."

"It's *much* better," said Umaru, and burst into tears.



She cried for a long time, at the end of the long table, without her hood. Fern came round the table and stood beside her, not touching, the way Fern stood beside people, until Umaru leaned on her, and then Fern put an arm round her, very stiffly, and held her, and looked at the ceiling. Rimuru went on drawing the window, very carefully, and did not look up. Mau put the lamp down on the table and went and stood on Umaru's other side.

"Two older brothers," said Umaru, into Fern's shoulder.

"Two," said Mau.

"You said. In the booth."

"I said."

"When Frieren wants a date," said Umaru, muffled, "I'll have someone else to bother. You said." She sniffed. "And he's very good at being bothered. He's had years of practice." A pause. "He's going to *hate* sharing."

"He can fight me for it," said Mau.

"He'll *lose*."

"Probably."

"He won't even try," said Umaru. "He'll just sigh. He's very good at sighing." She lifted her head off Fern's shoulder. Her face was blotched and wet. "Not yet."

"Not yet," said Mau.

"But there's a room."

"There's a room."

Umaru stood there a moment longer, between them. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, very thoroughly, and reached into the pocket of her sash, and took out her hood, and put it back on. Up. Over her hair. Down to her eyebrows.

She looked out from under it at the three of them.

"The Ministry of Fort Affairs," said Umaru, in her ordinary voice, almost, "thanks the long table for its consideration."

"The long table," said Fern, gravely, "acknowledges the Ministry."

Umaru nodded once, very seriously, and turned round, and walked back across the common room, through the arch, into the fort, and pulled the curtain behind her.

A moment later, faintly, from the toll booth, they heard a drawer slide open. And then, after a long pause, slide shut again.



They raised the west wing's first bent the next morning.

It was smaller than the main frame. Two posts and a beam, cut from Wakana's sorted leftovers, pegged with the cooper's oak pegs, lying flat on the new stone footings Wakana and Aira had laid along the west side of the house. Not the whole settlement on the ropes this time. Not everyone. But a lot of them. The house, mostly. And the fort.

Seika was on the second rope, in the ugly coat, between Nijika and Kita, pulling, with her face completely flat. She had said, at breakfast, that she did not need a room, she was fine on the mattress, and had been told by Nijika that she was getting one anyway, and had said *fine*, in a voice that meant something else.

Rem was on the third rope, with Lise, the girl from the old inn, one of the few who had stayed. Rem had said nothing at breakfast. But she had gone out to the west footings before anyone else was up, and stood for a long time at the place where Rimuru had drawn a room next to her turn, and come back, and poured everyone's tea with a smile nobody had seen on her face since the fortieth day.

Umaru was on the last rope.

She was in her hood. She pulled with her whole weight, harder than anyone, with her teeth gritted, and when Wakana said *three* and the bent rose off the stone and swung upright and dropped into its sockets with a deep wooden *thunk*, she did not cheer. She stood very still, holding her rope, looking up at the two posts and the beam against the sky.

Then she looked along the line of footings, past the first bent, to where the second would go. To where, in a week, there would be a room. With a south window. Toward the yard and the ink tree. For someone.

Mau, on the rope in front of her, felt her lean her forehead against his back for a moment. Just for a moment. Through his coat.

Then she let go, and stood up straight, and said, very loudly, in her Ministry voice: "*THE FORT REQUESTS THAT THE SECOND BENT BE RAISED BEFORE LUNCH.*"

It was raised before lunch the next day.
