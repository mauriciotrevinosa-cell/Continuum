*When: Days 116 and 117. The west wing's second bent was going up the same days. The gate was in its second week, and nobody had gone in since the queen.*

"There's no stage," said Seika.

She said it on the hundred-and-sixteenth afternoon, in the doorway of the music room, in the ugly coat, with her arms folded, looking at the corner where Kessoku played. The soup-pot drum. The stools. The rug. The private stair going up to Bocchi's room. Nothing else.

Kessoku looked at the corner.

"We don't need one," said Nijika. "We just play here. On the floor."

"You need one," said Seika.

"Onee-chan, it's a music room in a house, it's not a—"

"A band plays on a stage," said Seika. "Not on a rug. Not on the floor next to the soup pot." She looked at the corner. "Even if the stage is six inches high. Even if it's made of nothing. You step up onto it, and you're not in a room any more. You're on a stage." She paused. "It changes how you stand."

Nobody said anything.

Seika unfolded her arms.

"Who builds things here," she said. "The doll one?"



Wakana did not know what a pallet was.

Seika explained it to him in the workshop, with a piece of charcoal, on the end of the workbench, in the flat voice of someone who had built a great many things out of very little and did not intend to discuss it. A pallet was a flat platform of planks, she said, slatted, with blocks underneath to lift it off the ground, the kind of thing goods were stacked on in warehouses, in her world. You could get them for nothing. People threw them away. You could stack them, and join them, and lay boards across the top, and it made a floor. A raised floor. Strong enough to stand on. Strong enough to jump on.

"A stage," said Wakana.

"A stage," said Seika.

Wakana looked at her charcoal drawing for a long time.

"You built one," he said. "Before."

Seika did not answer at once.

"In a basement," she said at last. "In a hurry. With pallets from behind a convenience store and boards from a building site and secondhand amps." She looked at the drawing. "For somewhere to play. For a kid who wanted to play drums and had nowhere to do it."

Wakana did not ask who the kid was. He did not need to.

"No nails," he said.

Seika looked at him.

"In this house," said Wakana, apologetically. "It's a rule. Fern's treaty, from the Muslin. And the frame of the whole house." He looked at the drawing. "But pallets are easy. Pegs. And notches. I can make you eight by tomorrow."

"I'll make them," said Seika.

"You don't have to—"

"I'll make them," said Seika again. She picked up a saw from the bench and looked at it, testing the weight, the way someone looks at a tool they have not held for a long time and have not forgotten. "Show me the pegs."



She made them that night.

Nobody saw her do it. Wakana showed her the pegs, and the notches, and how to drawbore a joint so it pulled tight. Then he left her in the workshop with the offcuts from the west wing and a lamp, because she told him to. When Senku came up from his workroom at midnight to check on the box marked *NOT YET (AGAIN)*, she was still there, in the ugly coat with the sleeves pushed up, cutting slats by lamplight, with her face completely flat.

"Do you need—" said Senku.

"No," said Seika.

Senku went back to bed.

At three in the morning, Anko came by on her second round and saw a lamp moving in the music room. She looked in through the window. Seika was there, alone, kneeling in the corner where Kessoku played, fitting pallets together on the floor. Eight of them. Four by two. Pegging them. Laying boards across the top. Planing the edges smooth. She had moved the soup pot and the stools to the side. She did not look up.

Anko stood at the window for a while. Then she wrote in her notebook, *3 a.m. Music room. Seika. Building.*, and underlined nothing, and walked on.



Kessoku found it in the morning.

Nijika found it first, because Nijika was always up first. She came down the private stair from Bocchi's room, where she had been sitting with Bocchi until Bocchi fell asleep, and stopped on the bottom step.

The corner of the music room was not a corner any more.

It was a stage.

It was not large. It filled the corner, four pallets across and two deep, raised about a hand's breadth off the floor, a little more. The boards across the top were planed smooth and fitted so closely you could not have slid a knife between them. The edges were rounded, so you would not catch a foot. Someone had rubbed the whole surface with oil until it shone, faintly, in the grey morning light. The soup-pot drum stood at the back of it, in the middle, where a drum kit would stand. The stools stood in a row at the front. And at the back of the stage, against the wall, there was a board, painted white, propped up like a sign.

There was nothing written on it.

Seika was asleep on the floor in front of the stage, on her side, in the ugly coat, with the saw beside her and her head on her arm.

Nijika stood on the bottom step of the private stair for a long time.

Then she came across the music room, very quietly, and sat down on the edge of the stage, beside her sister, and put her hand on the oiled boards.

They were very smooth.

Seika opened one eye.

"Don't sit on the edge," she said, without lifting her head. "You'll warp it."

"You built it in one night," said Nijika.

"It's eight pallets." Seika closed her eye again. "It's nothing. It's six inches."

"It's *Starry*," said Nijika.

Seika did not say anything.

"The first one," said Nijika. Her voice had gone very small. "In the basement. You built it in a weekend. With pallets from behind the convenience store. You told me it was a temporary stage, until you could get a real one." She looked at the boards under her hand. "You never got a real one. You said the temporary one was good enough." She swallowed. "I played on it for years."

"It *was* good enough," said Seika, into her arm.

"You built it for me."

"I built it," said Seika, "because a band needs a stage. And you were in a band." She opened her eyes, both of them this time, and looked up at the ceiling of the music room. "That's all."

"Onee-chan."

"*That's all.*"

Nijika did not argue. She sat on the edge of the stage that her sister had told her not to sit on, with her hand flat on the oiled boards, and cried without making any sound, and Seika lay on the floor beside her and looked at the ceiling and did not tell her to stop.



The rest of Kessoku came down at seven.

Kita came first, and stopped, and said *oh*, and then said *oh* again, louder, and ran across the music room and climbed onto the stage and stood on it, in the middle, in her nightclothes, and turned round, and looked out at the empty room from six inches higher, with her face lit up.

"It's *different*," said Kita. "Standing up here. It's different."

"It changes how you stand," said Seika, from the floor.

Ryo came next, and looked at the stage for a long time without saying anything, and then walked round it, and then knelt down and looked underneath it at the pegs and the blocks and the notches, and then stood up and said, "It's very well made," which from Ryo was the equivalent of a parade, and stepped onto it, and stood at the left end with her bass, where a bassist would stand, and did not get off again.

Bocchi came last.

She came down the private stair very slowly, in her pink tracksuit, with her guitar, the way she came down every morning. She stopped on the bottom step, exactly where Nijika had stopped. She looked at the stage.

She did not move for a long time.

Then she looked at the floor in front of the stage, where Seika was sitting up now, with her back against the stage's edge, in the ugly coat, rubbing her eyes.

"Seika-san," said Bocchi.

Seika looked up at her.

"Thank you," said Bocchi. Her voice was almost nothing. "For— the stage. Again."

Seika looked at her for a long moment.

"Get on it," said Seika. "And practise."

Bocchi got on it. She stood at the right end of the stage, where a lead guitar would stand, beside the private stair, so that if anything made her jump she could be up it in two steps. She did not look at anybody. But she stood differently. Mau, who had come to the doorway of the music room at the sound of Kita's *oh*, saw it at once. She stood the way you stand on a stage.



Nijika painted the sign that afternoon.

She did it on the white board at the back of the stage, with Marin's red paint, in large uneven letters, while Seika watched from the doorway with her arms folded and her face completely flat.

*STARRY.*

And then, underneath, smaller, after a long pause, with Marin's finest brush:

*(2)*

She stepped back and looked at it.

"That's my club's name," said Seika, from the doorway.

"I know," said Nijika.

"You can't just—"

"It's your club," said Nijika. She did not turn round. "You built the stage. You're standing at the back with your arms folded. You're going to tell us our timing dragged." She put the brush down. "It's your club, Onee-chan. Wherever you are. That's how it works."

Seika did not say anything.

She stood in the doorway of the music room for a long time, looking at the sign. *STARRY (2).* In red. On a white board. On a stage made of eight pallets, six inches high, in the corner of a music room in a timber house with no nails, in a world with no electricity, where her little sister had been playing all winter on a rug next to a soup pot.

Then she turned round and walked away down the corridor, very fast, without a word.

Nijika did not go after her. She knew better.

But Mau, coming round the corner of the main stair a minute later with a basket of firewood, met Seika in the corridor, going the other way. And she was not walking fast any more. She had stopped by the window at the end, and was standing there with her back to him, looking out at the yard, at the ink tree, at the north field and the faint blue light past it, very straight, very still.

He did not say anything. He put down the firewood and went the other way.



That night, Kessoku played on the stage for the first time.

Not a show. Nobody had planned a show. They were only practising, the way they practised every night. But somebody had heard, and somebody had told somebody, and by the time Nijika counted them in on the soup pot there were thirty people in the music room, on the floor, on the benches, in the doorway, at the window from the yard, waiting.

Seika stood at the back. Arms folded. Ugly coat. By the door.

They played the song from the snow, and then the one Bocchi had written for someone getting better, and then three others. On a stage. Standing differently. Kita sang to the room instead of to the floor. Ryo did not move at all, but the bass came out of her bigger, somehow, from six inches higher. Bocchi played with her eyes shut, at the right end of the stage, by the private stair, and did not run up it once.

At the end, there was the usual silence. Then the usual noise.

Through it, from the back of the room, by the door, came Seika's voice, flat and clear and perfectly audible.

"Your timing dragged in the second verse," said Seika. "Nijika."

"I know," said Nijika, laughing, behind the soup pot.

"The pot lid's still flat."

"I *know*."

Seika looked at the stage. At the four of them on it, six inches up. At the sign behind them, red on white, *STARRY (2)*.

"That wasn't bad," said Seika.

She turned round and went out, before anyone could see her face. But Mau, who was nearest the door, saw it anyway, just for a moment, in the corridor, in the lamplight, before she got it closed again.

He did not tell anyone what it looked like.
