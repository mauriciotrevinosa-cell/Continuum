*When: Days 116 and 117: the invitation, and the weekly tea in the afternoon. That evening, Kessoku played the new stage for the first time.*

Echidna asked for two guests at the next tea, which she had never done, and she asked for them by name.

She asked Mau on the morning of the hundred-and-sixteenth, at the door of the library at the end of the north corridor, where he had gone to return a book Frieren had borrowed and forgotten. Echidna was in her chair inside the door, reading, in the soft yellow light that came from nowhere. She did not look up when he knocked.

"The tea," she said. "Tomorrow."

"Yes?"

"I would like to invite the sister." She turned a page. "And the girl with the guitar."

Mau looked at her.

"Seika," he said. "And Bocchi."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Echidna closed her book on one finger and looked at him, over the top of it, with that sharp, total interest.

"Because the sister can read music I cannot hear," she said. "And the girl with the guitar can play it." She opened her book again. "And I have wanted, for a very long time, to know what a song sounds like."



Bocchi said no, and then yes, and then no, and then hid in the cupboard under the private stair for an hour, and then came out and said yes.

Seika said, "Fine," without looking up from the pallet she was sanding, and that was all.

But that evening, Mau passed the music room and saw them both in the corner where Kessoku played, sitting side by side on the rug, by the soup pot. They had the small dark-bound book open between them. The one from Echidna's library. The book of songs written by hand, in some other world, by someone who was not anywhere any more. Seika was reading it, slowly, with her finger moving under the strange old notes, and saying something in a low voice. Bocchi was listening, with her guitar across her knees, and every so often she played two or three notes, very quietly, and Seika shook her head, or nodded, and read on.

They were still there when he came back two hours later.

Then Seika stood up, and closed the book, and gave it to Bocchi to keep, and went out toward the workshop, and did not come back that night. In the morning, the corner where they had sat was a stage.



The tea was at the agreed bell, on the hundred-and-seventeenth day, in the common room of the real house, at the long table. Not early. Not late.

There were ten. The seven, and Mau, and two guests.

Seika sat at the end of the table beside Echidna, in the ugly coat, with her arms folded, looking at the seven witches one after another with the flat assessing look she gave everyone who came through a door. The witches looked back. Daphne looked at her and went back to the bread. Minerva looked at her, very upright, and gave a small nod, soldier to soldier, though neither of them had been soldiers. Sekhmet, lying on the bench, opened one eye, looked at her for a long moment, and said, "You work too hard," and closed it again. Seika did not answer that.

Bocchi sat beside Carmilla.

She had not been told where to sit. She came in behind Seika, white as paper, holding her guitar in front of her like a shield, and stood in the doorway, and looked at the long table and the seven witches round it, and Mau thought she was going to run. Then Carmilla, at the quiet end, half behind Minerva's shoulder, moved over on the bench. Not much. A hand's width. Without looking up.

Bocchi went and sat in the space.

Neither of them said anything. Under the table, Mau saw, Carmilla's hand moved, once, very slightly, three times against the bench. The three notes. Bocchi's three notes, from the second tea, under the table. And Bocchi, after a moment, without looking at her, tapped them back.

Pride, at the end of the table, facing Mau, with her hands flat on the table, open, the way she had held them since the fourth tea, looked down the long table at all of it.

"Ten," she said quietly.

Mau poured the tea.



For a while they talked about the gate.

Everyone talked about the gate, now. Echidna had walked out to the red stake every day since it appeared and stood there looking at it, and had refused, politely, three times, to go in, and would not say why. Daphne wanted to know if anything in it was edible. Minerva wanted to know about the wolves, and whether anyone had been hurt, and when Mau told her about Eren's ribs and Maomao's bandages she looked at her own bandaged knuckles for a long moment and did not say anything. Satella, at Mau's left, very close, holding her ALWAYS card, wanted to know about the queen.

"She's not queen any more," said Mau.

"I heard," said Satella softly. "She wore your hoodie. And a crown. And you carried her." She looked down at her card. "For two days."

"It was a punishment," said Mau.

"Yes," said Satella. Her voice was very quiet, and very hoarse, and almost smiling. "I envy that very much."

Then Echidna put down her cup.

"The song," she said.



Bocchi stood up.

She did not mean to stand up. Mau saw that she did not mean to. She had been going to play sitting down, on the bench, the way she played everything when there were people. But when Echidna said *the song*, she stood up, all at once, the way you stand up on a stage, as if something under her feet had lifted her six inches higher. She stood beside the long table with her guitar, in her pink tracksuit, in front of seven witches and Mau and Seika, and she was shaking so hard her plectrum rattled against the strings.

Seika, at the end of the table, did not get up. She did not look at Bocchi. She opened the small dark book in front of her, on the table, to a page near the middle, and turned it round so that it faced Echidna.

"It's the eighth one," said Seika. "In the book." She did not unfold her arms. "It's in a minor key. It's sad. Then the middle goes up." She paused. "It's a love song. Somebody waiting for somebody."

Echidna looked down at the page. At the strange old notes, in faded ink, in a hand that had not moved for longer than anyone at the table had been alive. She did not say anything.

"We had to guess some of it," said Seika. "The notation's old. Some of the marks I don't know. The tempo's a guess. The middle's a guess." She looked at the page. "Bocchi-chan guessed better than me."

Bocchi, beside the table, made a small sound.

"So it's not right," said Seika. "Not exactly. It's not what they heard. Whoever wrote it." She looked at Echidna, at last. "It's what we think it was."

Echidna looked up from the page.

"That is all anyone has ever had," she said, "of anything."

Seika looked at her for a long moment.

Then she nodded, once, at Bocchi, without looking at her.

Bocchi played.



Mau would try, later, many times, to tell Frieren what it sounded like. He never managed it.

It was slow. That was the first thing. Slower than anything Kessoku played. It started low, on the bottom strings, a handful of notes that went down and down and stopped, and waited, and went down again. It was sad. Seika had been right about that. It was the sound of someone sitting in a room, in the evening, alone, listening for footsteps on a road. Bocchi played it with her eyes shut, standing beside the long table, and her hands had stopped shaking.

Then the middle went up.

It went up all at once, the way a window goes up in spring, and the notes climbed and climbed and opened out. It was not happy, exactly. It was hope. The sound of someone hearing, very far off, very faint, something on the road that might be footsteps, and standing up, and going to the door. Bocchi played it, and somewhere in the middle of it she stopped playing the notes in the book and started playing something that was not in the book, around them and under them, the way she did on the stage when she forgot where she was. And it was right. It was more right than the book.

Then it came down again. Not all the way. To the middle. And stopped there.

Not finished. Stopped. Waiting. At the door.

Bocchi opened her eyes.

Nobody at the long table said anything.

Satella had her hand over her mouth. Minerva was looking at the table, very hard, with her bandaged hands clenched in her lap. Daphne had stopped eating. Sekhmet's eyes were open, both of them, looking at the ceiling. Carmilla, at the quiet end, had come all the way out from behind Minerva's shoulder and was looking up at Bocchi with an expression Mau had never seen on her face. Pride sat at the end of the table, facing him, with her hands flat and open on the wood, and her eyes, which were very old, were wet.

Seika had not moved. She sat at the end of the table with her arms folded and her face completely flat, looking at the small dark book. But her jaw was very tight.

And Echidna.

Echidna was sitting very still. She was looking at nothing. At the air above the long table, where the last note had been. Her hands, on the table on either side of her cup, were trembling, very slightly.

"I read it," she said, at last. Her voice was not quite her voice. "A very long time ago. In a city that is not anywhere any more. I read it, and I kept it, the way I keep everything." She looked down at the page. "I knew it was a song. I knew the notes. I could have told you every one." She paused. "I did not know it sounded like that."

She looked up at Bocchi.

"I have read everything," said Echidna. "Everything. Every book I have ever been able to reach, in every place I have ever been. I keep all of it. That is what I am. I *keep*." She looked at the page again. "And there are things in every book I have kept that I have never had. Never once. Because they cannot be read. Only heard. Only seen. Only *done*." She stopped. "I have always known that. I did not know how much of it there was."

Nobody said anything.

"Thank you," said Echidna.

It was not a word Mau had heard her say before. He thought, from the way the other six witches went still, that none of them had heard it either.

Bocchi stood beside the long table with her guitar, and turned very red, and then very white, and then, without a word, sat down on the bench again, beside Carmilla, very fast, and put her face in her hands.

Carmilla, after a moment, put one hand on her back.



At the end of the hour, exactly at the end, the witches rose.

At the door, as always, the first witch stopped and turned. But this time she did not look at Mau. She looked down the table at Seika, who had not got up.

"You played," said Pride. "Once."

Seika did not answer.

"Your fingers," said Pride. "On the table. While she played. They were moving." She paused. "Not the way a listener's move."

Seika looked down at her own hands on the table. They had stopped moving. She did not unfold her arms.

"A long time ago," said Seika.

Pride looked at her for a long moment.

"The name was for someone smaller," she said quietly. "I understand." And went out into the yard.

Echidna stopped too. She stood at the door with the small dark book in her hands, which Seika had closed and given back to her, and looked at Seika and Bocchi, still at the table.

"The ninth," she said. "In the book. It is a song for a harvest." She paused. "I would like to know what it sounds like."

Seika looked at her.

"Next week," said Echidna. "If you would."

Seika did not say anything for a long time.

"Fine," she said.

Echidna inclined her head, and went out, with the book held against her chest, and Mau saw through the window, as she crossed the yard toward the north wing in the thin afternoon light, that she was walking more slowly than he had ever seen her walk. As if she were carrying something that might spill.

At the table, Bocchi lifted her face out of her hands.

"Seika-san," she said. "The ninth one. The harvest." She swallowed. "I don't know if I can—"

"You'll guess," said Seika. She stood up, and pushed the bench back, and put her hands in the pockets of the ugly coat. "You guessed better than me." She paused at the door. "The part in the middle. That wasn't in the book."

Bocchi went white.

"It was right," said Seika. "Do that again." And went out.
