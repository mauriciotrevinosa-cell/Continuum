## Chapter 14 — Not Twice

*When: The night of Day 116, the night before the weekly tea. The west wing's second bent is going up. Frieren was a queen four days ago, for two days, and has not stopped wearing the hoodie. The crackers table meets for the sixth time, in Seiko's room in the real house, through the wall from Momo's.*

"Not twice," said Seiko.

She said it before anyone had sat down. Before the tea was poured. She was on her cushion at the head of the low table, with the painted tin open beside her and the brazier glowing, and she said it to Maki, in the doorway, over the top of her fan.

Maki stopped.

"Five weeks," said Seiko. "Five tables. You've brought things. Small things. A bad dream. Yuta snoring. The smell of the incense the witches burn in the north wing on tea days." She lowered the fan an inch. "Small things. True things. But small." She looked at Maki. "You said *not tonight*. At the first table. And I said that's allowed once." She paused. "Not twice."

Maki did not say anything.

She stood in the doorway of Seiko's room with her hands at her sides. Behind her, in the corridor, Frieren had stopped too, with the orange hoodie on and the sleeves over her hands. And Mai, behind Frieren, with a blanket round her shoulders, the way she always came. And at the bottom of the doorframe, at the height of a cat, Turbo Granny, who had been complaining all the way down the corridor about the stairs and had gone quiet.

"I know," said Maki.

"Good," said Seiko. "Sit down."

They sat. Four cushions and one very small one. Seiko poured. The crackers went round. Turbo Granny got hers broken into pieces the size of a fingernail, and complained, and ate them.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Frieren, across the table from Maki, did not look at her. She looked at her tea. And her face did not do anything at all. Maki knew what that meant. Frieren was holding it. The thing Maki had told her by the river, six weeks ago. The floor and the sword and *destroy everything*. Holding it very carefully, at this table, with Mai sitting between them, eating a cracker, irritably.

Maki looked at her sister.

She could not say it. Not here. Not with Mai on the next cushion, close enough to touch. She had known that since the first table. She had said *not tonight* because she could not say the thing, and she could not bring something smaller and pretend it was the thing. And she could not bring nothing.

So she brought the other one.

"I have a granddaughter," said Maki.



Mai put her cracker down.

"You *what*," she said.

"A granddaughter," said Maki. She did not look at Mai. She looked at the brazier. "In the life I lived. With Yuta. Before this one." She was very calm. "We had a son. He had two children. A boy and a girl." She paused. "The girl's called Yuka."

Mai was staring at her.

"You had *grandchildren*," said Mai.

"I had grandchildren."

"With *Yuta*."

"With Yuta."

Mai looked at her sister for a long moment, with an expression that went through several things at once and settled, finally, on a kind of furious amazement.

"You never told me," she said.

"You never asked," said Maki.

"How would I *ask*? *Oh, by the way, Maki, did you happen to live an entire extra life and have grandchildren while I wasn't looking*—"

"She did," said Seiko, over her fan. "Be quiet. She's carrying something."

Mai closed her mouth.

Maki looked at the brazier.

"I have a coin," she said. "Like everyone. Since the bridge. I've had it in my pocket for a hundred days." She took it out. Small. Warm. She put it on the table, in front of her. "And I've thought about who. Every night. Not out loud. Yuta knows I'm thinking. He doesn't ask." She looked at the coin. "It's her. Yuka. It's always been her."

Nobody said anything.

"She was ten when Yuta died," said Maki. "In my life. In hers. I'd gone a few years before. She was the one who was always with him. *Grandpa's little girl*, everyone called her. She followed him round the house. She sat on the end of his bed when he was ill. He gave her his ring on a cord when she was six, on a beach, and told her it would watch over her until she was a real sorcerer." Her voice did not change. "And after he died, the family took the ring off her and gave it to her brother. Because they thought he was more suited."

Frieren had looked up.

"I know what happens to her," said Maki. "After. Some of it. I know she fights. I know she's loud, and brave, and stupid, and gets into everything, and hides things from people who love her because she doesn't want them to worry." She almost smiled. "I know who she gets that from."

"So bring her," said Turbo Granny. From her tiny cushion. With her mouth full. "What's the problem? You love her, she loves you, the coin works, done. Next."

Maki looked at the coin.

"She remembers me old," she said.

The table was quiet.

"She remembers a grandmother with grey hair and a cane," said Maki. "Who hit her brother with it every Sunday. Who was strict, and didn't say much, and taught her how to hold a spear when her parents said she was too young." She was very still. "If I bring her here, she'll come through that door and see me at eighteen. And Yuta at eighteen. With all our hair. Younger than her, nearly." She looked at the brazier. "She'll see the two people she buried, standing in a hall, looking like children."

Nobody said anything.

"And she'll know," said Maki. "Straight away. That we're the same people. And that we don't look it. And that everything she remembers about us is a thing that hasn't happened yet, here, to these bodies." Her hands were very still on the table. "I don't know if that's a gift or a cruelty. I've thought about it every night for a hundred days and I don't know."

The brazier ticked.

"That's what I came in with," said Maki. "Someone carry it."



"I'll carry it," said Mai.

Everyone looked at her.

She was sitting very straight on her cushion, between Maki and Frieren, with her blanket round her shoulders and her face doing the cool irritated thing it always did. But her hands, in her lap, were not cool. They were holding each other very tightly.

"For a week," said Mai. "Give it here."

Maki looked at her sister.

Mai looked back. Not irritably, for once.

"You never tell me anything," said Mai. "You lived a whole extra life. You had a son. You had grandchildren. You had a granddaughter who followed your husband round the house and got his ring taken away. And you never told me." She did not look away. "So give me this one. At least. Let me carry one thing of yours. For once."

Maki did not say anything for a long time.

Then she pushed the coin across the table. Not all the way. Halfway. To the place in front of Mai's cushion.

"Don't spend it," said Maki.

"I'm not going to *spend* it," said Mai. "I'm going to *hold* it. For a week." She picked it up. She looked at it on her palm. Small. Warm. Of no metal anyone had a name for. "It's warm."

"They're all warm," said Maki.

"I know," said Mai. She closed her hand round it. "I just didn't think yours would be."

Maki looked at her.

Mai did not look back. She put the coin in the pocket of her sleeping clothes, under the blanket, carefully. And picked up her cracker again. And ate it. Irritably.

Across the table, Frieren was looking at Maki.

Her face was very calm. But there was something in it, very quick, that Maki saw and nobody else did. Something that said *I know what you couldn't bring. And I know what you brought instead. And I know which one is heavier.*

Maki looked away.



"Frieren," said Seiko.

Frieren put her cup down.

"I'm happy," she said.

The table looked at her.

"That's what I came in with," said Frieren. She folded her hands inside the sleeves of the orange hoodie. "I was a queen. Four days ago. For two days. I made decrees." She almost smiled. "I got Rimuru's loft a door. I made Mau carry me piggyback across a dungeon. I got a hoodie." She pulled the sleeve slightly. "I made him promise something new, under a tree, and he did. And he hasn't been hurt since the winter. And I've laughed more since the first table than in the whole year before it." She paused. "I'm happy."

Nobody said anything.

"I don't know what to do with it," said Frieren. Very quietly. "I'm a thousand years old. I've been sad for a very long time. I'm very good at being sad. I know where to put it." She looked at her tea. "I don't know where to put this. It doesn't fit anywhere. It's too big for the places I keep things." She paused. "And I keep waiting for it to stop. Every morning. I wake up and I think, today's the day something comes out of the hills."

The brazier ticked.

"That's also a weight," said Seiko.

Frieren looked at her.

"Happiness," said Seiko. "When you're not used to it. It's heavy. It's the heaviest thing there is, for people like us." She closed her fan. "Old women. Elves. Hags." Turbo Granny made an offended noise. Seiko ignored her. "We spend our lives getting ready for the bad thing. And then the good thing comes and we don't know how to hold it. We hold it like it's going to break." She looked at Frieren. "It isn't. Or it is. Either way, holding it like that doesn't help."

"Then how do you hold it?" said Frieren.

Seiko considered this.

"Loosely," she said. "Like a cracker." She held one up. "You don't squeeze a cracker. It crumbles. You just hold it. And eat it while it's good." She bit it. "Then you get another one."

Frieren looked at her for a long time.

Then she took a cracker out of the tin. And held it. Loosely. In her cold small fingers. And ate it.

"I'll carry yours," said Maki. "Your happiness. For a week."

Frieren looked at her.

"Somebody should," said Maki. "You won't. You'll just wait for it to stop." She held out her hand on the table, palm up. "Give it here. I'll hold it loosely."

Frieren looked at the hand.

Then she put hers on it. Cold. Small. And this time she did not take it back straight away. She left it there for a moment. On Maki's palm. Across the low table. With the crackers between them.

"Both ways," said Frieren.

"Both ways," said Maki.



Seiko went last.

"I'm keeping my coin," she said.

They looked at her.

"I've had it since the bridge," said Seiko. "Like everyone. I'm old. Everyone I'd bring is dead in my world, or would hate it here." She turned the fan over in her hands. "I promised it on the fortieth day. To Yuta's teacher, for his doctor, when he comes. Momo keeps asking if I'm sure. I tell her I don't say things twice." She paused. "That's not the weight."

"What is, then?" said Mai.

Seiko looked at her over the fan for a long moment.

"Someone's going to need that doctor," she said. "Someone in this house. Not him. Someone here." She closed the fan. "I've been a medium for sixty years. I know when something's coming. I don't always know what." She put the fan down on the table. "So I keep it in my pocket, and I wait for the day. That's what I'm carrying." She looked round the table. "And none of you can carry it, because it's not finished yet. So I'll keep it."

Nobody argued.

On the corner of the table, on her tiny cushion, Turbo Granny cleared her throat.

"The boy kissed the girl," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Okarun," said Turbo Granny. "And Momo. On the back stair. Last week. When they thought nobody was looking." She folded her paws. "I was looking. I'm always looking. I'm in his *pocket*."

Seiko had gone very still.

"That's not a weight," said Mai.

"It is for *me*," said Turbo Granny. "Now I have to hear about it. *Constantly*. From inside his *pocket*." She glared at the tin. "Somebody carry that. I'm not carrying that. It's *disgusting*."

Seiko looked at Turbo Granny for a very long time over the top of her fan.

Then she laughed. Out loud. The first time anyone at the table had heard her laugh. A short, dry, delighted bark of a laugh, from a very small, very old woman, behind a paper fan.

"*Finally*," said Seiko.



They walked back up the corridor together after midnight, the way they always did. Mai first, to her room, with Maki's coin in her pocket and her hand over it. Then Maki and Frieren, side by side, in the dark, slowly.

At the foot of the roof ladder at the end of the east corridor, Frieren stopped.

Above them, through the open hatch, you could just see the ridge of the roof against the stars. Empty now; the roof night had broken up before the table did. But at the far end of it, small and dark against the sky, there was a shape on the shingles. A cup. Upside down. Rinsed already.

"They put a fourth cup out," said Frieren quietly. "Every seventh night. Since Kaneki came up with the coffee." She looked up at the ridge. "He comes now. Not always. Sometimes. He sits at the end. He doesn't talk much. In the morning the cup's rinsed and upside down."

Maki looked up at the small dark shape for a long moment.

"Good," she said.

They stood at the foot of the ladder in the dark.

"Thank you," said Maki. Very low. "For Mai. At the first table. What you said. *When they love them so much it frightens them*." She did not look at Frieren. "She's been different since. With me. Not much. A bit." She paused. "She looked at me just now. Across the table. When she took the coin." Her voice did something. "She's never looked at me like that."

Frieren did not say anything for a moment.

"You gave her something to carry," she said. "Something of yours. For the first time." She looked up at the empty ridge. "That's what she was waiting for. Not the truth. Not yet. Just something."

Maki was quiet.

"I still can't tell her," she said. "The other thing."

"I know."

"I don't know if I ever can."

"I know," said Frieren. "I'm still holding it." She looked at Maki in the dark. "I'll hold it as long as you need. That's what both ways means."

Maki looked at her.

Then she did something she had never done. Not in this world. Not in the life before. She put her arm round the elf's small shoulders, in the dark corridor, at the foot of the ladder, and pulled her in. Sideways. Briefly. Roughly. The way she pulled Yuta in, sometimes, when he did not expect it.

Frieren went very still.

Then, very slightly, she leaned.

"Good night, Frieren," said Maki. And let go.

"Good night," said Frieren.

Maki went on up the corridor to the room with YUTA / MAKI on the door in ink. Frieren stood at the foot of the ladder a moment longer, looking up at the ridge of the house they had built, and the small upturned cup at the end of it, against the stars.

Then she went into the east room, and lay down beside him in the bed that was hers, and put her cold foot against his leg, which was not the reset hour, which was not anything. And was not frightened. And held it loosely.

And slept.
