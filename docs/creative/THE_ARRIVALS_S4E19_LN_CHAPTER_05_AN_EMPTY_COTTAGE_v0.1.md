*When: Days 121 and 122: the evening the family left, and the day after.*

Fern wrote it at the long table that night, under the day, in her small neat hand.

*Day 121. Departures: one family (four). To the river towns, by the ford. Walked by Mikasa, Eren, Stark, Yuta, Mau. Back before dark.*

She looked at it for a long time. Then, under it:

*Bees: Daphne. Cushion: kept.*

She read the day aloud at dinner, the way she read every day. When she came to *departures*, she stopped, the way she had stopped on the hundred-and-second night at *arrivals*, and the long table waited. Then she read it. Nobody said anything. Rimuru, at the cold-hearth end, said *looked, quiet* for the night report, the way he did, and Senku's chalk squeaked on the board.

Then Momo brought out the stew, and the table ate, and talked about the gate, which had water in it this week, and about the west wing's third bent, and about who had eaten the last of the good honey, which nobody would admit to. Seika said, from the back, that someone in this house had put sugar in her tea without asking, and that she would find out who, and the whole fort went very quiet.

It was an ordinary dinner. That was what Mau noticed. An ordinary dinner, loud and warm, with the lamps lit and the stew hot and Kessoku arguing about a chord.

And four places that had never been at the long table, because the family who kept bees had always eaten in their own cottage, were empty anyway. You could feel it. Not at the table. Somewhere past it. A small cold place in the settlement, at the end of the plaza road, where a lamp had been lit every evening for two years and was not lit tonight.



In the morning, Mau went to the washing court.

He had gone most mornings since the eighteenth day. Nobody sent him any more. He went because he went. He took his shirts and Frieren's and anything else that needed doing, and knelt at the troughs with the women from the valley, and washed, slowly, badly, while they sang. Dessa's friend still sang worst of all. She still used the hand cream. The pot sat on the edge of her trough, half empty now.

This morning there was one woman fewer.

Not from the family who kept bees. They had washed at their own cottage. But a woman from the valley, one of the ones who had been born there, whose mother had washed at these same troughs. She had not come. Her place at the end of the second trough was empty, and the others had not moved along to fill it.

Mau knelt in his usual place and began on his shirts.

For a while nobody said anything. The water was cold. The singing started, ragged, the way it always started, and stopped, and started again.

Then Dessa's friend, at the next trough, without looking at him, said: "She's gone to her cousin's. Up the valley. Away from the road." She wrung out a sheet. "For the spring, she says." She looked at the empty place at the end of the second trough. "She won't come back."

Mau did not say anything.

"More will go," said Dessa's friend.

"I know."

"Not because of you." She did not look at him. She never looked at him, when she said things like that. "Because of the road. And the thing in the woods. And the blue light in your field, that the shepherds can see from the hills." She slapped the sheet down on the board. "People are frightened. When people are frightened, they go somewhere smaller."

"Will you?" said Mau.

Dessa's friend did not answer at once. She scrubbed the sheet. She was not gentle with it.

"Not yet," she said.

She wrung it out. She hung it on the line. She came back to the trough and knelt and picked up the next one.

"Dessa washed here," she said. "Thirty years. Her mother before that." She did not look at the empty place. "Somebody has to sing badly at these troughs. Or the women from over the bridge will think we've all gone soft."

She started to sing. Badly. Louder than before.

After a moment, very quietly, so that nobody could hear how bad he was, Mau joined in.



In the afternoon, he went to the last cottage.

He did not know why. He had no reason to. It was empty. Wakana had checked it the night before, and closed the shutters, and latched the door, and written on the plan of the settlement on the long table's board, beside the cottage, in pencil: *empty*. Not *abandoned*. Not *free*. Just *empty*.

The Fastest Child was sitting on the step.

She was sitting on the cold stone step of the last cottage with her knees up and her arms round them and her chin on her knees, looking at the closed door. She did not look round when Mau came. He did not say anything. He sat down on the step beside her, not too close, and looked at the door too.

The lean-to behind the cottage was empty. The three hives had gone to Daphne's barn the night before, wrapped in straw, carried by Minerva, very carefully, one at a time.

They sat there for a long time.

"She's going to come back," said the Fastest Child at last. Not a question. "She's got a pass."

"She's got a pass," said Mau.

"It doesn't expire."

"It doesn't."

The Fastest Child looked at the door. "The road's going to get worse," she said. "Everyone says. Even Fern." She tightened her arms round her knees. "So she can't come back. Not for ages. Maybe not ever."

Mau did not tell her that wasn't true. He did not know whether it was true.

Somebody came round the corner of the cottage.

It was Seika. In the ugly coat, with her hands in her pockets. She had been walking. She did that, in the afternoons, alone, round the edge of the settlement, inside the line, the way she had probably walked round the block outside her club in her own world, between soundcheck and doors. She stopped when she saw the two of them on the step.

She looked at the closed door. At the empty lean-to. At the Fastest Child, with her chin on her knees.

She did not say anything for a moment. Then she came and stood in front of the step, with her hands in her pockets, and looked at the cottage.

"People leave clubs," said Seika.

The Fastest Child looked up at her.

"Regulars," said Seika. "People who came every week, for years. Then one week they don't. They moved. They got a job somewhere else. They got married. They got tired." She looked at the door. "Bands break up. A band that played your stage fifty times. One day they ring and say *we're done*." She paused. "And the stage is still there. And it's empty. On that night."

"What do you do?" said the Fastest Child.

Seika did not answer at once.

"You keep the stage swept," she said. "In case."

The Fastest Child looked at her for a long time.

Then she unfolded herself from the step, and stood up, and went round the side of the cottage, to the lean-to, where there was an old broom propped against the wall that nobody had taken. She came back with it. She looked at the step of the last cottage, which had a little mud on it, and some dead leaves blown in from the plaza, and a scatter of straw from the hives.

She began to sweep.

Seika watched her for a moment with her face completely flat. Then she turned round and walked on, round the edge of the settlement, inside the line, with her hands in her pockets.

Mau sat on the end of the step, out of the way, and watched the Fastest Child sweep the step of an empty cottage very thoroughly, with her mouth set, until there was not a single leaf left on it.

When she was done, she stood the broom against the door, upright, where anyone coming up the step would see it.

"There," she said.



That evening, Fern's ledger said, under the day:

*Day 122. The cottage: empty. Swept.*

And then, in a different hand, crooked and enormous, in charcoal, because Umaru had got up for a glass of water:

*THE CUSHION IS STILL THERE.*
