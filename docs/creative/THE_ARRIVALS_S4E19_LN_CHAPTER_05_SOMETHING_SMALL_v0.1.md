*When: Days 118 and 119, into the morning of Day 120. The west wing was rising a bent at a time, Seika and Bocchi were already on the ninth song, and the gate was about to change.*

The merchant came up the bridge road on the hundred-and-eighteenth day, on time for once, with his hired guard and his mule and a cart that rode level. He sold Momo salt and lamp oil and three bolts of plain cloth, and bought forty pots of hand cream from Maomao at a price that made Fern's pen stop moving. Then he stood in the plaza with his hat in his hands and asked, very politely, if anyone would walk him back as far as the ford.

"The low woods," he said. "Where the bear that wasn't came out. I've been through twice since. Nothing. But my guard says he heard something, last time. In the trees. Talking."

"Talking," said Mikasa.

"He says." The merchant turned his hat round in his hands. "He's not a man who hears things. That's why I hired him."

So Mikasa and Eren walked him back.

It was not a long walk. Half a day to the ford, along the south road, through the white fields and down into the river valley and into the low woods, where the road went between bare trees for a mile before it came out at the water. Half a day back. They had walked it a dozen times in the winter, on materials runs, with the Searchers. They knew it.

They took their old blades. Not the shop's. The shop's blades were for the gate, and there were none yet. Mikasa's were still on the list, next after rations, and Fern had said *next week*, in the voice that meant she had already done the sum.

"Steel's fine out here," said Eren, at the red stake, buckling his on. "It's only in there it doesn't work."

"I know," said Mikasa.

She checked the nick in her left blade anyway, with her thumb, before they left.



The walk down was nothing.

The merchant talked, the way he always talked, about the river towns, and the price of salt, and the price of fear, which he said was higher this spring than he had ever known it. The guard, a broad quiet man with a scar through one eyebrow, did not talk at all, and watched the trees. The mule walked. The snow had gone off the south road almost everywhere now, and the mud had come instead, and the cart's wheels sucked in it.

At the ford, the merchant shook their hands, both of them, and held on to Eren's a moment longer than he needed to.

"They're saying things," he said quietly. "In the towns. About you."

"We know," said Eren.

"Worse than before." The merchant looked back up the road, toward the settlement they could not see. "Since the voice. Since the deer. Since the blue light in your field, that a shepherd saw from the hills and told everyone about." He let go of Eren's hand. "Monster town. They say it like it's a place's name now. Not an insult. A name."

"It's not," said Mikasa.

The merchant looked at her.

"I know it's not," he said. "I've eaten Momo's soup." He put his hat on. "But I'm one man." He paused. "Be careful on the way back. Through the trees. My guard heard something talking."

Then he went across the ford, with the guard and the mule and the cart, and up the far bank, and was gone.



They came back through the low woods at dusk.

It was not the plan. They had meant to be through before the light went. But the mud had been worse on the way down than either of them had thought, and they had stopped at the ford longer than they meant to, and by the time they reached the edge of the trees on the way back, the sun was low and red behind them and the woods ahead were full of long blue shadows.

They went in anyway. There was no other road.

Mikasa walked on the left, Eren on the right, a little apart, the way they had walked through forests in another world, where things came out of trees. Neither of them spoke. The road went between the bare trunks, straight, muddy, and the light came through the branches in red bars, and the woods were very quiet. No birds. No wind.

Halfway through, Mikasa heard it.

It was very faint. It came from the trees on the left, not far off the road. A voice. Low. Muttering. She could not make out the words at first. Only the sound of them, going on and on, under the silence, the way water goes on under ice.

She stopped. Eren stopped a step after her, without being told.

They listened.

The voice went on. Closer now, or louder. And she could make out words.

*...they don't belong...*

*...came over the bridge, from nowhere, from nothing...*

*...monster town...*

*...they're not like us, they don't even look like us, some of them don't even look like PEOPLE...*

*...and the one with the shadow...*

*...the one with the shadow...*

Mikasa's hands were on her blades.

The voices were not one voice. She heard that now. They were many, overlapping, a man's and a woman's and an old man's and a child's, all muttering at once, all saying the same things, in the same low, frightened, hateful mutter. Like a crowd in a market, whispering, after someone strange has walked past.

*...the one with the shadow is the worst...*

*...they say it nearly killed a whole plaza...*

*...why do they let him walk around...*

"Eren," said Mikasa, very quietly.

"I hear it."

Something moved in the trees.

It came out onto the road about twenty paces ahead of them, between two trunks, into a bar of red light, and stopped.

It was small. That was the first thing. Smaller than either of them had braced for. About the height of a child of eight or nine. It stood on two legs, roughly, though its legs did not bend the way legs bent. It was grey, the grey of old mud, and lumpy, and it was hard to look at straight, because it did not have a face.

It had a great many faces.

They covered it. Its whole body. Front and back and sides, all over, like scales, overlapping. Small faces, the size of a hand, all different. Men and women and children and old people, and all of them, every one, turned slightly away. Looking off to the side. Not meeting anyone's eyes. And all of their mouths were moving, all the time, muttering.

*...they don't belong...*

*...monster town...*

*...the one with the shadow...*

It looked at them. Or the faces did not look at them, and that was the same thing.

Then it said, in all its voices at once, quite clearly:

*Go back where you came from.*

And came at them.



It was not fast. It was not strong. Afterward, both of them would say that, separately, to the long table, and again to each other, many times, as if they could not quite believe it.

It came at Eren first. It ran, in its strange jerking way, down the muddy road, with all its faces muttering, and reached for him with an arm that was mostly mouths. Eren drew and cut in one movement, the way he had cut a thousand times, an ordinary cut with an ordinary blade, and the steel went into the thing's shoulder.

It went in.

Not like the wolves. Not skidding off. It went in, the way a blade goes into anything soft, and the thing made a sound. All its faces at once, crying out. Not in pain, exactly. In outrage. As if it could not believe someone like him would dare.

Mikasa took it from behind. Two cuts, crossing, at the place where a neck would be if it had a neck.

It came apart.

Not in blue sparks. Not in light. It simply fell apart, on the muddy road, into a heap of grey lumpy stuff that steamed faintly in the cold dusk and smelled of nothing, or of old wet paper. All the small faces in it went slack. Their mouths stopped moving, one by one, all over the heap, like candles going out down a long table.

The last one to stop was a child's face, near the top, turned away. Its mouth moved once more.

*...don't belong...*

Then it stopped too.

The woods were very quiet.

Mikasa and Eren stood over it in the red dusk with their blades out, breathing hard, though neither of them had done anything that should have made them breathe hard.

"That's it?" said Eren.

Mikasa did not answer. She was watching the heap. It was not doing anything. It was steaming, very faintly, and getting smaller. Not melting. Just getting less, the way a puddle gets less on a warm day, as if it had never really been there to begin with.

"Steel worked," said Eren.

"Steel worked," said Mikasa.

"It was *small*." He sounded almost offended. "It was the size of a kid. It went down in two cuts." He looked at his blade. There was nothing on it. No blood. No grey. Clean. "What *was* it?"

Mikasa looked at the heap. It was the size of a cat now. Then a rabbit. Then a handful. Then a smudge on the mud, steaming. Then nothing.

There was nothing left. No crystal. No bones. Nothing on the road but mud, and their own footprints, and a faint smell of old wet paper that was already going on the wind.

"It said what they say," said Mikasa slowly. "In the towns. The merchant said." She looked at the empty place on the road. "*Monster town.* *They don't belong.* *The one with the shadow.*" She stopped. "It was saying what they say about us."

Eren looked at the place too.

"So it was a— what? A rumour? With legs?"

Mikasa did not answer that either. She did not know.

They stood there a moment longer. Then Eren wiped his clean blade on his sleeve anyway, out of habit, and sheathed it, and so did she, and they walked on, out of the low woods, into the last of the light, up the long road toward the settlement, faster than they had walked down it.

Neither of them looked back. But neither of them spoke until they could see the lamps.



They told the long table that night. Exactly. All of it. The voices in the trees. The faces. *Go back where you came from.* Two cuts. Nothing left.

Anko wrote it in SIGHTINGS. She had started a third page. *Day 119. South road, low woods, dusk. Small, grey, child-sized. Covered in faces, all turned away. Spoke in many voices: what the river towns say. Steel works. Two cuts. Left nothing.* She underlined *steel works*, and then, after a moment, *left nothing*.

There was a short silence at the table. Then it went round, the way it always went round.

Senku wanted to know what it was made of, and was disappointed to hear there was nothing left to look at. Himmel said that in his world there had been things in the woods that wore stolen faces, and they had always been worse than they looked, and then said, honestly, that this did not sound like those. Maomao asked if either of them had been cut, or bitten, or breathed on, and checked Eren's shoulder anyway, where the thing's arm of mouths had nearly reached, and found nothing. Rimuru asked Ciel, and Ciel said she had not seen it, it had been outside the line and outside the relay, and she could not read a thing that was not there any more.

Then it moved on. There were other things to talk about. The west wing's third bent was going up in the morning. The gate had changed again that dawn, into its third week, into something with water in it, and nobody had gone in yet. Seika had agreed to teach Kita a chord. Momo wanted to know who had eaten the good honey.

Most of the table went with it.

Mau had listened to all of the report. He had not asked a question. At *the one with the shadow*, his fingers had stopped on the side of his bowl. They had not started again.

He waited until Mikasa finished. Then he put his spoon down and stood.

"I'm going to bed," he said.

Nothing else. He did not take his bowl to the kitchen.

Frieren looked up. For a moment she was looking at a different evening, at the backpack, at the place where he had stood to tell them he was going away for two days.

Then she got up too.

After they had left, Yuta looked at the empty place.

"He got up like that before the two days," he said, quietly.

Fern looked toward the stairs.

"He said bed," she said.

Rem took his bowl to the kitchen. There was still enough in it to keep.



Frieren caught him before the bedroom door.

He turned at her footsteps. She had meant to ask something. He saw her face before she found the words.

Mau put his arms round her.

She stood very still for a moment. Then her hand closed in the back of his shirt.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I worried you."

"Are you going somewhere?"

"No. Just bed." His cheek rested against her hair. "Sleep caught up with me. That's all."

She held him a little longer than he had expected.

He did not let go first.

Inside, he took off his boots and lay down. Frieren pulled the blanket over him. He was asleep before she had finished straightening its edge.



In the Trial, there had been a life after the last person died.

He had not died with them.

The dream did not give him the beginning. No road leading there. No face he could save by reaching sooner. Only the part after, when there was nobody left to call him home.

A street broke beneath his feet.

People ran. Someone fell. He turned toward the sound and something else broke, farther away, where he had not meant to look.

*Monster.*

The word came from a mouth he could not find.

Then from another.

He remembered trying to stop. He remembered that the place where he stopped became another place people had to flee.

There had been a title, in that life.

*Demon Lord.*

He had become one. The dream gave him that much without giving him back the people who might have known what to do with it.

It went on.

He could not tell how much time passed between one ruined place and the next. The voices changed. The words changed.

*God of Destruction.*

He had heard that too.

It had not been his name.

At the end of the fragment, something held him. Or met him. He could not remember its face, its shape, whether it had a voice. Only the pressure of it, and his own, locked against each other. Nothing advancing. Nothing ending.

He pushed.

It did not move.

He could not stop pushing.



Something cold touched his shin.

It did not belong there.

"One."

The pressure stayed. He was still in it. His hands were closed, though he could not see them.

The cold moved against his leg again.

"Mau."

He took a breath that hurt.

A ceiling. Timber. The blanket against his fingers. Frieren beside him, awake now, looking at him in the dark.

"Still here," he said.

She put her hand over one of his.

He looked at it until he could open his fingers. Then he moved the blanket aside.

"Where are you going?"

"Bathroom."

He sat on the edge of the bed first. Both feet on the floor. When he stood, he knew he was standing.



He closed the bathroom door without letting the latch sound.

His hands would not stop shaking.

He put them against the basin. They shook there too. Water ran over his fingers, and he watched it go down the drain, and waited for his knees to believe the floor.

Nobody was calling him anything.

There were no footsteps running away from him. Through the wall, the house made its small night sounds. A board settling. Water in a pipe. Someone closing a door, carefully, farther down the passage.

He knew where he was.

His hands kept shaking anyway.

He stayed until he could turn the water off without missing the tap.



Frieren was half awake when he came back. Her eyes opened at the edge of the blanket lifting.

"Better?"

"A bit."

She moved closer. When he lay down, her arm went round him from behind, and her forehead rested between his shoulders.

He put his hand over hers.

She did not ask him to explain it in the dark.

After a while, her breathing lengthened. He listened. His hands were not quite steady yet, but he could feel hers beneath one of them, warm now, and the bed holding both of them.

He slept.



Ori came to wake them for breakfast.

She knocked softly and pushed the door open a little.

"Mau?"

Neither of them answered.

Frieren was behind him, her arm still round his middle. His face had sunk into the pillow. The room was full of pale morning light, and they looked so deeply asleep that Ori stopped in the doorway.

She had meant to tell them Momo was putting the bowls out.

Instead she shut the door quietly behind her, took off her slippers and climbed in at the edge of the bed in front of Mau.

He moved without opening his eyes. An arm came round her, and she settled against him, and for a moment his hand rested on her back.

Ori closed her eyes.

Downstairs, breakfast went on without them.



Milim found them after the bowls had been cleared away.

"Ori."

No answer.

"*Ori.*"

Ori stirred. Mau opened one eye. Behind him, Frieren made a small sound into his shoulder.

Milim stood by the bed with her hands on her hips.

"You promised we'd play after breakfast."

Ori blinked at her.

"Breakfast?"

"Finished. Ages ago."

Mau lifted his head. His arm was still round Ori. Frieren's was still round him.

"All of it?" he asked.

"Yes. All of it. And I waited."

Frieren opened her eyes at last.

"Five minutes," she said.

Milim looked at the three of them.

"That's what you said yesterday."
