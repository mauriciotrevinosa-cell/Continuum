*When: Days 120 and 121, the mornings after the thing in the woods was told at the long table, while the west wing's third bent went up.*

The woman who kept bees came to the long table on the hundred-and-twentieth morning, before breakfast, with her husband behind her and her hands folded in her apron.

Fern was there, of course. Fern was always there. She looked up from the ledger and saw the woman's face, and put her pen down, and did not pick it up again for some time.

"We're going," said the woman.

She was one of the four families who had stayed when the window closed. Everyone in the house knew her a little. She had come over the bridge two summers ago with her husband and their two children, from a world with green hills and white houses and hives in every garden, and they had stayed in the refuge, and then in the cottages, and she had kept bees. She had brought three hives' worth of them over the bridge in a basket, somehow, and kept them alive through two winters in a lean-to behind the last cottage, wrapped in straw. Kusuri had used her beeswax in the hand cream. Momo used her honey, the good honey, on the high shelf.

"To the river towns," said the woman. "My sister's there. In the second town past the ford. She married a miller." She looked at the ledger, not at Fern. "She's been asking us since the autumn. We kept saying no."

Fern did not say anything.

"It's not you," said the woman. "It's not the house. It's not anyone." She looked down at her hands. "It's the road."



She explained it at the long table, with the house coming down to breakfast round her in twos and threes and stopping when they saw her face, and sitting down quietly, and listening.

It was the thing in the woods, she said. Not because it was dangerous. Mikasa and Eren had said it was small, and it went down in two cuts, and she believed them. It was what it had *said*.

"The things they say in the towns," said the woman. "Coming out of a thing in the trees. In all their voices." She was quiet for a moment. "My husband's brother said that, about us, once. In our world. *Go back where you came from.* About the people from over the mountains." She looked at her husband, who looked at the table. "And he wasn't a bad man. He was frightened. That's all. People say it when they're frightened."

The common room was very quiet.

"If they're saying it in the towns," said the woman, "and now there's something on the road saying it too—" She stopped. "Then it's only going to get worse. The road. The towns. All of it. Every month. Until there isn't a road at all." She looked up, at last, at Fern. "And my sister's on the other side of it. If we're going to go, it has to be now. While it's still a road. While two people can walk us to the ford and come home again before dark." She swallowed. "In a year, I don't think they could."

Fern looked at her for a long time.

"It's yours," said Fern quietly. "Your choice. Nobody here is going to argue with it."

"I know," said the woman. "That's why I came to tell you first. And not just go."

Fern picked up her pen.

She did not write anything. She held it, over the page, for a long moment, and then put it down again, and closed the ledger.

"When?" she said.

"Tomorrow," said the woman. "If someone will walk us."

"Someone will walk you," said Fern.



The house did not make a fuss.

Mau noticed it again, the way he had noticed it on the night Nijika held her coin. Nobody said *stay*. Nobody said *it's the worst time*. Nobody told the woman who kept bees that the road would get better, because nobody believed it would, and she would have known.

Instead, things happened.

Momo packed food. More food than four people could carry to the second town past the ford, in a basket and two sacks. Bread, and the pear bread, and dried meat, and a small jar of the good honey from the high shelf, which she put in last, on top, wrapped in a cloth, without saying whose it had been.

Wakana went down to the last cottage with his tools and looked at their cart, which was old and small and had a wheel that wobbled, and took the wheel off, and was there until dark.

Maomao brought a small bag of things. Bandages. A salve. Something for coughs. Something for fevers, with the dose written on a slip of paper in her small hard hand, and underlined twice.

Kusuri came with her and gave the woman a pot of the hand cream, the first pot, the one from the first batch, which she had kept back for herself. "It's got your wax in it," said Kusuri, not looking at her. "So it's half yours anyway."

And Daphne came for the bees.

She came in the afternoon, alone, to the lean-to behind the last cottage, where the three hives sat in their straw. She stood in front of them for a long time with her hands folded. The woman who kept bees came out and stood beside her.

"I can't take them," said the woman. "Not on the road. Not in a cart. Not into a town where I don't know where they'll go." Her voice was steady. "They'd die."

"Yes," said Daphne.

"Will you keep them?"

Daphne looked at the hives.

"I keep what grows," she said. "And what feeds. And what makes things grow." She looked at the woman. "They do all three." A pause. "I will keep them."

The woman did not say anything for a moment.

"Keep them warm," she said. "In the barn. Not too close to the door. They don't like draughts. And in the spring, when the fruit trees flower, take them out early, before the frost's quite gone. They'll know what to do." She stopped. "And talk to them. Sometimes. They like it. My grandmother said they need to be told things. When someone comes. When someone goes." She looked at the hives. "Tell them I've gone. Or they'll wonder."

Daphne looked at the three hives in their straw for a long moment.

"If you come back," she said, "they will know you."

The woman turned away, quickly, and went back into the cottage.

Daphne stood in front of the hives alone. Then, very quietly, in her low unhurried voice, she began to tell them things.



The children were the hardest.

There were two of them. A boy of about seven, very quiet, who had never once spoken to Mau in two years but had always waved, from the cottage door, when Mau went past to the washing court in the mornings. And a girl of about ten, who was best friends with the girl with the bean. The Fastest Child. They had raced on the sled slope together all winter. They had sat in the front row of the movie room together for the alien film, all five times.

The Fastest Child did not cry. She stood at the cottage door on the evening before, while the girl packed her things into a small bag, and watched, with her arms folded, exactly the way Seika stood at the back of rooms.

Umaru came at dusk, with the fort.

Not all of the fort. Umaru, and Suika, and Ori, and Coco. They came in a small serious line down the plaza road to the last cottage, in hoods, and stood in front of the door, and Umaru knocked, and when the girl came to the door, Umaru held out a card.

It was a pass. Stiff paper, cut neat, with the Ministry's seal. The cushion, the teacup, the sleeping cat. Umaru had made it at the toll booth that afternoon, and Mau, who had seen her making it, had seen her stop three times and put her face in her hands and then go on.

It said *ONE DAY PASS*, and under that, in Umaru's large crooked capitals, the girl's name, spelled wrong once and crossed out and spelled right. And under that:

*VALID: WHENEVER YOU COME BACK.*
*NO EXPIRY.*

The girl looked at it for a long time.

"In case," said Umaru. Her voice was very steady, in the way it was steady when it was being held up. "The fort keeps your cushion. Next to hers." She nodded at the Fastest Child. "Nobody else sits on it. Ministry decree."

The girl took the pass. She held it in both hands. Then she looked past Umaru, at the Fastest Child, standing at the side of the door with her arms folded.

"Keep my cushion," she said.

"*Obviously*," said the Fastest Child, and turned round very fast and walked off into the dark plaza, and did not come back until the girl had gone in.



They walked them to the ford on the hundred-and-twenty-first morning.

Mikasa and Eren. Stark, with the shop axe. Yuta. And Mau, who had asked, and been looked at by Fern, and been told *yes*, which surprised him.

The family went in the middle, with the cart Wakana had mended, which rode level now, pulled by the husband, with the basket and the sacks and the children's two small bags on it. The woman walked beside it. The boy rode on top, on the sacks. The girl walked, holding the pass inside her coat.

Half the house walked with them as far as the end of the plaza road, where the line was. Then they stopped there, because that was where the line was, and watched the cart go on down the road, between the white fields, toward the river valley.

The woman turned round once, at the top of the slope down. She looked back at the settlement. At the plaza. At the real house, with its long bark roof and its wide eaves and its two vermilion columns at the gate. At the ink tree. At the forty people standing at the line.

She lifted one hand.

Forty people lifted theirs.

Then she turned and went on down the slope, and the settlement went out of sight behind the hill.



The walk was nothing. The low woods were nothing. Nothing talked in the trees. Nothing came out onto the road. They went through between the bare trunks in daylight, quickly, with Mikasa on the left and Eren on the right and Stark in front with the axe, and came out at the ford before noon.

At the water, the woman stopped.

She turned round to all of them. To Mikasa and Eren. To Stark, with his axe. To Yuta. And last, to Mau, at the back.

She looked at him for a long time.

"It's not you," she said.

"I know," said Mau. "It's the road."

"No," said the woman. "I mean it's not you." She looked at him steadily. "In the towns. When they say *the one with the shadow*. When that thing in the woods said it." She paused. "I've lived next door to you for two years. You walk past my cottage every morning to the washing court. You carried water for my husband when his back went, in the first winter. You wave at my boy." She looked at the boy, on top of the sacks, who was looking at Mau, and lifted one small hand, and waved. "That's who you are. Not what they say."

Mau did not say anything. He did not trust his voice.

"I'm going because of the road," said the woman. "Because of what's coming. Not because of you." She put her hand on the cart. "I want you to know that. Before I go."

"Thank you," said Mau, at last.

She nodded once.

Then she turned, and her husband lifted the cart's shafts, and they went down into the ford, the cart rocking, the water to the axles, the boy on top of the sacks holding on with both hands. And up the far bank. And onto the road on the other side, which went away between the trees toward the river towns, toward a second town past the ford, and a sister who had married a miller.

At the top of the far bank, the girl turned round.

She took something out of her coat. A card, stiff, white. She held it up, high, over her head, in both hands, so they could see it from across the water.

Then she put it back inside her coat, carefully, and ran after the cart.

They stood at the ford until the road on the far side was empty. Then Mikasa said, "We should go," and nobody argued, and they walked back through the low woods in silence, in daylight, quickly, and nothing talked in the trees.
