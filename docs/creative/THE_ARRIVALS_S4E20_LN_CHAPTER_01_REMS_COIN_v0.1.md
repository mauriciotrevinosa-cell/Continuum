*When: Day 123, the day after the empty cottage was swept. The west wing's rooms were going up a bent at a time, and the gate was in its third week, with water in it.*

Rem told Fern first, because that was the rule, and because Nijika had.

She came to the long table on the hundred-and-twenty-third morning, very early, in the grey light, with a teapot. She always came with a teapot when there were things to say. She poured Fern a cup without being asked, and set it down beside the ledger, and turned the handle toward Fern's right hand, and then stood there, holding the pot, not sitting down.

Fern looked at her for a moment. Then she put down her pen.

"The room is finished," said Rem.

She did not say which room. She did not need to. Wakana had finished it the evening before: the second room in the west wing, beside the turn of the north corridor, where Rimuru had drawn it on the floor of the common room on the queen's second day. Small. A bed. A window looking west, toward the fields and the hills where the sun went down. A door that opened onto the corridor two steps from Rem's own door at the turn. Wakana had hung the door at dusk and stood back and looked at it for a long time, and then gone and found Rem in the kitchen and told her, and gone away again before she could say anything.

"The room is finished," said Rem again. "So I'm ready."

Fern looked at her.

"The List says *when we're ready*," said Fern carefully. "Not when you are."

"I know," said Rem.

"The road's worse. The family went south on Tuesday. There's something in the woods that talks."

"I know."

"And she'd come here. To this. To *monster town*, as they call it now." Fern did not say it unkindly. She was only laying it on the table, the way she laid everything on the table. "To a world where the road closes. Where things in the trees say *go back where you came from*."

"I know," said Rem. She looked down at the teapot. "She'll hate it." A pause. "She'll hate the road, and the cold, and the fort, and the food, and everyone in this house." Another pause. "She'll say all of it out loud. At dinner. On the first night."

"Rem—"

"She's been alone for a long time," said Rem. "Where she is. Without me." Her voice did not change. Her hands on the teapot did. "I don't know how long. It's been months for me. It might be a day for her. It might be a year." She looked up at Fern. "But whatever it's been, it's been too long. And there's a room now. With a window. Next to mine."

Fern looked at her for a very long time.

Then she picked up her pen, and turned the ledger to the page of the List, and beside the second line, *2. Rem: her sister. When we're ready.*, in the margin, very small, she wrote the date.

"Tonight?" said Fern.

"Tonight," said Rem.

She poured Fern a second cup, though the first was still full, and went back to the kitchen.



The house knew by lunch. It always did.

It did not make a fuss. It had learned how not to, on the night of Nijika's coin, and it did the same things again, quietly, because they had worked. Momo made the pear bread, the second loaf, and put it in the pantry under a cloth: *FOR TOMORROW (OR WHENEVER)*. Nobody touched it. Umaru made a slate at the long table, in coloured chalk: *APPLICANT: RAM (REM'S SISTER). STATUS: PENDING ARRIVAL. CUSHION: RESERVED (NEXT #2).* And under *notes*, after a long pause, very small: *rem says she will hate everything. let her. (again.)*

Seika saw the slate on the long table at lunch and read it, with her arms folded.

"Another one," she said.

"Another older sister," said Umaru.

Seika considered this.

"Good," she said. "I was outnumbered."

And Frieren, at dinner, leaned over to Mau.

"She's frightened," said Frieren.

"Rem?"

"Not of the road." Frieren watched Rem at the far end of the long table, by the teapot, pouring for everyone, smiling her small careful smile, exactly herself. "Of the coin."



Mau understood what Frieren meant at ten o'clock that night, when Rem knocked on the door of the east room.

She did not knock after dark. Nobody did. It was Fern's rule, from the Muslin, and it held in the real house: nobody knocks on Mau and Frieren's door after dark unless something is on fire or someone is dying. Rem had never once broken it.

She knocked very softly. Three times.

Frieren opened the door. Rem stood in the corridor in her maid's dress, with her hands folded in front of her apron, and her face very pale.

"I'm sorry," said Rem. "I know the rule. Nothing is on fire." She swallowed. "I need to ask something. Before I do it. And Ori said—" She stopped. "Ori said Mau would understand."

Frieren looked at her for a moment. Then she stood aside.

Rem came in. She did not sit down. She stood in the middle of the east room, by the shelf, which was in the middle of the floor again, and looked at Mau, sitting on the edge of the bed.

"The coin," said Rem. "Rimuru said. After Nijika." She was speaking very carefully, as if each word were a cup she might drop. "It only carries if they love you back. If they don't, it goes cold. And dark. And stays in your hand." She looked down at her own folded hands. "That's what Ciel said. That's the rule."

"That's the rule," said Mau.

Rem was quiet for a long time.

"My sister lost her horn," she said, "when we were children. Saving me."

Mau did not say anything. Neither did Frieren.

"Our village was attacked. In the night. Everyone was dying. And my sister went out into it. And she was so strong, then. Stronger than anyone. Stronger than me. She was the one they talked about. The one born with everything." Rem's voice did not shake. Her hands, folded on her apron, were white at the knuckles. "And she lost her horn. Because of me. Because she was protecting me. And after that, she wasn't the strongest any more. She was tired all the time. She couldn't do the things she could do before. She couldn't even—" Rem stopped. "Everything she was going to be. Everything they said she would be. It went with the horn. Because of me."

The east room was very quiet.

"She never once said it was my fault," said Rem. "Not once. In all the years." She looked up. "But I know what I took from her."

"Rem," said Frieren gently.

"So I'm frightened," said Rem, "that I'll hold the coin. And think of her. And it'll go cold." She was still not crying. "And then I'll know."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Mau got up off the bed, and came across the east room, and stood in front of Rem, by the shelf.

"In the Trial," he said, "four hundred and thirty-seven times, I thought I knew what people thought of me. I was wrong every time. Every single time." He looked at her. "We're bad at that. Knowing what the people who love us think of us. We're the worst people to ask."

Rem looked at him.

"You said she was rude," said Mau. "And lazy. And she thinks she's better than everyone. And she's usually right." He paused. "And she's the best person you know."

Rem's face moved.

"The best person you know," said Mau, "lost her horn going out into the dark for you. And never once said it was your fault. Not in all the years." He did not look away. "Rem. That's not someone who's going to make a coin go cold."

Rem stood very still by the shelf.

Then she bowed. Very low. The way she bowed to Rimuru, and to Diablo, and to nobody else in the house.

"Thank you," she said, to the floor.

And went out, and closed the door, very softly, behind her.



She did it in her own room, at the turn, at midnight.

Nobody was with her. She had asked for that. Ori had offered, and Umaru, and the whole fort, and she had said no to all of them, gently, one at a time. She wanted to do it in her own room. The narrow room at the turn of the north corridor, which Mau had left unmarked in the Muslin for a whole season, with a note pinned inside the door. The room she had written her own name on, in pencil, one afternoon, and never let anyone go over in ink.

So nobody saw it. Nobody but Rem.

She told Ori in the morning, and Ori told nobody, and it was only much later that Mau heard any of it, from Ori, on the roof, in a few words.

Rem sat on the edge of her bed in the dark with the coin in her hand.

She closed her hand round it. She closed her eyes.

She thought of her sister.

Not the village. Not the night of the fire. She had thought she would think of that, and she did not. She thought instead of a kitchen in a mansion, very early in the morning, and her sister sitting at the table doing nothing at all, with a cup of tea, while Rem did everything, the way it always was. Her sister saying *Rem, the tea's cold*, without looking up. Her sister saying *Rem, you missed a spot*, about a floor Rem had scrubbed twice. Her sister's hand on her head, once, very briefly, on a night when Rem had been crying and thought nobody knew, saying nothing at all.

Pink hair. A cold voice. A small, sharp, rare smile.

*Nee-sama.*

The voice came. It came to the narrow room at the turn, and to nowhere else.

*You are sure? Ram.*

Rem sat on the edge of her bed in the dark.

"Yes," she said.

Her voice did not shake.

"Yes. I'm sure."

The coin went warm in her hand. Then warmer.

Then, all at once, it was gone.

Rem opened her eyes. She opened her hand.

Her palm was empty.

She looked at it for a long time. At the empty palm, pink where the coin had been. Not cold. Not dark. Not a small dull stone sitting in her hand to tell her what she had always been afraid of.

Gone.

It had carried.

Ori, who had been sitting on the floor of the corridor outside Rem's door in the dark the whole time, without telling anyone, without telling Rem, heard a sound through the door then. She told Mau about it later, on the roof. She said she had never heard Rem make a sound like it. She said it was not crying, exactly. It was the sound of someone who had been holding something very heavy, for a very long time, since she was a child, and had just been told that she could put it down.

Ori did not knock. She sat in the dark corridor with her back against the wall beside Rem's door, with her knees up, until the sound stopped.

Then she went and got Umaru. And Umaru got the fort. And by one in the morning, the fort, in hoods, with blankets, was sitting on the step of Arrival House, in the cold, in a row, waiting.

Rem came at two. She came across the plaza in her maid's dress with a shawl over it and a teapot, and found them there, and stopped.

"I didn't—" said Rem.

"We know," said Umaru. She moved over on the step. "Sit."

Rem sat.

She poured them all tea, from the pot, into the cups she had brought, because she had brought enough cups, because some part of her had known. Then she sat on the step of Arrival House between Umaru and Ori, with the empty pot in her lap, and looked at the dark door behind her, and the dark plaza in front.

"She'll hate this," said Rem, very quietly. "A crowd."

"Let her," said Umaru.

Rem laughed. It was small, and wet, and it went on for a long time.
