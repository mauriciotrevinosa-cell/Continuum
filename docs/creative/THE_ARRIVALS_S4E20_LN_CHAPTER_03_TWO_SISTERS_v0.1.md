*When: Day 125, the next morning. Outside the door, the house went on: the fourth bent, the gate, the washing court.*

Ram woke a little after dawn on the hundred-and-twenty-fifth day, and the first thing she said was: "You're on the floor."

Rem had not slept. She had sat on the floor of the room with the west window all night, with her back against the wall beside the bed and her sister's hand in hers. Some time after midnight the hand had stopped being limp and had closed, once, round her fingers, in sleep, and Rem had not moved after that for fear of waking her.

Now the grey light was coming in through the west window, which faced the wrong way for dawn, so that the room filled up slowly with a pale reflected light from the yard. And Ram's eyes were open. Still red at the whites. Still shadowed underneath. But looking at her.

"You're on the floor," said Ram again. "Why are you on the floor. There's a chair." Her voice was still dry and cracked. But it was stronger than it had been in the second witness room. "There's a chair right there. In the corner. I can see it."

"I wanted to be close," said Rem.

"The chair is close."

"Closer."

Ram looked at her for a long moment, with her red eyes.

"You're an idiot," said Ram.

"Yes," said Rem.

Ram's hand, in hers, did not let go.



For a while neither of them said anything. Ram lay in the bed and looked at the ceiling, which was clay, washed white, with a beam across it, pegged, no nails. She looked at it with an expression of deep and growing disapproval, as if it had personally failed an inspection.

"Where is this," she said at last.

"Home," said Rem.

Ram turned her head on the pillow and looked at her.

"It's a house," said Rem. "A real one. We built it. Everyone. By hand, mostly. There was a man who made four thousand pegs." She stopped. She did not know how to say all of it. "There's a bath. There's a movie room. There's a fort. You have a cushion in it. Number two." She swallowed. "It's home, Nee-sama. It's where I live. I live here." A pause. "So do you, now. If you want."

Ram looked at her for a long time.

"Crude," she said.

Rem began to laugh. She could not help it. She sat on the floor of the room with the west window, holding her sister's hand, and laughed, and it turned into something else halfway through, and she put her forehead down on the edge of the bed beside Ram's hand and did not lift it for a while.

Ram did not say anything. She lay and looked at the clay ceiling. Her free hand came across, slowly, and rested on the back of Rem's head. Like a lid.

"You were in the middle of a sentence," said Ram quietly, to the ceiling.



She told it sitting up, later, with three pillows behind her that Rem had arranged and rearranged four times, and a tray across her knees that Momo had sent up, which had porridge on it, and bread, and a small pot of Momo's plum jam.

"It was the kitchen," said Ram. "In the mansion. Early. Before anyone else was up." She was looking at the tray, not at Rem. "You were peeling potatoes. For breakfast. I was sitting at the table doing nothing, which is what I do, because you do everything, which is what you do."

"Yes," said Rem.

"You were talking," said Ram. "About Barusu. You were saying he'd folded the towels wrong again. Into triangles. You were saying, *Nee-sama, he folds them into triangles, and then he says it's a technique from his country, and I don't know whether to believe him*." Ram picked up the spoon. "And then the knife was on the floor."

Rem did not move.

"The knife was on the floor," said Ram, "and the potato was on the floor. Half peeled. Rolling. And you weren't." She put the spoon down again. "You weren't anywhere. You were standing at the counter, talking, and then you weren't. No sound. No light. Nothing. The knife fell, and I looked up, and the kitchen was empty, and the potato was still rolling."

The room was very quiet.

"I said your name," said Ram. "I thought you'd gone into the pantry. I said it again. Then I went and looked in the pantry." She looked at the porridge. "Then I said it a lot of times."

"Nee-sama—"

"Barusu came down," said Ram, over her. "Because of the noise. I don't make noise. He'd never heard me make noise. He came down in his ridiculous clothes and saw the knife and the potato, and saw my face, and he understood before I said anything." She paused. "He's an idiot. But he's fast. About some things."

Rem's hand, in hers, had gone very tight.

"He went into the forest," said Ram. "That morning. Shouting your name. Running. He didn't take anything. Not a coat. Not food. He ran into the forest shouting *Rem* until he couldn't shout any more, and then he kept going, whispering it." She looked at the window. "He came back at dawn the next day. He'd fallen in the stream. Twice. He was soaked and shaking and he couldn't talk. He drank some water and went out again." She was quiet for a moment. "He did that every day. Six days. He didn't sleep in a bed once. He slept in the forest, under trees, when he fell down."

Rem had her other hand over her mouth.

"Emilia-sama searched with the spirit," said Ram. "With Puck. Every field. Every road. The village. The villagers came out to help, all of them, even the old ones. Even the children." She paused. "On the second night, Puck told Emilia-sama he couldn't feel you anywhere. Not far away. Not hidden. *Anywhere.* Like you'd never been." She looked down. "Emilia-sama cried. In front of everyone. In the yard. She didn't care who saw."

"Beatrice-sama?" said Rem, behind her hand.

"Opened every door in the mansion," said Ram. "Every single one. With her door. Twice. In case you'd gone into one of hers by accident." A pause. "She didn't say anything. The whole six days. She just opened doors. And closed them. And opened the next one."

Rem was crying now, without making any sound.

"And you," said Rem. "Nee-sama. What did you—"

Ram did not answer at once.

She looked at the tray. At the porridge, which was going cold. At the small pot of jam.

"I looked," said Ram.

She said it very simply. As if it were nothing.

"I looked through everything," said Ram. "Every bird. Every rabbit. Every deer in the forest. Every dog in the village. Every horse on every road. Every eye I could reach, I looked through, for you." She touched, very lightly, the corner of one red eye with her fingertip. "For six days. Without stopping."

Rem stared at her.

"Nee-sama," she said. "Your mana. You don't have the mana for that. Not for six days. Not for six *hours*. Without the horn, you— it would have—"

"I know," said Ram.

"It would have *killed* you—"

"I know," said Ram. She picked up the spoon again. "I was very tired." She ate a spoonful of porridge, cold, and made a face. "This is barely edible."

"*Nee-sama!*"

"It is. Tell the cook." She ate another spoonful. "On the sixth night," said Ram, "I was sitting at the table. In the kitchen. Where you'd been. I couldn't look any more. My eyes wouldn't open properly. I was sitting there with my head on the table, and Barusu was asleep on the floor by the door, because he'd come in and fallen down, and the potato was still on the counter, because nobody had moved it. Because I wouldn't let anyone move it."

She stopped.

"And a voice said *Rem*," said Ram.

The room was very quiet.

"Just that," said Ram. "Your name. Not loud. Not anything. Out of nowhere. Like someone calling you from the next room." She looked at the porridge. "That's all."

"And you—"

"I was already standing up," said Ram.

Rem looked at her.

"I didn't know what it was," said Ram. "I didn't care. It said your name." She put the spoon down. "I stood up. And then I was on a floor, somewhere, in the dark, and then there was a door opening, and then you were shouting." She looked at Rem, at last. "You were very loud."

Rem did not say anything. She could not.

"So," said Ram. "That's what happened. Six days." She looked at the porridge again. "How long was it for you?"

"Months," said Rem. Her voice was almost nothing. "A long time. Since I came. A very long time."

Ram was quiet for a moment.

"Then I'm late," she said. "Sorry."



It was some time after that, when the porridge was finished, all of it, though Ram had said four more times that it was barely edible, that Rem started to say it.

"Nee-sama. The horn. If I hadn't—"

"Don't," said Ram.

"But you've been— your whole life, because of me, you've had to—"

"*Don't*," said Ram.

She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. She had the voice that made people stand up straighter, and she used it now, quietly, in the room with the west window, and Rem stopped.

"I've been awake one hour," said Ram, "and you're going to apologize for the horn." She looked at Rem with her red eyes. "No."

"But—"

"It was mine," said Ram. "The horn. Mine. Not yours. Mine to keep or spend." She looked at the window, at the pale light coming in from the yard. "I spent it on what I wanted." A pause. "I'd spend it again. Every time. The same way."

Rem stared at her.

"Now," said Ram. "Stop crying. You're getting the blanket wet. And tell me who that elf is." She settled back against her three pillows and closed her eyes. "The one with the ancient mana. Who put her hand on my head without asking."

"Frieren," said Rem, through her tears. "She's— she's Mau's. She's the one who—"

"Who is Mau."

Rem opened her mouth. Then she closed it. Then, slowly, she began to smile.

"That," said Rem, "is going to take a very long time to explain."

Ram did not open her eyes.

"I have time," she said. "Apparently." And then, a moment later, much more quietly, almost asleep again: "You're smiling. You didn't smile like that. Before."

"No," said Rem.

"Good," said Ram, and went to sleep.



Rem sat on the floor beside the bed for a long time after that, holding her sister's hand, in the pale light from the west window.

Then she got up, very quietly, and went down to the kitchen, and found Momo, and told her, very seriously, that her sister had said the porridge was barely edible, and had eaten all of it.

Momo looked at her for a long moment.

Then she went to the pantry and took down the pear bread, the second loaf, *FOR TOMORROW (OR WHENEVER)*, and cut it, and put the best slice on a plate, and gave it to Rem.

"Tell her," said Momo, "that this one's barely edible too."
