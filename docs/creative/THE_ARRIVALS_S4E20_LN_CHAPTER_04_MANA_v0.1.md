*When: Days 126 to 128: Ram's first dinners, and Frieren in the west wing twice a day. Meanwhile the west wing's fourth bent went up and the gate changed into its fourth week.*

Ram came down to dinner on the hundred-and-twenty-sixth night, on her own feet, in her own maid's dress, with Rem at her elbow, and said everything Rem had said she would.

She said it in order.

She stood in the arch of the common room and looked at the long table, and the forty people round it, and the high seat, and the lamps, and the plan on the wall. Then she said, in her dry cool voice that carried without being raised: "Crude."

The long table went quiet.

She walked to the place Rem had kept for her, beside Rem's own, at the far end, by the teapot, and sat down, and looked at the bowl of stew Momo had put there. She tasted it.

"Barely edible," said Ram.

Momo, at the kitchen door, put both hands over her mouth and turned round and went back into the kitchen. From the kitchen, very faintly, came a sound that might have been crying and might have been laughing and was probably both.

Ram ate the whole bowl. Then she looked across the common room at the fort, in its bay, half again as large as it had been drawn, with its hamster flag and its toll booth and its thirty citizens in hoods all looking back at her from their cushions, in silence. Umaru, at the arch of the fort, in her sash, was holding a slate. On it, in coloured chalk: *CUSHION #2: RESERVED.*

Ram looked at the fort for a long time.

"A disgrace," she said.

Umaru, at the arch, turned to the fort with her face shining.

"*Let her,*" Umaru whispered.

The fort, very quietly, cheered.

And then Ram looked round the long table, slowly, at all of them. At Himmel, who had stood up to bow and was still standing. At Milim, who was vibrating gently. At Senku, who had a pencil behind each ear. At Okarun, whose glasses were steaming up. At Seiko on her high seat. At Rimuru, at the cold-hearth end, in his human shape, who was smiling at her very kindly. At Mau, beside Frieren.

"Everyone in this house," said Ram, "is an idiot."

She picked up her cup. Rem poured tea into it.

"Except Rem," said Ram.

The long table sat in total silence for a moment.

Then, at the back, by the fort, in the ugly coat, with her arms folded, Seika said: "Hah."

Ram turned her head and looked at her.

They looked at each other, down the length of the common room, for a long time. The tall woman by the fort, in the ugly brown coat, with her arms folded and her face completely flat. The small pink-haired girl at the end of the table, with her cup, and her red-rimmed eyes, and her face completely flat.

"Who," said Ram, "is that."

"Seika," said Rem quietly. "She's— Nijika's older sister. She came last week. By coin. The first." She paused. "She said she liked you. Before you came. When I told her what you'd say."

Ram looked at Seika a moment longer.

"Acceptable," said Ram.

Seika unfolded her arms. She came down the common room, past the long table, to the far end, and stood beside Ram's chair, and looked down at her.

"Another older sister," said Seika.

"Yes," said Ram.

"Younger one does everything."

"Everything," said Ram.

"Never says thank you."

"Never," said Ram.

Seika looked at her for a long moment more. Then she pulled out the empty chair on Ram's other side, sat down in it, held out her own cup to Rem without looking at her, and said, "Tea."

Rem poured it.

At the other end of the long table, Nijika had put her face in her hands. Beside her, Kita was holding onto her arm. Bocchi had gone under the table.

"Oh no," said Nijika, into her hands. "Oh no. There's *two* of them."



Frieren filled her every day.

Once in the morning, after breakfast, in the room with the west window. Once at dusk. Ram sat on the edge of her bed with her eyes closed and Frieren stood in front of her with one hand flat on her forehead, under the short pink fringe, where the horn should have been, and something went from one to the other, steady and slow, for as long as it took. Usually about a quarter of an hour.

Ram did not thank her for it. Ram did not thank anyone for anything. But on the second morning, with her eyes still closed, she said:

"Your mana is very old."

"Yes," said Frieren.

"It's like drinking from a well." A pause. "A deep one. In a cold place. Where nobody's lowered a bucket in a long time."

Frieren considered this.

"It is a well," she said.

Ram opened one eye and looked at her. Then closed it again.

Ram did not say anything else, and Frieren did not ask.



Mau walked in on the third day.

He did not mean to. He had been sent. Momo had made tea and put two cups on a tray and given it to him at the bottom of the main stair and said *take this to Frieren, she's in the west wing, she's been up there since breakfast, she'll want it*. So he took it, along the north corridor, past Rem's door at the turn, to the room with the west window. The door was open a hand's width. He knocked on the frame with his elbow and went in.

Ram was sitting on the edge of her bed. Frieren was standing in front of her with one hand on her forehead. Neither of them moved when he came in. Ram's eyes were closed.

"Tea," said Mau, and stopped. "Sorry. I'll—"

"Sit," said Ram, without opening her eyes.

He looked at Frieren. Frieren did not look at him; she was holding the thing she held, very precisely, with her breathing slow. But the corner of her mouth moved.

Mau put the tray down on the windowsill and sat on the floor by the wall, where Rem had sat for two nights, and waited.

"You're Mau," said Ram.

"Yes."

"Rem talks about you." Her eyes were still closed. "A great deal. At night. When she thinks I'm asleep."

Mau did not know what to say to that.

"She says you're an idiot," said Ram.

"That sounds right."

"She says it the way she used to say it about Barusu," said Ram. "Which means she likes you." A pause. "Not that way. The other way. The way you like someone who's an idiot in a direction you approve of."

Frieren's hand did not move on Ram's forehead. But Mau saw her shoulders shake, very slightly, once.

"Who's Barusu?" said Mau.

Ram was quiet for a moment.

"An idiot," she said. "In my world. Who ran into a forest for six days shouting her name and fell in a stream twice." A pause. "He'd like you. He'd hate that he liked you. He'd follow you round and say stupid things and you'd let him." Another pause. "He's very loud."

"Like Milim," said Mau.

Ram opened one eye and looked at him.

"Which one is Milim."

"The one who was vibrating at dinner."

Ram closed the eye again.

"Louder than that," she said. "But less pink."

Mau laughed. It came out before he could stop it, the way it always did now, since the roof. Ram opened both eyes and looked at him from under Frieren's hand.

"You laugh like an idiot," said Ram.

"I've been told."

"Good," said Ram. She closed her eyes again. "Rem likes idiots. She always has."



For a while nobody said anything. The west window was full of afternoon. Frieren's breathing was slow and deep. Ram's was slow and deep. Mau sat on the floor by the wall and listened to the two of them breathing, and to the house beyond the door, the west wing's fourth bent going up somewhere outside with Wakana shouting *steady*, the fort arguing about something, a guitar.

"She smiles," said Ram.

Mau looked up.

"Rem," said Ram. "Here. She smiles all the time. Pouring tea. Folding things. Walking down a corridor." Her eyes were closed. "She smiled before. In my world. Sometimes. A small one. Careful. Like a person carrying a very full cup across a room." She paused. "This isn't that one."

Mau did not say anything.

"She told me," said Ram, "that when she came here, you didn't ask her to stay." A pause. "You didn't ask her anything. You just left a room. At the turn of a corridor. Empty. For a whole season." Another pause. "And waited to see if she'd want it."

Mau looked at the floor.

"In my world," said Ram, "nobody ever left her a room. People gave her things to do. Floors. Dishes. Orders. Barusu came closest. But even he gave her things to hold." She was quiet for a moment. "Nobody ever left her a room and waited."

"It wasn't much," said Mau.

"No," Ram agreed. "That's why."

Frieren took her hand away from Ram's forehead.

Ram opened her eyes. They were clearer than yesterday. The red at the whites was nearly gone. She looked at Frieren, and then at Mau, on the floor, and then at the tray on the windowsill, with two cups on it.

"There are two cups," said Ram.

"Momo sent one for Frieren," said Mau. "And—" He looked at the tray. "I don't know who the other one's for."

Ram looked at the tray a moment longer.

"Give it here," she said.

He got up and gave it to her. She held it in both hands and looked at it.

"Barely drinkable," she said, without tasting it.

"You haven't—"

"I can tell from the colour," said Ram, and drank it, all of it, and held out the cup for more, which there was not, and looked at him with an expression of deep personal grievance until he went back down to the kitchen and got some.



That evening, at dinner, Ram said the stew was barely edible, and the house was crude, and the fort was a disgrace, and everyone was an idiot except Rem, all over again, in the same order, exactly as she had the night before.

But when she said *everyone in this house is an idiot*, she was looking at Mau. And when she said *except Rem*, she paused, very slightly, before *Rem*. As if there had been, for a moment, another name she had considered adding, and had decided against.

Rem, pouring her tea, saw it. She did not say anything. But her smile, Mau thought, watching from the other end of the table, was not the careful one.

It had not been the careful one for three days.
