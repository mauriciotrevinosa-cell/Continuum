## Chapter 21 — Once a Week

*When: Days 128 and 129. Frieren had been filling Ram twice a day for four days. The west wing's last beam was waiting.*

It was Maomao who said it out loud, at the long table, on the hundred-and-twenty-eighth morning, because Maomao always said the thing nobody wanted to.

"It's not sustainable."

Frieren, at Mau's side, was eating her porridge very slowly. She had been in the west wing at dawn, and would be again at dusk. She had been in the west wing at dawn and at dusk for four days. She had not been to the red stake since Ram came. She had not opened her grimoire from the mimic since the queen's second night.

"Twice a day," said Maomao. "Every day. A quarter of an hour each time, and she comes back looking like she's carried water from the river." She looked at Frieren. "You've got more than anyone in this house. But it's not bottomless. And she can't go anywhere. Not the gate. Not more than a day's walk. If something happens to Frieren, or Frieren has to leave—" She stopped. "Ram has two days. Maybe three."

The table was quiet.

At the far end, Ram, who was eating her porridge with an expression of profound distaste, did not look up.

"Then I'll stay," said Frieren.

"That's not an answer," said Maomao. "That's a cage. For both of you."

Ram put her spoon down.

"Agreed," she said.



Rimuru asked Ciel. Of course he did.

*The root is still there,* said Ciel, after a long time. Rimuru said it aloud for the table, the way he did. *Under the skin. Where the horn grew from. It was cut, not torn out. A long time ago. It scarred over.* A pause. *The scar is what stops it. The root could still drink, a little, from the air. If the scar were not in the way.*

"Can you heal a scar like that?" said Fern. "Maomao?"

"Not one that old," said Maomao. "Not one that deep. I can stop bleeding. I can set bones. I can't make old flesh new." She looked down the table. "Not with herbs."

There was a pause.

Then everyone, very slowly, turned and looked at Yuta.

Yuta was halfway through a piece of bread. He stopped.

"Reverse cursed technique," said Maki, beside him. Not a question.

"I can heal," said Yuta slowly. He put the bread down. "Other people. Not just myself. Outward. It's hard. It takes a lot. But I can." He looked at the far end of the table, at the small pink-haired girl in the maid's dress, who was looking back at him with her red-rimmed eyes and an expression of cool assessment. "I've never tried it on something that old. A scar that's been there since someone was a child." He paused. "And I've never tried it on someone who isn't— who doesn't have cursed energy. Whose body works on something else."

*I will watch,* said Ciel. *If it starts to go wrong, I will tell him to stop.*

"Mau can do it too," said Umaru, from the fort. "Can't he? He's got the healing thing. Ciel said."

Mau shook his head.

"Mine's a light version," he said. "Ciel says. It closes scratches. Small cuts." He looked at his own hands. "This isn't a scratch."

*His is the beginning of the thing,* said Ciel. *Yuta's is the finished form. This needs the finished form.*

Yuta looked at Ram.

"If you want," he said. "I'll try."

Ram looked at him for a long moment.

"You're the one with the ring," she said.

Yuta's hand went, without thinking, to the ring on his finger.

"Yes," he said.

"It smells," said Ram, "of something that was very angry once. And isn't any more." She tilted her head. "Is that you?"

Yuta did not answer for a moment.

"It was someone I loved," he said. "A long time ago."

Ram looked at him a moment longer. Then she picked up her spoon again.

"Try," she said. "After breakfast. This porridge is barely edible and I intend to finish it."



They did it in the room with the west window. Ram on the edge of the bed. Yuta kneeling in front of her. Rem standing at Ram's shoulder with both hands clasped so tightly the knuckles were white. Frieren in the corner, watching, with her hands in her sleeves. Mau in the doorway. Rimuru beside him, with Ciel.

Yuta put his hand on Ram's forehead, under the short pink fringe, where Frieren put hers.

He closed his eyes.

Mau felt it. Not the way he felt Frieren's mana, like heat with your eyes shut. This was different. Yuta's cursed energy was always there, around him, a faint dark pressure, like weather. But this was the other thing. The reverse. Something turned inside out, the dark pressure folded over on itself until it was not dark at all but something warm and bright and strange, flowing out of Yuta's hand into the place where the horn had been.

Ram's eyes closed.

Her face did not change. But her hands, on the edge of the bed, closed on the blanket.

*Slowly,* said Ciel. *Slower. The root is drinking. It has not drunk in a very long time. It does not know how. Let it learn.*

Yuta slowed.

It went on for a long time. Longer than Frieren's quarter of an hour. Sweat came out on Yuta's forehead. His breathing went short. Maki, behind Mau in the corridor, said his name once, very quietly, and he did not stop.

*The scar is softening,* said Ciel. *At the edges. It is not going. It will not go. It is too old. But it is softening. Opening, a little, at the edges. The root is touching the air.* A pause. *There. That is—*

She stopped.

*That is enough,* said Ciel. *Stop now, Yuta. Stop.*

Yuta took his hand away.

He sat back on his heels on the floor, grey, breathing hard. Maki was there before anyone saw her move, kneeling behind him with her hands on his shoulders.

Ram opened her eyes.

She sat very still on the edge of the bed. Then she lifted one hand, slowly, and touched her own forehead, under the fringe, where Yuta's hand had been.

"It's warm," she said.

*It is drinking,* said Ciel. *A little. From the air. On its own.* A pause. *Not enough to fill her. It will never be enough to fill her. The scar is still there. But enough that she leaks slower. Enough that what is poured in stays longer.* Another pause. *Once a week, I think. Not twice a day. Once a week, from Frieren, should be enough.*

Nobody said anything.

Rem made a sound and put both hands over her mouth.

"I can try again," said Yuta, from the floor, still breathing hard. "Not today. In a few days. When I've— Ciel said the scar was softening. If I came back to it, more times, slowly, maybe the whole root could—"

"No," said Ram.

Everyone looked at her.

She was still touching her forehead. She took her hand away and folded both hands in her lap, very neatly, on her apron, the way Rem folded hers.

"Stop there," said Ram.

"But it might—"

"It might grow back," said Ram. "The whole root. The horn, even. Someday. If you kept coming. Is that what you mean?"

Yuta did not answer.

"I don't want it," said Ram.

The room was very quiet.

"Nee-sama," said Rem, behind her hands. "Nee-sama, if it could—"

"If it grew back," said Ram, not looking at her, "you would stop fussing over me." She looked at the west window. "You would stop bringing me tea I haven't asked for. And checking whether I've eaten. And sitting on the floor next to my bed because the chair isn't close enough." A pause. "And then what would you do with your hands?"

Rem stared at her.

"Once a week is fine," said Ram. "Once a week is very good. Better than I've had in years." She looked at Yuta, on the floor. "Thank you." It came out stiff, as if the word were not used to being said in her voice. "It was well done."

Then she looked at Rem again.

"And the horn," said Ram, quietly, so quietly that Mau in the doorway almost did not hear it, "was mine. I spent it. I'm not asking for change."

Rem took her hands away from her mouth. Her face was wet. She did not argue. She came round the end of the bed and sat down on it beside her sister, very close, and leaned her head on Ram's shoulder, and Ram allowed it.

Yuta got up off the floor, slowly, with Maki holding his arm. He looked at the two of them on the bed for a moment. Then he bowed, a small bow, the way he bowed to people he respected, and went out past Mau in the doorway without a word.

In the corridor, Mau heard Maki say, very low, "That was good. That was really good." And Yuta say, "I know," in a voice that was trying not to shake. And then nothing, for a while.



So it was once a week.

Frieren filled her on the seventh day of every week, after breakfast, in the room with the west window. A quarter of an hour. Then she went back to her life. To the red stake. To her grimoire. To the reading chair. To arguing about the shelf.

Ram called it *the appointment*. She said it the way the witches had once said it, about the room at the old inn with the chair that wobbled: as a thing that happened, regularly, and was tiresome, and could not be avoided.

"Like their tea," she said, at dinner, nodding at the witches' end of the table. "Once a week. Very tedious."

"It's not tedious," said Pride, from the witches' end, with her hands flat on the table. "It's a conversation."

Ram looked at her for a long moment.

"Acceptable," said Ram.

And on the evening of the first appointment day, Frieren came back from the west wing, and did not look as if she had carried water from the river. She sat down beside Mau at the long table, and picked up her spoon, and ate her stew, and then, after dinner, went up to the east room and sat in the reading chair and opened the grimoire from the mimic, for the first time in five days, and read until midnight, while Mau held the lamp.

"You missed it," said Mau, at some point.

"What?"

"The book."

Frieren did not look up from the page.

"I didn't mind," she said. "She needed it more." She turned a page. "But yes." Another page. "I missed it."
