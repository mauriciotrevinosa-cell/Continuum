*When: Days 129 and 130: the west wing's last beam, the List, and a roof night. The gate is in its fourth week, and Mikasa has her new blades.*

The house noticed Rem before it noticed anything else, and it took the house about two days to understand what it was noticing.

It started with the tea.

Rem poured the tea at breakfast, the way she had poured it every morning since she came. From the left. The handle turned toward the right hand. She poured Fern's first, and Seiko's, and Rimuru's. She came to Senku, who was drawing something on the back of a ledger page with a pencil in each hand, and poured his, and set it down, and said, pleasantly:

"Senku-sama. If you draw on the table again, I'll serve your tea in a cup with a hole in it. You won't know which day."

Senku looked up.

Rem smiled at him, sweetly, and moved on to Okarun.

Senku looked at the table, where there was, faintly, the ghost of a gear train in pencil beside his plate. He looked at Rem's back. He looked at Suika, who was staring at Rem with her mouth slightly open.

"Did she just—" said Senku.

"Yes," said Suika.

"*Rem*?"

"*Yes*," said Suika.



By lunch, it had happened four more times.

Rem told Himmel, who had struck a pose in the doorway of the kitchen, that his columns at the gate were very heroic and that she had noticed he walked past them three times a day to look at them, and that she would happily paint a small portrait of him on one, if he'd like, so he could look at himself directly and save time. Himmel was so delighted by the idea that it took him a full minute to realize he was being teased, and then he was even more delighted.

She told Vamola, who was on her fourth grey brick from the first box of gate rations and had declared them excellent, which had made Umaru stop speaking to her for a day, that she was the only person in two worlds who liked them, and that Rem would make her a fifth out of mud and see if she noticed. Vamola considered this seriously, and said she would probably notice, but would eat it anyway, to be polite.

She told Milim, who had broken another cup, that she had moved all the cups in the house to a shelf exactly one inch higher than Milim could reach without flying, and that if Milim flew in the kitchen she would be served her dinner on a plate, in the fort, by Umaru, with a speech.

She told Fern, who was writing in the ledger, that the ledger was the only thing in the house with good posture, and Fern looked up, startled, and went pink, and then, slowly, *laughed*, which Fern did not do at the long table.

And she told Mau, at lunch, when he came in from the timber yard with sawdust in his hair, that he had sawdust in his hair, and that Frieren had told her he looked like a sheep that had lost an argument with a carpenter, and that Rem agreed.

Mau stood in the arch of the common room with sawdust in his hair and stared at her.

Rem smiled at him. Not the small careful smile. Not the one like a person carrying a very full cup across a room. A wide, bright, wicked one, with something dancing at the back of it.

"Welcome back, Mau-kun," said Rem, and went to get the soup.

Mau looked at Frieren, at the long table.

"Did you tell her—"

"Yes," said Frieren, not looking up from her bread.

"*A sheep*—"

"That lost an argument with a carpenter," said Frieren. "It was a good line. She improved it."



"It's the old one," said Ram, at dinner.

She was sitting at the far end of the table, by the teapot, in her place, with Seika on her other side. They had sat that way at every meal for four days, two older sisters side by side with their arms folded, saying nothing to each other and everything about everyone else, and the whole house had stopped trying to sit anywhere near them.

"The old what?" said Umaru, from the fort's end.

"Rem," said Ram. She nodded down the table at her sister, who was pouring Okarun's tea and telling him, very kindly, that his glasses were steaming up again and that she could see him looking at Momo through the steam, and that so could everyone. Okarun had gone the colour of a beetroot. Momo, beside him, was looking very hard at her stew. "That's the old Rem. From before."

"Before what?" said Umaru.

Ram did not answer at once. She looked down the table at her sister.

"Before she decided she had to be careful," said Ram. "With everyone. All the time. Because she'd done something once she was sorry for, and she thought if she was careful enough for long enough, it would make up for it." She picked up her cup. "Then, in my world, something bad happened. And it ended well. And for a few weeks after, she stopped being careful." She drank. "She was like this. Kind. Sharp. Laughing at everyone. Teasing the idiot we lived with until he couldn't speak." A pause. "That's the one who was peeling potatoes. That's the one I lost in the kitchen."

The fort's end of the table had gone quiet.

"She was careful here," said Umaru, in a small voice. "When she came. So careful. All the time. Like she was carrying something that might spill."

Ram looked at her. Then down the table at her sister.

"Because you weren't here," said Seika, beside her. She did not look at Ram. She was looking at Nijika, across the room, who was laughing at something Kita had said. "When the person you're careful *for* isn't there, you get careful about everything. You don't know which thing to be careful about any more. So you're careful about all of it." She drank her tea. "Then they come back. And you remember which thing it was. And you can put the rest down."

Ram looked at her for a long time.

"Acceptable," said Ram.

"Hah," said Seika.

Down the table, Rem had reached the fort's end with the teapot, and was telling Umaru, very gravely, that the Ministry's last decree had a spelling mistake in it, and that she had corrected it in pencil, and that the Ministry should check its archives. Umaru stared at her in total betrayal, and then ran to the toll booth to check, and came back, and said "*REM*," in a voice of anguish, and Rem laughed.

It was not a small laugh. It was not careful. It went all the way down the long table and back, bright and clear, and half the house turned to look at her, and she did not stop, and she did not seem to care who saw.

*One hundred percent,* said Ciel, very quietly, inside Mau. Not to anyone. Just to herself, it seemed. *That is what it looks like. When someone is all the way back.*



The house saw it in other ways too.

On the hundred-and-twenty-ninth day, the west wing's last beam went up. It was the long one, for the roof of the corridor, the one Wakana had said would need twenty people on ropes. Milim walked over to lift it, the way she lifted things, and found that Rem had already lifted the other end.

Rem was standing at the far end of the beam, in her maid's dress, holding forty feet of oak at shoulder height by herself, without visible effort, with an expression of mild polite interest. Something had changed in how she stood. Mau could not have said what. Only that she had always been strong, and careful about it, and now she was strong and not careful about it at all. On her forehead, under the blue fringe, for a moment, there was a faint pale shimmer. Not a horn. The suggestion of one. Gone as soon as he looked.

Milim stared at her.

"*You're strong*," said Milim.

"Yes," said Rem.

"You were *always* strong?"

"Yes," said Rem. "I just didn't use it very much." She shifted her grip on the beam. "Shall we put it up, Milim-sama? Gently?"

Milim's whole face lit up, the way it had lit up at the gate, and in the corridor, and on the ridge beam. Someone else, at last, in this house, who could carry the other end.

"*Gently*," said Milim, enormously happy, and they put it up together, gently, with Wakana underneath shouting *left, left, your left*, and the whole west wing roof went on in an afternoon.

Ram watched from the window of her room, with her arms folded, and did not say anything. But when Rem came back in that evening, Ram looked at her for a long moment and said, "You were showing off."

"Yes," said Rem.

"Good," said Ram.



On the hundred-and-thirtieth morning, Fern opened the ledger at breakfast and turned it to the page of the List.

*THE LIST.*

*1. Nijika: her sister. First. HERE.*

*2. Rem: her sister. When we're ready.*

She dipped her pen. After *When we're ready*, she wrote one word, in ink. Then she turned the ledger round on the table so that everyone could see.

*2. Rem: her sister. When we're ready. HERE.*

At the far end of the table, by the teapot, Rem put the teapot down.

She looked at the ledger for a long time. Then she looked at her sister, beside her, who was looking at her porridge with an expression of profound distaste.

"Nee-sama," said Rem. "You're in the ledger."

"I can see," said Ram, not looking up. "Fern's handwriting is very neat." A pause. "Yours is terrible."

"I know," said Rem, and laughed.

And then there was a pause at the long table. Everyone was looking at the ledger, and at the space under the second line, where there was nothing yet.

Fern did not write anything. She looked down the table, at Yuta.

Yuta had stopped eating. He was sitting very still, beside Maki, with his hand on the table, and the ring on his finger. Beside him, on her high seat at the north end, Seiko had put down her fan.

"My teacher," said Yuta.

The table was quiet.

"And my coin," said Seiko, from the high seat, "for the doctor. When he wants her." She picked up her fan again. "I said so on the fortieth. I don't say things twice."

Yuta looked down at the table. At his own hand. At the ring.

"Not yet," he said. "Not this week. But next." He looked up. "When the west wing's dry. When there's a room." He paused. "He'll want a big one. With a window. He'll complain about it either way."

Fern dipped her pen.

*3. Yuta: his teacher. When we're ready.*

She did not turn the ledger round. She did not need to. Everyone had seen her write it.

Under the long table, Maki's hand found Yuta's, and held it.



That night was a roof night. It was the seventh day, and the roof was once a week now, since the snow, since the laugh. Mau climbed up first, then Rimuru, in human shape, with a blanket. Then Yuta.

The snow was nearly gone off the roof of the real house. The bark shingles were wet and dark under the stars. Far off, past the north field, the blue light of the gate stood up out of the last of the snow, humming, faintly, the way it always hummed now. Week four. Mikasa had gone in that morning with Maki and Yuta and a pair of new gate-forged blades from the shop, the third thing the house had ever bought, and had come out grinning, which nobody had ever seen her do in a dungeon.

They sat on the ridge in a row, the three of them, and did not say anything for a while.

"He's going to be insufferable," said Yuta at last.

Rimuru laughed.

"He's going to walk in," said Yuta, "and look at all of you, and say something terrible. To Milim, probably. Or Diablo." He was looking at the gate. "And then he's going to find out about the rematch."

"The rematch," said Mau.

"Sukuna," said Yuta. "He's going to find out Sukuna's out there. In this world. And he's going to want his rematch." He was quiet for a moment. "And he's going to look at you, Mau. At what you are. At the wheel. And he's going to say something I can't predict, because I never could." He rubbed his face. "He's going to be *so* insufferable."

Nobody said anything.

"He'd have loved this," said Yuta. Then he stopped, and looked down at his hands, and laughed, very quietly, at himself. "No," he said. "He's *going* to love this."

Mau looked at him.

Yuta was smiling. At the gate. At the roof. At the dark settlement under them, with its lamps going out one by one, and the new west wing with its fresh roof against the stars, and Ram's window, and Rem's at the turn, and the empty room beside them both, built and finished, with a bed in it and the stove laid, waiting.

"He's going to love this," said Yuta again, more quietly, and did not say anything else for a long time.

Below them, in the west wing, a lamp was still lit in the room with the west window. Through it, if you leaned out over the edge of the ridge, which Rimuru did, you could see two girls in maid's dresses, sitting side by side on the edge of the bed, one with pink hair and one with blue, not talking. The blue-haired one had her head on the pink-haired one's shoulder.

The pink-haired one was allowing it.
