## Chapter 1 — Yuta's Coin

*When: Day 131, the morning after the roof night. The gate is in its fourth week, Ram's appointment is once a week, the west wing's empty room has a bed in it and the stove laid, and Seika's ninth song for Echidna is half learned.*

Yuta told Fern first, because that was the rule now. Nijika had done it, and Rem had done it, and the rule had been made by the doing.

He came down at dawn on the hundred-and-thirty-first day and found her where she always was, at the long table with the ledger open and a pen in her hand, writing nothing, listening to the house wake up. He sat down across from her. He did not say anything for a while. Neither did she.

"The room's ready," said Yuta at last.

Fern looked up.

"The empty one," said Yuta. "In the west wing. Rimuru's rule. One empty room, always." He was looking at the table, at his own hand on it, at the ring. "Wakana laid the stove yesterday. There's a bed. There's a window." He paused. "He'll hate the window. He'll say it faces the wrong way. He says that about every window."

Fern did not write anything. She turned the ledger to the page of the List and looked at the third line.

*3. Yuta: his teacher. When we're ready.*

"Are we ready?" said Fern.

Yuta was quiet for a long moment.

"I don't know if *he's* ready," he said. "For us. For this." He looked out of the window at the yard, grey in the dawn. "But I don't think he ever got to be ready for anything. Not once. Everything just happened to him, and he was the strongest, so he handled it, and nobody ever asked." He turned the ring on his finger. "I'd like to ask him. Just once. Even if the answer's *no, I'm dead, leave me alone*."

"Can it reach him?" said Fern quietly. "If he's—"

"Rimuru asked Ciel," said Yuta. "After Nijika. After the rule." He did not look away from the window. "Love doesn't need a living mouth. That's what Ciel said. It finds them where they are." He swallowed. "Wherever that is."

Fern looked at him for a long time.

Then she dipped her pen, and beside the third line, in the margin, very small, she wrote the date.

"Tonight?" said Fern.

"Tonight," said Yuta.



The house knew by lunch. It always did.

It did what it had done twice before, and it did it without being asked, because it had learned how. Momo made the pear bread, the third loaf. *FOR TOMORROW (OR WHENEVER).* Umaru made a slate: *APPLICANT: YUTA'S TEACHER (NAME: GOJO). STATUS: PENDING. CUSHION: RESERVED (NEXT #3).* And under *notes*, after a very long pause and a whispered consultation with Maki: *is apparently very tall. and very loud. and likes sweets. the ministry is preparing sweets.*

Seiko said nothing at all.

She sat on her high seat at the north end of the common room all afternoon with her paper fan, watching the house prepare, and every so often her hand went to the pocket of her robe, where her coin was. She had promised it on the fortieth day. *To Yuta's teacher. For the doctor.* She had said she did not say things twice.

At dinner, Umaru, who could not bear it any longer, asked her.

"Grandma Seiko. Your coin. Are you going to—tonight? Too?"

"No," said Seiko.

The table went quiet.

"I said I'd give it to him," said Seiko. "*Him.* Yuta's teacher. To use for the doctor he loves." She snapped the fan shut. "I'm not calling a woman I've never met to a house where the man she's grieving hasn't even arrived yet. That's not a gift. That's a cruelty." She looked down the table at Yuta. "When he's here, and standing, and has eaten something, I'll put it in his hand. And he can decide. Not before."

Yuta looked at her for a long moment.

"Thank you," he said.

"Hmph," said Seiko, and opened the fan again.

From Okarun's shoulder, Turbo Granny said that in her day people did not go around summoning the dead with coins, they summoned them properly, with a curse and a grudge and a phone number, and that this whole business was very lax. Okarun told her to be quiet. She was not quiet. She went on being not quiet for some time.

Nobody minded. It was something to listen to.



Mau found Yuta on the roof at eleven.

It was not a roof night. Roof nights were the seventh day, and this was the first. But Yuta was there anyway, on the ridge of the real house, where the bark shingles were wet from the thaw, sitting with his knees up and his arms round them and the ring catching the starlight. Maki was beside him. Close. Her shoulder against his.

Mau stopped at the top of the ladder.

"I can go," he said.

"Stay," said Maki, before Yuta could say anything. She did not look round. "He wanted you here. He didn't want to ask."

So Mau came up, and sat down on Yuta's other side, on the cold ridge, and the three of them looked out at the dark settlement and the blue light of the gate standing up out of the north field, humming, and did not say anything for a while.

"I keep thinking about the scar," said Yuta.

Mau looked at him.

Yuta had never said a word about the scar. Not once, in all the time Mau had known him. The thin line of old stitches across his forehead, under his hair, pale and neat, as if someone had once sewn him shut very carefully and then taken the thread out. Everyone in the house had seen it. Nobody had ever asked. It was Yuta's, the way the ring was Yuta's, and that had always seemed like enough.

"He's going to see it," said Yuta. "The second he takes the blindfold off. He'll know exactly what it is." He touched it, under his hair, very lightly, with two fingers. "And I'm going to have to tell him."

"Tell him what?" said Mau.

Yuta did not answer for a long time.

"Not tonight," he said at last. "I'll tell everyone. When he's here. Because he'll make me." He let his hand fall. "But not tonight. Tonight I just want to—" He stopped.

Maki took his hand.

He took the coin out of his pocket with the other.



He did not close his eyes. That was the thing Mau remembered afterward. Nijika had closed hers, and Rem, in her room at the turn. Yuta sat on the ridge of the real house with the coin in his fist and his eyes open, looking at the stars over the black hills, the way you look at someone across a room.

He thought of his teacher.

Mau could not see what Yuta thought of. But he could see Yuta's face, in the starlight, and he watched it go through things, the way Kita said Nijika's had. It did not go through anything sad. That surprised him. He had thought it would be the end, the thing that happened at the end, whatever it was. But Yuta's face, on the ridge, was doing something else entirely. It was trying not to laugh.

A train. Rice cakes. A man lying across three seats with his blindfold over his face saying *you're doing great, I'm so proud of you, I'm going to be sick.*

A hand on top of Yuta's head, once, in a corridor, when he was very young and very frightened and had a curse living in his ring that killed people, and everyone was afraid of him. A voice, light and careless and absolutely certain, saying something to him that nobody else had ever said.

Yuta's mouth moved. Mau did not hear what it said.

Then the voice came.

It came to the roof, and to nowhere else. Maki did not hear it. Mau did not hear it. They only saw Yuta's face go very still, and his eyes go wide in the starlight, and his fist close so hard round the coin that his knuckles went white.

*You are sure? Satoru Gojo.*

Yuta sat on the ridge of the real house.

"Yes," he said. "Obviously."

He said it the way you say something you have been saying in your head for a very long time. Steady. Almost cheerful. Almost the way a careless certain man might once have said something to a frightened boy in a corridor.

The coin went warm. Then warmer.

Then it was gone.

Yuta opened his hand. His palm was empty, and pink where the coin had been, and he looked at it for a long time.

It had carried.

Mau understood what that meant at the same moment Yuta did. He saw it land on Yuta's face, in the starlight. Love both ways, Ciel had said. Love doesn't need a living mouth. It finds them where they are.

Wherever Gojo was, the coin had found him. And he had loved Yuta back.

Yuta made a sound. It was not crying. It was something like a laugh that had been stepped on. Maki put her arm round him and pulled his head down onto her shoulder, hard, the way she did everything, and held it there.

"Idiot," said Maki, into his hair. "Of course it carried."

"I know," said Yuta, muffled.

"Then why are you—"

"Because I *know*," said Yuta, and did not say anything else for a long time.



Mau left them there, after a while. He climbed down the ladder into the yard and stood under the ink tree in the dark and looked up at the two shapes on the ridge against the stars, close together.

*Something in you,* said Ciel, quietly, *is turned toward Arrival House.*

Mau went still.

*Not the way it turns toward a threat,* said Ciel. *The way it turned toward the gate. On the first day. The way it turns toward something it has never met, and wants.* A pause. *I do not know why. I am telling you because you asked me to tell you everything.*

Mau looked across the dark yard and the dark plaza at the black windows of Arrival House, and the bell tower over it, silent.

He put his hand flat on his chest, over the place behind his breastbone, and left it there.

*Watch it,* he thought.

*I am,* said Ciel.



By two in the morning the fort was on the step of Arrival House in a row, in hoods, with blankets, the way the fort was now on the step of Arrival House whenever a coin had been spent. It was the third time. The fort had a system. Suika had the tea. Ori had the blankets. Mai, at the end, had nothing, and had come anyway.

By three there were twenty of them. By four, Seiko, in her armchair, carried out by Okarun and Momo and set down at the foot of the step with her fan in her lap and her coin in her pocket, saying nothing, watching the door.

By five, Yuta and Maki came down off the roof and across the yard and sat down on the step in the middle, where everyone had left a space for them without being asked.

Yuta sat with his elbows on his knees and his hands loose between them, looking at the door of Arrival House behind him, and then at the road in front of him, and then at the door again.

"He's going to be so annoying," he said, to nobody, in a small cracked voice. "About the crowd."

Nobody answered. But all along the step, in the grey light, twenty people smiled.

At the far end, very quietly, Seiko took her hand out of her pocket, and looked at the coin in her palm, and closed her fingers over it again.

"Not yet," she said, to the coin. "When he's eaten something."
