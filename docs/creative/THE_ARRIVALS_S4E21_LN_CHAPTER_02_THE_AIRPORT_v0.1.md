*When: Day 132, the morning after Yuta's coin. Twenty people on the step since before dawn; Seiko's coin still in her pocket.*

He did not wait for the bell.

That was the first thing. Seika had come at the eleventh bell, and Ram before the door was open, but both times the bell in the tower over Arrival House had rung once, by itself, first. This time, at a little after seven, with Jinshi still crossing the plaza with the key in his hand, the door of Arrival House opened from the inside.

Twenty people on the step turned round.

A man was standing in the doorway.

He was very tall. Taller than Himmel. Taller than anyone in the settlement except Diablo, and he stood as if he had never once in his life considered that height was something you might apologise for. He wore dark clothes with a high collar, cut close, the kind of thing a student wore in Yuta's world. His hair was white. Not grey. White, like new snow, and it stood up off his head in every direction as if it had never met a comb and had no intention of starting. Across his eyes, tied at the back of his head, there was a black band of cloth.

He had one hand on the doorframe and the other in his pocket. He was looking out at the plaza, at the step, at twenty people in blankets with tea, at the dawn coming up grey and pink over the black hills, with his whole blindfolded face, as if he could see all of it perfectly. Which, Mau found out later, he could.

"Oh," said the man. "Huh."

Nobody moved.

"So," said the man, brightly, to the plaza in general. "I was at the airport."

Nobody said anything.

"Really nice airport," the man went on. "Very clean. Everyone I ever lost was there. We were talking about where to go next. North, south, that kind of thing. I was just about to pick a direction." He tilted his head. "And then somebody said my name."

He turned his blindfolded face, slowly, along the step. Past the fort in their hoods. Past Seiko in her armchair. Past Mau and Frieren. Past twenty people holding their breath.

To the middle of the step. Where Yuta was sitting, very still, with Maki's hand in his.

The man looked at him for a long time.

"Yo," he said.



Yuta stood up.

He did it slowly, the way you stand up when you are not sure your legs will hold you. He stood on the step of Arrival House with the blanket sliding off his shoulders and looked at the man in the doorway, and his face was doing so many things at once that Mau could not follow any of them.

"Gojo-sensei," said Yuta.

"Yuta." The man grinned. It was a wide, careless, delighted grin, and it took up the whole lower half of his face. "Look at you. You got tall." He paused. "Taller. You were already tall. You got *taller*."

"I didn't," said Yuta.

"You did. I can tell. I'm very perceptive." The man came down the first step, and then the second, unhurried, with his hands in his pockets. "You've got a ring. And a girl holding your hand. And a whole town sitting on a step at dawn waiting for me, which is honestly the most flattering thing that's ever—"

He stopped, two steps from Yuta.

He stopped, and stood very still, and his blindfolded face was turned toward Yuta's, and for one instant, so brief that Mau almost missed it, the bright careless grin was not there at all. The face under the blindfold was turned very slightly upward. Toward Yuta's forehead. Toward his hair, and what was under his hair.

Then the grin came back, as if it had never gone.

"—flattering thing that's ever happened to me," said the man, smoothly. "And I'm *very* flattered, as a rule."

Yuta did not say anything. He stood on the step with his hand still in Maki's and looked at his teacher.

Then he let go of Maki's hand, and closed the last two steps, and put his arms round the man in the doorway, hard, and put his face against the high collar, and held on.

The man stood there for a moment with his hands still in his pockets.

Then he took them out, and put them round Yuta, and held on too.

"Hey," he said, quietly, into Yuta's hair, in a voice that was not bright or careless at all. "Hey. You're shaking."

"I know," said Yuta, muffled.

"You did great," said the man. "I don't even know what you did. But I can tell you did great." A pause. "I'm so proud of you." Another pause. "I'm going to be sick."

Yuta made a sound that was half a laugh and half something else entirely, and did not let go.



His name was Satoru Gojo. He told the register girl himself, leaning on her table in the intake hall with his chin on his hand, while she wrote it down in her small careful script with her face pink to the ears.

*Gojo Satoru. Arrived. By coin. The third.*

And then, in the margin, very small, after a long pause: *(tall.)*

He wanted to see everything. That was clear in the first ten minutes. He walked out of Arrival House into the plaza with Yuta on one side of him and Maki on the other and twenty people trailing behind, and he turned in a slow circle in the middle of the plaza, looking at everything, with his blindfolded face.

"Okay," he said. "Okay, okay, okay. This is great. This is *so* great." He pointed at Rimuru. "You're a slime."

"Sometimes," said Rimuru.

"You're a slime who's also a guy. And you've got something in you that's talking." Gojo tilted his head. "Hi, something."

*Hello,* said Ciel, through Rimuru, sounding faintly offended.

"She says hello," said Rimuru.

"*She.* Great." He turned. He pointed at Milim, who was standing very straight beside Ori with her hands clenched under her chin, vibrating. "You're a *dragon*."

Milim's whole face lit up.

"I'm a Dragonoid," she said. "And a Demon Lord. And the Guardian of the fort. And Head of Fun."

"That's a lot of jobs."

"I'm very good at all of them."

"I bet." He turned again. He pointed at Diablo, who was standing at the edge of the crowd in his butler's coat with his hands folded and an expression of the most profound and polite hostility. "You're a demon. A really old one. And you hate me already."

"Kufufufu," said Diablo, which was not a no.

"Love that," said Gojo, and turned again, and pointed, and stopped.

He was pointing at Frieren.

She was standing beside Mau at the edge of the crowd in her shawl, with her hands in her sleeves, looking at him the way she looked at a grimoire she had not read yet: with total, polite, unhurried interest.

Gojo looked at her for a long moment.

"You're very old," he said.

"Yes," said Frieren.

"Like, *old* old. Older than the demon."

"Much older."

"And the thing in you isn't cursed energy. It's—" He tilted his head the other way. "It's like cursed energy, but it doesn't come from feeling bad. It just comes from *you*. Like a well." He sounded genuinely delighted. "That's so cool. Where does it go when you're asleep?"

Frieren considered the question seriously.

"Nowhere," she said. "It stays."

"*Huh*," said Gojo, with enormous satisfaction, as if she had told him something he had wondered about for years.

Then he looked past her. At Mau.

He stopped smiling.

It was not like the moment on the step. It was not a flicker. The grin simply went, and stayed gone, and the long white-haired man stood in the middle of the plaza with his blindfolded face turned toward Mau and did not move at all.

The plaza went very quiet.

Mau stood still. He could feel it, the way he had felt it at the gate. Something behind his breastbone, small and old, lifting its head. Listening.

And something else. Something new. A pressure. Not from inside. From outside. From the man in the blindfold, looking at him. As if he were being read. All of him. Every line.

"Huh," said Gojo, very quietly.

Nobody moved.

"Infinity," said Gojo. "In there. Not like mine. Wrapped up. Tied down. Something's holding it shut." He tilted his head. "And Blue. And Red. And—" He stopped. His voice changed, very slightly. "And the Void. My Void. In a box. In you."

Frieren's hand had come out of her sleeve. It was on Mau's arm.

Gojo looked at Mau a moment longer.

Then, slowly, the grin came back.

"So," said Gojo, lightly. "You stole all of mine."

The plaza held its breath.

Mau did not know what to say. He opened his mouth. He did not have anything to put in it.

"Relax," said Gojo. "Relax. Everybody relax. I'm *flattered*." He put his hands back in his pockets. "Mostly."

He turned away, toward the real house, with its long bark roof and its two vermilion columns, as if nothing had happened.

"Yuta," he said. "I was told there would be sweets."



There were sweets. The Ministry had been preparing them since the previous afternoon, in a state of great seriousness, and they were laid out on the long table in the common room on every plate the house owned: Momo's pear bread, and dried fruit in honey, and a tray of small round rice cakes from the batch Seiko had made for the ridge beam, which she had been saving, and a bowl of something Umaru described as *experimental* and refused to explain.

Gojo ate all of it.

He sat at the long table with his blindfold on and ate everything, steadily, happily, in an order that seemed to make sense only to him. He talked the whole time, with his mouth full, to anyone near him. He asked Senku how the waterwheel worked and listened to the whole answer, which nobody had ever done. He told Umaru the experimental bowl was the best thing he had ever eaten, and she went pink and had to sit down. He told Okarun he had a yokai's speed in his legs, which Okarun knew, and that it was "sick", which Okarun did not know, and Okarun went so red his glasses fogged. He asked Rem for more tea four times, and on the fourth time Rem told him he was going to float away, and he laughed so hard he nearly fell off the bench.

He did not look at Yuta's forehead again. Not once. Mau watched for it. He did not do it.

But he did not look at Mau again either. Not directly. Not once, the whole meal.

When the last rice cake was gone, Seiko stood up.

She came down off her high seat, which she did not often do, and walked the length of the common room with her fan in one hand. She stopped beside Gojo at the long table.

He turned his blindfolded face up toward her, chewing.

"You've eaten something," said Seiko.

"I've eaten *everything*," said Gojo. "Ma'am."

"Good." Seiko took her other hand out of the pocket of her robe and held it out to him, closed. "I said I'd give you this. On the fortieth day. Before I'd ever met you. Because the boy loves you, and he told us about a doctor." She opened her hand. The coin lay on her palm, small and faceless and warm. "One coin. One person. Someone you love." She held it out. "For the doctor. If you want her."

Gojo did not move.

He sat at the long table and looked at the coin in the old woman's palm with his blindfolded face, and the common room went very quiet, and for the third time that morning the grin was not there.

"Shoko," he said.

It was not a question. He said it the way you say a word you have not said out loud in a long time, to check it still works.

"Yuta says she was there," said Seiko. "After. That she was the one who—" She stopped. "That she'd be grieving."

Gojo did not say anything for a long moment.

Then he reached out, very carefully, as if it might break, and took the coin out of Seiko's palm, and closed his long fingers round it.

"Thank you," he said. Quietly. Not brightly. "Ma'am. Really."

"Seiko."

"Seiko." He held the coin. "Not yet, though."

The common room looked at him.

"She'd kill me," said Gojo, and the grin came back, a little crooked. "If I called her to a place I haven't even checked. A place with a slime and a dragon and a demon who hates me and a kid with my Void in a box." He tucked the coin into the inside of his high collar, very carefully. "She buried me. She deserves better than *surprise, I'm alive, it's weird here*." He stood up. "Let me look around first. Let me make sure it's somewhere I'd want her." He looked round the common room, at all of them. "It's probably somewhere I'd want her. But let me look."

Seiko looked at him for a long moment.

"Hm," she said.

"Hm," said Gojo, exactly the same.

And Seiko Ayase, who did not approve of anyone, nodded once, slowly, and went back to her high seat.



That night, in the east room, Mau lay in bed and did not sleep.

"He knows something," said Frieren, beside him, in the dark.

"About the coin?"

"About Yuta." She was quiet for a moment. "On the step. When he stopped. He looked at Yuta's forehead. Just for a moment. Then he smiled again." Another pause. "Himmel used to do that. Smile over something. So nobody had to see it yet."

Mau did not say anything.

"And he didn't look at you again," said Frieren. "Not once. All through the meal. After the plaza."

"I know."

"Does it hurt?"

He thought about it. About the pressure, in the plaza. Being read. Every line of him, in a single look, by someone who knew exactly what every line was.

"No," he said. "It felt like being seen." He was quiet for a moment. "All the way down. By someone who knew what he was looking at."

Frieren did not say anything.

Then her cold foot found his leg under the blanket, early, hours before the reset hour, which it almost never did.

"One," she said, very quietly. "Still here."

"Still here," said Mau.

Behind his breastbone, very quietly, something small and old was still listening. Toward the west wing. Toward the empty room that was not empty any more, where a tall white-haired man was lying awake on a bed that faced the wrong way, with a coin tucked inside his collar and the blindfold off, looking at the ceiling with eyes that saw everything.
