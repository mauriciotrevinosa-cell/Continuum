# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 24
## Chapter 9 — The Dress

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 24  
**Reading order:** 9  
**Continuity state:** Day 137 | Senku lends the lantern: 'Ten billion percent she's going to cry' | Wakana sewed the pale blue magical-girl dress from her first-week descriptions | the movie room; she cries quietly | his painted slide of her, the way he sees her | they dance to nothing; 'Next time you're wearing one too'

*When: Day 137, the first of the three dating days. The same day as Bocchi and Kita's, at dawn, in the next chapter. The fort has taken over every chore in the house and is doing them very loudly.*

Wakana asked Senku for the lantern on the morning of the hundred-and-thirty-seventh day, and Senku said yes before he had finished asking.

"For the evening," said Wakana. "Just the evening. I'll be careful with the lens. I won't touch the—"

"Take it," said Senku. He was at the long table, staring at the cracked lens, which he had not stopped staring at for two days. "Take the slides. Take the oil. Take the movie room." He waved a hand without looking up. "Take whatever you need. You've built half this house. You can have one evening of the lantern."

Wakana stood there for a moment.

"Senku," he said. "Thank you."

Senku looked up at last. He looked at Wakana's face, and at the roll of pale blue cloth under his arm, wrapped in brown paper, which Wakana had been carrying round the house all morning as if it might escape.

"Ten billion percent," said Senku quietly, "she's going to cry."

"I know," said Wakana.

"Good," said Senku, and went back to his lens.



He had been sewing for two nights.

He had not told anyone. He had not needed to tell anyone, because nobody went into the workshop after dark except Wakana, and Senku, who did not notice anything that was not a lens. So for two nights he had sat at the bench with the lamp turned down and the pale blue cloth spread out in front of him and a needle in his fingers, and sewn.

He was good at sewing. He had always been good at sewing. In his world, before, it had been the thing he did with his grandfather's dolls: the small perfect kimonos, the tiny folds, the stitches you could not see. And then a girl had come into his life who wanted to be other people. Characters from shows, from games, from the comics she loved. She wanted to wear their clothes and stand like them and *be* them for a day. He had sewn for her. Costume after costume. It was how he had fallen in love, though it had taken him an embarrassingly long time to notice.

He had not sewn a costume for her once since the bridge.

He had sewn a fort. Bunk beds. Sashes for the Ministry, from the refuge curtains. Bathing robes, with Momo. He had never once sewn her anything that was only for her, for being someone she loved for a day.

So he sewed her the dress.

He sewed it from memory. Her memory, not his. In the first week after the bridge, when everything had been strange and frightening and new, Marin had talked about her favourite show for hours, at night, in the dark, to keep from thinking about home. A magical girl. Pale blue. A long dress with a high collar and layered sleeves that opened like flowers, and a sash, and a ribbon in the hair. She had described every piece of it. Every fold. With her hands, in the dark, over and over, because talking about it made the strange new world feel smaller.

He had remembered all of it. He had not known he was remembering. He found out at the bench, the first night, when his hands started cutting the cloth without asking him.



He took her to the movie room at dusk.

She did not know where they were going. He had asked her, at lunch, to keep the evening free. She had said *I've kept the whole three days free, I'm the one who decreed it*. Then she had looked at his face, and gone quiet, and said *okay*. She had spent the afternoon trying very hard not to guess, and had guessed eleven different things, all of them wrong, and told Milim all eleven.

At dusk he knocked on the door of their room above the workshop, which he had never done, and she opened it, and he was standing there in the corridor holding a parcel wrapped in brown paper.

"What's that?" said Marin.

"Open it in the movie room," said Wakana.

So they went down, across the yard, into the east section, to the long low room at the back with its three steps of cushions and its white wall. It was empty. The fort had been told, very sternly, by Umaru, that the movie room was off limits on Dating Day *by Ministry decree*. Senku's lantern stood on the top step, on its little stand, with the wick already trimmed and the lens polished and a single slide waiting beside it in a wooden frame, face down.

Wakana lit the lantern.

The white wall filled with soft gold light, a circle as wide as a door.

He held out the parcel.

Marin took it. She looked at him. She looked at the parcel. She sat down on the bottom step, in the light from the wall, and untied the string, and folded back the brown paper.

She did not say anything.

She sat on the step with the paper open in her lap and the pale blue cloth inside it, and the lantern light falling across it. Across the high collar, and the layered sleeves, and the sash folded on top, and the ribbon, pale blue, coiled on the sash like a sleeping thing.

She put one hand on it, very lightly, the way you touch a sleeping thing.

"This is—" said Marin.

She stopped.

"From the show," said Wakana. "The one you told me about. In the first week." He was standing very stiffly, by the lantern, with his hands at his sides, the way he stood when a joint he had cut was about to be tested. "You described it. Every night. With your hands." He swallowed. "I remembered. I didn't know I was remembering until I started cutting."

Marin lifted the dress out of the paper.

She held it up in front of her, in the light from the wall, at arm's length. The sleeves fell open like flowers. The collar stood up, perfect. The pale blue caught the gold of the lantern and went soft and luminous, like the sky over the sea at dawn.

Then she started to cry.

Not the way she usually cried, at the end of a movie or a story or a sad song, loudly and happily and all at once. Quietly. With the dress held up in front of her and her face going very still behind it, and the tears just coming, one after another, down her face and off her chin and onto the pale blue cloth.

"You made me a costume," said Marin.

"Yes."

"You haven't made me a costume since—"

"I know," said Wakana. "I'm sorry." He took a step toward her. "I made a fort. And beds. And sashes. And robes. And a whole house." Another step. "I didn't make you anything that was just for you. For being someone you love for a day." He stopped in front of the step. "I'm sorry. I forgot that was the most important thing I know how to make."

Marin looked up at him over the dress, with her face wet.

"Can I put it on?"

"It's yours," said Wakana. "That's what it's for."



She put it on behind the curtain at the side of the movie room, where the slides were kept, and it took a long time, because her hands were shaking, and because the sash was complicated. She called him to help with the back, and he came, and tied it, his fingers very careful, the way he tied the joints in the frame: so tight you needed a mallet, so neat you could not see how.

Then she stepped out from behind the curtain, into the light.

Wakana had carved the faces of dolls for his whole life. He had painted their eyes with a brush of three hairs. He knew exactly, to a fraction of a hair, how the light fell on a face and made it look alive.

He had never seen anything like it.

She stood in the gold circle on the white wall, in the pale blue dress, with her hair loose and the ribbon in it, and the sleeves open like flowers, and she did not pose. She always posed. She did not, this time. She just stood there in the light, looking at him, with her face still wet, and the dress catching the lantern and going soft and blue and luminous.

"Well?" said Marin. Her voice wobbled.

Wakana did not answer.

He went up the steps to the lantern. He picked up the slide that had been waiting beside it, face down, in its wooden frame. He turned it over. And he slid it into the lantern.

On the white wall, behind her, enormous, in the gold light, a girl appeared.

A girl in a pale blue dress. With a high collar and sleeves like flowers and a ribbon in her hair. Painted on glass, very small, with a brush of three hairs, in colours from Daphne's roots and flowers, so finely that you could see the folds in the sleeves and the light in her eyes. She was standing exactly the way Marin was standing now, without posing, looking out of the wall with a small surprised smile.

It was Marin. He had painted her the night before, from memory, in the dress she had not yet seen. The way he had always seen her.

Marin turned round.

She stood in the light in front of the white wall, in the dress, and looked up at herself, painted, ten times her size, wearing the same dress, smiling the same small surprised smile.

For a long time she did not say anything at all.

"That's how I look?" she said at last, very quietly. "To you?"

"That's how you look," said Wakana, from the lantern. "To everyone. Always." He paused. "That's just the only way I know how to show it."



She made him dance.

There was no music. The music room was on the other side of the house, Bocchi and Kita were on a date of their own, and nobody had thought to bring a guitar. Marin said it did not matter. She came up the steps and took his sawdust-lined hand in hers, and pulled him down into the light in front of the white wall. She put his other hand on the sash at her waist, very correctly, the way people did in the old films she loved, and put hers on his shoulder.

"I can't dance," said Wakana.

"Neither can I," said Marin. "Not really. Not like in the films." She started to sway, very slightly, in the gold light. "That's okay. Nobody's watching."

Nobody was. The fort had been told. The movie room was off limits by Ministry decree.

So they danced. Badly. Slowly. To nothing. In the light from Senku's lantern, in front of a painted girl in a pale blue dress ten times their size, who smiled down at them both with her small surprised smile.

At some point Marin put her face against his shoulder and stopped swaying and just stood there, in the dress, holding on.

"Next time," she said, into his shoulder, "you're wearing one too."

Wakana went red to the ears.

"Which one?"

"I'll think of one," said Marin. "I have a list."

"Of course you have a list."

"It's a very long list," said Marin. "I made it in the first week. In the dark. For when we got home." She was quiet for a moment. "I think this is home now. So I suppose I'd better start."

Wakana did not say anything.

He held on to her, in the light, in the empty movie room, with the lantern hissing softly on the top step and the painted girl smiling on the wall. And for the first time since the bridge, for one whole evening, neither of them made anything for anybody else at all.
