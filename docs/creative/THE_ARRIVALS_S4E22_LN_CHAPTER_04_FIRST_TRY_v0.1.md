## Chapter 10 — First Try

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 24  
**Reading order:** 10

*When: Day 137, at dawn. Marin and Wakana's evening is still hours away; the fort is already doing the breakfast dishes by decree, badly.*

They both planned it. That was the problem.

Kita planned it on the evening of the decree, in bed, in the dark, in Kessoku's room, very quietly, with her lips moving. It was a good plan. It had a picnic in it, and the river, and the place below the weir where the water was loud enough that nobody could hear you, and a blanket, and the pear bread, which she had asked Momo for in advance and been given with a look. It had a moment, near the end, when she was going to say something she had been practising.

Bocchi planned it on the same evening, in the music room, alone, in the cupboard under the private stair, with the door shut and a candle. It was also a good plan. It had the ink tree in it, at dawn, before anyone else was awake, and her guitar, and a song. A new one. She had been writing it since the snow, in the same notebook as *Looked, Quiet*, on the pages after, and had not shown it to anyone, and was going to show it to exactly one person, once, at dawn, under the tree, and then possibly die.

Neither of them told the other.

So at a quarter to six on the hundred-and-thirty-seventh morning, in the grey half-dark, Kita crept out of Kessoku's room with a basket over her arm and a blanket under it, very quietly, so as not to wake anyone. And at exactly the same moment, Bocchi crept up the private stair from the music room with her guitar case in both arms, very quietly, so as not to wake anyone.

They met on the landing.

They stood there looking at each other in the grey light. Kita with her basket. Bocchi with her guitar.

"Oh," said Kita.

"Oh," said Bocchi, very faintly.

There was a long silence.

"I was going to take you to the river," said Kita.

"I was going to take you to the tree," said Bocchi, at almost the same time.

They looked at each other.

Then Kita started to laugh. She put her hand over her mouth to stop it, because of the sleeping house, and it came out through her fingers anyway, helplessly, and Bocchi, standing on the landing with her guitar case, went red, and then redder. And then, to her own complete astonishment, she started to laugh too, a small wheezing sound like a bellows, with her face in the top of the guitar case.

"Both," said Kita, when she could. "We'll do both. The tree first. It's closer."



The ink tree stood in the middle of the yard in the grey dawn, with its great silver-green crown moving very slightly in a wind you could not feel. Under it, on the bench Wakana had built round the trunk, the frost had not melted yet. Kita spread the blanket on it. She sat down. She put the basket on her knees and looked at Bocchi, who was standing at the edge of the root circle holding her guitar case and not moving.

"You can go first," said Kita.

Bocchi did not move.

"Hitori-chan," said Kita gently. "You came up the stairs with your guitar at quarter to six in the morning. You can go first."

Bocchi looked at the guitar case for a long moment.

Then she sat down on the bench, at the very far end of it, as far from Kita as the bench went. She opened the case. She took out the guitar. She tuned it, very slowly, for much longer than it needed, with her hair over her face.

She took the notebook out of the case pocket, and opened it on her knee, to a page after *Looked, Quiet*.

She did not look at Kita.

"It's not finished," said Bocchi, to the notebook. "It doesn't have a name. The second verse is bad. I'm going to change the bridge. Ryo-san will say the bass line is boring, and she'll be right." She took a breath. "It's—it's for you. It's the first thing I ever wrote that's for one person. Not for the band. Not for a crowd. For—" She stopped. "For you."

Kita did not say anything. She sat very still at the other end of the bench with the basket on her knees.

Bocchi played.



It was quiet. That was the first thing. Bocchi's songs for the band were loud, even the slow ones, because Ryo and Nijika made them loud and Kita sang them out. This one was not. It was Bocchi alone, under the tree, in the grey light, playing very softly, so softly that the leaves overhead were almost louder.

She did not sing. She never sang in front of people. But she said the words. Half-said, half-breathed, under the guitar, very fast at first and then slower, with her eyes on the notebook. Kita leaned in to hear.

It was about a hand.

About a hand in a sleeve, hidden, cold, and somebody who always knew where it was. About standing in a yard full of people looking, and shaking afterward, alone. About somebody coming back an hour later, when nobody had asked them to, and sitting down on the floor in the red light, and reaching into the sleeve, and finding it on the first try.

*You always know where my hand is*, said the song. *Even when I don't.*

The second verse was bad. Bocchi had been right. The bridge needed changing. But Kita did not hear any of that. She sat at the other end of the bench with the basket on her knees and her hand over her mouth, and her eyes on Bocchi's fingers on the strings, and listened to every word.

When it ended, Bocchi stopped playing, and put her hand flat on the strings to quiet them, and looked at the notebook, and did not look up.

The yard was very quiet. The leaves moved overhead with their sound like rain.

"It doesn't have a name," said Bocchi again, very small.

Kita moved down the bench.

Not all at once. One space. Then another. Until she was sitting beside Bocchi, close, at the end of the bench where Bocchi had gone to be as far away as possible. She put the basket down on the frosty wood. She reached over, without looking, and found Bocchi's hand on the guitar strings. First try. She always did.

"I know what it should be called," said Kita.

Bocchi looked at her, at last.

"*First Try*," said Kita.

Bocchi stared at her.

Then her face did something very complicated. Several things at once, all of them red. And Kita, who had been practising something for the river, a whole speech, with a beginning and a middle and an end, forgot every word of it. She leaned over the guitar and kissed her.

Just once. Quickly. In the grey dawn, under the ink tree, with the frost on the bench and the leaves moving overhead.

Bocchi made a sound like a kettle reaching the boil. Then she put her face in the top of the guitar, and stayed there for a long time, and did not let go of Kita's hand.



At the window of Kessoku's room, upstairs, which looked out over the yard, Nijika had been awake since the landing.

She had heard them laughing. She had crept to the window in her socks and eased it open a crack and looked out, and seen them go under the tree. She had been about to close it again, because rule four said nobody may spy, and Nijika was a person who followed rules. But Ryo had come up behind her, yawning, and looked over her shoulder. And then the song had started, faint, through the cold air, and neither of them had been able to move.

Now Ryo was leaning on the windowsill beside her, chin in her hand, watching the two figures under the tree.

"Rule four," said Nijika, not taking her eyes off the yard.

"I'm not spying," said Ryo. "I'm birdwatching."

"There aren't any birds."

"There's two," said Ryo.

Nijika elbowed her. Not hard this time.

Then a third person came to the window behind them, in the ugly coat, with her arms folded, and stood at the sill, and looked down at the yard. At the tree. At the guitar, and the two heads close together over it, and the hands.

Seika looked for a long moment, with her face completely flat.

"Leave them," she said.

Nijika and Ryo turned round.

"Leave them," said Seika again. She did not take her eyes off the yard. "That's a first song for one person. You don't get to hear those." She turned away from the window. "Neither do I."

Nijika looked at the empty doorway where her sister had been.

Then she took Ryo by the sleeve, and pulled her away from the window, and shut it, very quietly, so the latch did not click.

"Your sister," said Ryo, "is a romantic."

"Don't ever say that to her face," said Nijika.

"I'm not stupid," said Ryo, and went back to bed.

But Nijika stood by the shut window for a while longer in the grey light, smiling at the floor. Then she went down to the kitchen, and found Momo, and asked, very casually, whether there was any of the pear bread left. There was a basket going down to the river later, she said, and it might want a second loaf.



They went to the river after.

The blanket, and the pear bread, and the place below the weir where the water was loud enough that nobody could hear you. Kita had a whole speech for it, with a beginning and a middle and an end, and she found, sitting on the blanket by the loud water with Bocchi's head on her shoulder and Bocchi's hand in hers, that she did not need any of it.

She said one line of it, near the end. The only line that mattered. She said it into Bocchi's hair, under the noise of the water, so that nobody in the world could hear it but one person.

Bocchi heard it.

She did not say anything back. Bocchi never said anything back, the first time. But that afternoon, in the music room, Ryo found a new page in the notebook, after *Looked, Quiet*, with the song on it. Above the first line, crossed out twice and written again, very small, in Bocchi's crabbed handwriting, was a title.

*First Try.*

Ryo looked at it for a long moment. Then she closed the notebook and put it back exactly where she had found it, and did not tell anybody she had seen it.

That evening, at dinner, she told Bocchi the bass line for the second verse was boring. Bocchi said she knew. Ryo said she had an idea for it. And Bocchi, very red, said: "Okay."
