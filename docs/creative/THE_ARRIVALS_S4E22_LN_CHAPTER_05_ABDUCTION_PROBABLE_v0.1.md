# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 24
## Chapter 11 — Abduction (Probable)

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 24  
**Reading order:** 11  
**Continuity state:** Night of Day 138 | Okarun's kit and star chart | the bell tower | Turbo Granny 'supervising', Vamola 'guarding' from the ladder | Seiko: 'There's soup' / 'You were jealous' | Momo remembers on purpose now | a shooting star: 'UFO' / 'a rock, on fire'; her wish: that he keeps looking for aliens; his: that she keeps believing in ghosts

*When: The night of Day 138, the second dating day. The same night as Fern and Stark's, in the next chapter. Mau has started counting things without meaning to, and nobody has told Okarun.*

Okarun had a kit.

He had made it in the two days after the decree, in a state of intense concentration that Momo described, to Aira, as *frightening*. It was a basket. In the basket there was a blanket, and a flask of tea, and two cups, and a bag of the dried fruit Momo liked, and a sky chart. He had drawn the chart himself on the back of a ledger page Fern had given him without asking why, with every star he could see from the north field marked in pencil and joined into shapes he had named himself.

*THE GREAT SAUCER*, said one. *THE TRACTOR BEAM*, said another. *ABDUCTION (PROBABLE)*, said a third, which was a cluster of six faint stars near the horizon that Okarun had looked at for an entire evening.

"It's a stargazing date," said Okarun, at the foot of the bell tower over Arrival House, at nine o'clock on the night of the hundred-and-thirty-eighth, with the basket over his arm. "The highest point inside the line. Best view in the settlement. We sit up there and look at the sky."

"For aliens," said Momo.

"For *stars*," said Okarun. "Mostly stars." A pause. "If anything moves, that's a bonus."

Momo looked at him for a long moment, in the dark, with her arms folded.

Then she started up the ladder.



The bell tower was not big. There was a platform at the top, under the bell, with a rail round it and just enough room for two people to sit if they sat close. Okarun spread the blanket. Momo sat on it. Okarun sat beside her, close, because there was no other way to sit, and went red, and poured the tea.

They looked at the sky.

It was a very good sky. The snow had gone from almost everywhere, and the air was cold and clear and still, and above the black hills there were more stars than either of them had ever seen in their own world, where the city lights drowned everything. The Great Saucer was there. The Tractor Beam. Abduction (Probable), low over the north field, beside the faint blue glow of the gate.

"That one," said Okarun, pointing, "I'm almost sure is a—"

"*Don't tell me what that one is*," said a voice from his shoulder. "I've heard. I've heard about that one for an hour. It's a star. It's always been a star. It's going to die a star."

Momo closed her eyes.

Turbo Granny was on Okarun's shoulder. She was always on Okarun's shoulder, or on his windowsill, which was her separate address, or in his pocket, in the small cat-shaped body she lived in now. She had not been invited on the date. She had come anyway, because she always came anyway, and Okarun had been so focused on the kit that he had not noticed her climb up his coat at the foot of the ladder.

"Granny," said Okarun. "This is a date."

"I know it's a date," said Turbo Granny. "The whole house knows it's a date. There's a decree. In crayon." She settled herself more comfortably on his shoulder. "I'm supervising."

"You're not supervising."

"Somebody has to. You'd sit up here all night talking about saucers, and she'd fall asleep, and you'd let her, and then you'd both catch cold and blame me."

"Why would we blame *you*?"

"Everyone always blames me," said Turbo Granny, with deep satisfaction.

And then, from below the platform, from the top rung of the ladder, a second voice said, very calmly:

"I'm here too."

Momo opened her eyes.

Vamola's head and shoulders were visible above the edge of the platform, on the ladder. She was holding on with one hand and eating something with the other.

"Vamola," said Momo.

"Yes."

"It's a *date*."

"I know," said Vamola. She considered this, chewing. "I'm guarding it."

"From *what*?"

Vamola looked round the platform. At the sky. At the bell. At the dark settlement below. At Turbo Granny on Okarun's shoulder.

"Her," said Vamola, nodding at Turbo Granny.

"*Me?!*"

"You're interfering," said Vamola. "I'm guarding the date from interference."

"You're *also* interfering!"

"I'm on the ladder," said Vamola. "The ladder isn't the date. The date is the platform." She took another bite. "I checked."

Momo put her face in her hands.

Okarun, beside her, holding a cup of tea in each hand, started to laugh. He tried not to. He could not help it. It came out of him in a helpless wheeze, and the teacups rattled. Turbo Granny on his shoulder told him to stop laughing, it was undignified, and Vamola on the ladder told Turbo Granny to stop interfering, and Turbo Granny told Vamola she was interfering by telling her to stop interfering. Momo sat in the middle of all of it with her face in her hands, and then, slowly, her shoulders started to shake too.



It was Seiko who ended it.

Her voice came up from the bottom of the tower, from the dark plaza, carrying the way only a very old woman's voice can carry. She had not been told about the date. She had been told about the date by everyone. And she had walked across the plaza at nine-fifteen with her paper fan and stood at the foot of the bell tower and listened for exactly long enough.

"*Vamola,*" said Seiko. "*Turbo.* Down. Now."

There was a pause on the platform.

"I'm guarding—" said Vamola.

"You're a nuisance," said Seiko, from below. "Both of you. Come down and leave the children alone. There's soup."

There was a longer pause.

"What kind of soup?" said Vamola.

"The good kind."

Vamola considered this for one second. Then she was gone down the ladder. She did not climb. She dropped, and landed in the plaza with a thump, and walked off toward the kitchen without looking back.

Turbo Granny did not move.

"I'm not going anywhere," she said, from Okarun's shoulder. "I'm supervising."

"*Turbo,*" said Seiko, in a voice that was very quiet and very old and had once, in another world, made a whole room of spirits go silent.

Turbo Granny went very still on Okarun's shoulder.

Then, without a word, she climbed down his coat, and down the ladder, one rung at a time, muttering. At the bottom, Seiko picked her up without looking at her, tucked her under one arm like a parcel, and walked away across the dark plaza toward the lit windows of the real house.

From under Seiko's arm, faintly, the two of them heard Turbo Granny say: *I was SUPERVISING.*

And Seiko say: *You were jealous.*

And then nothing.



The bell tower was very quiet after that.

Okarun and Momo sat on the blanket under the bell, close, because there was no other way to sit, and drank the tea, which had gone lukewarm, and looked at the sky.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

"Sorry," said Okarun at last. "About the—"

"Don't," said Momo. "It's fine." She was looking up. "It's always like that. With us. Everyone's always there." She paused. "I sort of like it."

Okarun looked at her.

She was not looking at him. She was looking at the sky, at Abduction (Probable), low over the gate, with her knees drawn up and her chin on them, and in the starlight her face was doing something he did not see very often, because Momo's face was mostly busy being annoyed at him.

"Momo," said Okarun.

"Mm."

"Do you remember the first time? In our world. When you said ghosts were real and I said aliens were real and we bet—"

"I remember," said Momo.

She said it very simply. Very carefully. The way you say something you once, for a while, could not.

Okarun went quiet.

Because there had been a time, in this world, after the bridge, when she had not remembered. Not all of it. Not him. When she had looked at him across a table in the old inn and not known why he was looking at her like that. It had been fixed. It had come back. But he still thought about it sometimes, at night. And she knew he did.

"I remember it on purpose now," said Momo. "Every day. A little bit. The bet, and the tunnel, and you being an idiot about the saucers." She did not look at him. "So it doesn't go anywhere again."

Okarun did not say anything. He did not trust his voice.

And then, overhead, something moved.

A streak of white light. Fast. Across the black sky from the Great Saucer toward the north field, bright, and then gone, leaving a faint line behind it on the dark that faded while they watched.

Okarun was on his knees on the blanket before he knew he had moved.

"*UFO*," said Okarun.

"It's a shooting star, idiot," said Momo.

"It was *moving*—"

"That's what shooting stars *do*."

"It could have been—"

"It was a rock," said Momo. "On fire. Falling." She was smiling. She did not try to hide it. "Make a wish."

Okarun stared at her.

"Make a wish," said Momo again. "That's what you do. When you see one. In your world and mine." She closed her eyes. "I'm making mine."

He watched her make it. Her eyes shut. Her lips moving very slightly. Her face, in the starlight, perfectly serious, the way she was serious about the things she pretended not to care about.

She opened her eyes.

"What did you wish?" said Okarun.

"You're not supposed to tell."

"*Momo.*"

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked back up at the sky, at the place where the light had gone.

"That you keep looking for aliens," said Momo. "Forever." A pause. "Because when you look up like that, at the sky, all excited about some stupid rock on fire, you look exactly like you did the first time." She did not look at him. "And I want to keep remembering that face."

Okarun sat back down on the blanket, very slowly, beside her.

He did not say anything for a long time.

Then he found her hand on the blanket between them, the way Bocchi and Kita did now, in front of everyone. And held it.

"My wish," said Okarun, very quietly, "is that you keep believing in ghosts."

"*Why?*"

"So we can keep arguing," said Okarun. "Forever."

Momo laughed. It was small, and surprised, and it went up past the bell into the cold clear sky.

Then she put her head on his shoulder, where Turbo Granny usually sat, and left it there. They sat under the bell for a long time after that, looking up at the Great Saucer and the Tractor Beam and Abduction (Probable). Neither of them said anything at all, and nothing moved overhead again, and neither of them minded.
