# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 24
## Chapter 12 — Stark Picks

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1  
**Volume:** 24  
**Reading order:** 12  
**Continuity state:** Day 138 | Mau counting leaves; Fern asks his advice ('don't tell Frieren-sama') | six plans crossed out: 'Stark picks' | 'You never take any of your own' | the timber yard; Mau told Stark | 'I still want slower... different things' / 'I just want to be the one you're slow with' | the panel: two hands' width

*When: Day 138, afternoon into night, the same night as Okarun and Momo's bell tower. Fern has written the date in the ledger as a scheduled activity and then crossed it out.*

Fern came to Mau for advice, which she had never done, about anything.

She found him in the yard, under the ink tree, in the middle of the afternoon on the hundred-and-thirty-eighth day, doing nothing. He had been told to do nothing. Maomao had said it, and Gojo had said it, and Frieren had said it by putting him on the bench under the tree with a blanket and a cup of tea and sitting on his feet. Frieren had gone in an hour ago to fill Ram. Mau had stayed. He was looking at the leaves.

He was counting them, actually. He had not meant to. He had looked up at the crown of the tree, and his eye had started, and now he knew there were four thousand, one hundred and twelve leaves on the lowest bough alone, and that eleven of them had a small brown spot where the frost had touched them, and he did not know how he knew, and it frightened him.

Fern sat down on the bench beside him.

She had the ledger. She always had the ledger. She held it on her knees, closed, with both hands flat on the cover, and looked straight ahead at the yard, and did not say anything for a long time.

Mau stopped counting.

"Fern?"

"I need to ask you something," said Fern. "And you're not allowed to tell Frieren-sama."

Mau looked at her.

"I'm not allowed to tell Frieren anything?"

"*This*," said Fern. "You're not allowed to tell her *this*." She was very pink. "She'll be insufferable. She'll make that face. The serene one." She stared at the yard. "I know you two don't—have secrets. I know. But just this one. Please."

Mau looked at her a moment longer. At the ledger on her knees, and her hands flat on it, and the pink in her face. At the girl Frieren had raised, who was the most competent person in the house, who ran the List and the coins and the shop's purse and the rationing of potions and every scheduled activity in the settlement. Who had come to him, of all people, under a tree, on a dating day.

"Okay," said Mau. "I won't tell her."

Fern let out a breath.

"I don't know what to do," she said. "For the date."

Mau waited.

"I've written six plans," said Fern. She opened the ledger. There were six pages, each in her small neat hand, each with a heading. *Option A. Option B.* Times. Places. What to bring. A column, on each, headed *Contingency*. "I've timed them. I've costed them. I've checked them against the gate schedule and the washing court and the west wing eave." She closed the ledger. "They're all terrible."

"Why are they terrible?"

"Because they're *plans*," said Fern. "Because I made them. Because they've got contingency columns." She stared at the cover. "Stark doesn't want a contingency column. Stark wants—" She stopped. "I don't know what Stark wants. That's the problem. I always know what everybody wants. I know what the fort wants and what the kitchen needs and how much flour is left. I don't know what Stark wants for a *date*." Her voice went very small. "And I'm frightened that if I plan it, it'll be a scheduled activity. And if I don't plan it, I won't know what to do, and I'll stand there, and it'll be awful."

Mau looked at her for a long time.

He thought about the first time he had ever seen Fern. On the road, a long time ago, before the bridge, before the inn, before anything. A small serious girl walking behind Frieren with a staff and a face that said she was the only adult present. He thought about her treating him like a child when he behaved like one, and being right. He thought about her standing at the head of the long table every morning, holding the house up with a pen.

He thought about the dotted wall between her room and Stark's, and the panel in it, and a sound in the night, a hand's width.

"Don't plan it," said Mau.

Fern looked at him in horror.

"*Don't*—"

"Don't plan it," said Mau. "Give him the day. Let him pick." He looked at the ledger in her lap. "You plan everything, Fern. Every single thing in this house. You hold all of it up, every day, so the rest of us don't have to." He paused. "Let somebody hold something up for you. For one day. Let it be him."

Fern did not say anything.

"He's been waiting," said Mau gently. "For a long time. Since the bracelet. Since before. For you to let him carry something." He looked out at the yard. "He split wood all night behind the timber yard because he couldn't walk into the gate after you. He'd carry anything you let him carry. You never let him."

Fern looked down at the ledger in her lap for a long time.

"What if he picks something stupid?" she said at last, very quietly.

"Then it's stupid," said Mau. "And it's his. And you'll be there." He nudged her shoulder with his. "That's the date, Fern. Not the place. Being there while somebody else is in charge of it."

Fern sat very still.

Then, slowly, she opened the ledger. She found the six pages. *Option A* through *Option F*. She looked at them for a long time.

And then she took her pen, and drew one long straight line through all six of them, one after another, very carefully, the way she crossed things out when they were done.

Under the last one, she wrote: *Stark picks.*

She closed the ledger.

"Thank you," said Fern. She stood up. She did not look at him. "You're not telling Frieren-sama."

"I'm not telling Frieren."

"*Ever.*"

"Ever," said Mau.

Fern nodded once, stiffly, and walked away across the yard with the ledger under her arm. Halfway across, she stopped, and turned round, and looked back at him on the bench under the tree.

"You're good at this," she said. "Advice. You know." She frowned, as if this were a problem. "You never take any of your own."

She went in before he could answer.



Stark picked the timber yard.

He picked it at dinner. He leaned over to Fern at the long table, very quietly, and said it into her ear, and Fern went pink and then red and then nodded, very fast, without looking at him, and went back to her soup.

So at nine o'clock that night, while Okarun and Momo were climbing the bell tower across the plaza, Fern walked round the back of the timber yard behind the workshop in her good coat, the one she kept for occasions. She found the woodpile, and the chopping block, and a lamp hung on a nail, and Stark.

He was sitting on the chopping block. He had brought two cups and a flask and nothing else. No plan. No basket. No contingency column.

"This is where you were," said Fern. "That night. Splitting wood."

"Yeah."

"When you couldn't go in."

"Yeah." He held out a cup. "It's where I come to think. When I can't do anything about something." He looked at the woodpile. "I thought maybe it should be somewhere I'd been unhappy. And then I'd been happy there too. So it'd be both."

Fern took the cup.

She looked at him for a long moment, on the chopping block, in the lamplight. At the axe, the new one, from the shop, leaning against the woodpile, where he could reach it. At the old one beside it. At his hands round his own cup.

"That's a very stupid reason for a date," said Fern.

"Yeah," said Stark. "Mau said you'd say that."

Fern froze.

"*Mau* said—"

"He came and found me," said Stark. "This afternoon. After you." He looked at his cup. "He said you'd crossed out six plans and written *Stark picks* and that if I picked something with a contingency column he'd let Milim drop a beam on me."

Fern stood in the lamplight with her cup in her hand and her mouth open.

"He *promised*," she said. "He promised he wouldn't tell Frieren-sama."

"He didn't tell Frieren," said Stark reasonably. "He told me."

Fern opened her mouth. Then closed it. Then, after a long moment, she sat down on the chopping block beside him, very stiffly, because there was nowhere else to sit, and it was a small chopping block, and so their shoulders touched.

"I'm going to kill him," she said.

"Okay," said Stark.

"Slowly. Over a long time."

"Okay."

She drank her tea. It was very hot. She held the cup in both hands and looked at the woodpile and did not say anything for a while.

"I wanted slower," she said at last. "I said that. On the plan. In the Muslin. I said slower."

"I know," said Stark.

"I still want slower." She did not look at him. "I don't want it to stop. I want it slower. Those are different things." Her voice went very quiet. "I need you to know they're different things."

Stark looked at her for a long time, on the chopping block, in the lamplight, with her good coat and her pink ears and her cup in both hands.

Then he reached over, slowly, and took the cup out of her hands, and set it on the woodpile beside his own. And he took her hand. Just took it. And held it, on the chopping block, between them.

"Slower is fine," said Stark. "Slower is good." He looked at their hands. "I split a whole winter of wood out here, Fern. I can wait." He paused. "I just want to be the one you're slow with."

Fern did not say anything.

She sat beside him on the chopping block with her hand in his and looked at the woodpile, where he had split wood all night once because he could not walk into the gate after her, and her eyes were very bright. She did not cry. Fern did not cry where anyone could see.

But she did not take her hand back. Not for the whole hour. Not when the lamp started to gutter. Not when they heard, very faintly, from across the plaza, the sound of Seiko's voice carrying from the bottom of the bell tower, and then, a little later, Turbo Granny's, under somebody's arm, saying *I was SUPERVISING*.

At ten o'clock, Stark walked her back to the house. At the door of her room, two doors from Frieren's, he said goodnight, and she said goodnight, and he went into his own room next door, on the other side of the dotted wall, and closed his door.

Fern stood in her room in the dark for a long time.

Then she went to the sliding panel in the dotted wall, the one Wakana had made so that it could open, and put her hand on it.

She slid it open. Not a hand's width, this time.

Two.
