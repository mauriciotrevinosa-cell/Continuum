## Chapter 13 — Sixty Years

*When: Day 139, the third dating day, morning. The same day as Maomao and Jinshi's walk and the five's question. Anko has already written up three violations of rule four, all of them Gojo.*

"What do you do," said Yuta, "on a date, when you've already had sixty years?"

He said it to the ceiling, lying on his back on their bed in the room beside the east end, at dawn on the hundred-and-thirty-ninth day, with his hands folded on his chest. Maki was sitting on the edge of the bed pulling her boots on.

"You've asked me that eleven times," said Maki.

"I know."

"Since the decree."

"I know." He looked at the ceiling. "It's just—everyone else is doing first dates. Real ones. Kita and Bocchi with a *song*. Wakana made Marin a *dress*. And we've—" He stopped. "We've had our first date. And our hundredth. And a wedding. And a family. And grandchildren. And—" He stopped again. "I don't know what a date *is*, for us. After all that. In these." He held up his hand, young, with the ring on it. "It feels like cheating."

Maki finished lacing her boot. She stood up. She picked up her spear from where it leaned against the wall, the cursed tool, and looked at him over her shoulder.

"Get up," she said.

"Why?"

"Because I'm taking you on our first date," said Maki. "Again."



She took him to the south field.

He realised where they were going halfway down the slope, and stopped. It was the field. The same field. The place where six days ago he had held his hair up off his forehead and told his teacher everything, and three domains had opened in the snow, and Mau had screamed in Rika's arms. The snow had melted from most of it since then. But there were still marks. A ring of small dark holes in the ground where swords had been. A long gouge where Gojo had gone down on his knees. A patch of earth, near the middle, where nothing would grow for a while.

"Maki," said Yuta.

"I know where we are."

"Why here?"

Maki walked out into the middle of the field, to the patch where nothing would grow, and stopped, and turned round, and planted the butt of her spear in the earth.

"Because you've been avoiding it," she said. "For six days. You walk round it. You don't look at it from the house." She looked at him. "And because it's where we had our first date."

Yuta stared at her.

"We didn't—our first date was in a café. In Tokyo. You hated it. You said the cake was too sweet and the chairs were too small and you threw a—"

"That was our *second* date," said Maki. "Our first date was a training ground. At the school. Before any of it. Before Rika was gone. Before you got strong." She lifted the spear and turned it in her hands. "You asked me to train with you. You were terrified. You could barely hold a sword. And I put you on the ground forty times in an hour." She almost smiled. "And afterward you lay there in the dirt and said *thank you*, like an idiot. And I thought, *oh no*."

Yuta did not say anything.

"That was the first date," said Maki. "You just didn't know it was one. I did." She settled into a stance. "So. Again. Here. In these." She glanced down at her own young hands on the spear. "Where your teacher came at you and ended up on his knees. Where you held your hair up. Where you got your domain back for four seconds and then puked in the snow." She looked at him. "We take it back. We make it ours."

Yuta looked at her for a long time across the field, with the ring of sword-holes round them and the patch of dead earth under her feet.

Then he drew his sword.

"I'm going to put you on the ground," said Yuta.

"You've never once put me on the ground," said Maki.

"I did in the forties."

"You did *once* in the forties. I let you. It was your birthday."

Yuta opened his mouth to argue.

She was already moving.



They sparred for an hour.

It was not like the fight. Nothing about it was like the fight. There was no Infinity, no Void, no wheel. There was a spear and a sword, and two people who had trained together for sixty years in another life and knew every single thing about how the other one moved. And they were finding out, all over again, in these young bodies, what that was like.

Yuta wrapped his blade in cursed energy and came in fast. Maki turned it aside with the haft of the spear without looking, and swept his feet, and he went down.

"One," said Maki.

He got up. He came in again, low this time, the way she had taught him in the second year, in the other life. She knew the way she had taught him. She stepped over it.

He went down.

"Two."

He got up.

She put him on the ground nineteen times. He put her on the ground once, near the end, with a feint he had never used in either life, which she did not see coming. When she was lying on her back in the mud looking up at him, with his sword point at her collarbone and her spear three paces away, she started to laugh.

It was a real laugh. Maki's laugh. Short and rough and surprised, the one she only let out a few times a year, in either life. It went up into the grey morning over the south field and rang off the fence at the top of the slope.

"*Where did you get that*?"

"I just made it up," said Yuta, breathing hard.

"You *made it up*."

"Just now. I thought, she knows everything I do. So I did something I don't do." He lowered the sword. "It was terrible. It shouldn't have worked."

"It was terrible," Maki agreed, from the mud, still laughing. "It shouldn't have worked." She held up her hand. "Help me up."

He took her hand, and pulled her up, and she came up all the way and kept coming, and put her arms round his neck. She was covered in mud. So was he. She kissed him, in the middle of the south field, on the patch of dead earth where nothing would grow for a while, with the ring of sword-holes round them.

"Thank you," said Maki, when she let him go.

"For putting you on the ground?"

"For lying in the dirt and saying thank you," said Maki. "The first time." She held on to him. "Like an idiot."



They sat on the edge of the field afterward, in the mud, with their backs against the fence post at the bottom of the slope, and looked at the field.

It looked different. Yuta could not have said how. It was the same field. The same holes. The same patch of dead earth. But it did not look like the place where his teacher had gone down on his knees any more. It looked like the place where Maki had laughed.

"I'm ready," said Maki.

Yuta looked at her.

She was not looking at him. She was looking at the field, with her arms on her knees and her spear across them.

"For her," said Maki. "Yuka." She said the name carefully. The way you say a name you have not said out loud in a very long time, and in a different life. "I've had the paper in my pocket since the List. *Written. Not yet.*" She took it out. A small square of paper, folded twice, soft at the creases from being carried. She did not open it. "I was waiting. For the house. For it to be safe. For the gate and the road and the devil in the woods." She turned it over in her fingers. "And then your teacher came. And there was a fight. And I thought, it's never going to be safe. Not really. Not here."

"No," said Yuta quietly.

"So I'm not waiting for safe," said Maki. "I'm waiting for ready. And I'm ready." She looked at the paper. "After the dates. After this. When everyone's slept it off." She paused. "I want her to see us young. Before we get any older. Before anything else happens." Her voice went very quiet. "I want her to see you with all your own face."

Yuta did not say anything for a long time.

He reached over and took the folded paper out of her fingers, very gently. He did not open it either. He held it for a moment. Then he put it back in her hand and closed her fingers over it with his own.

"She'll be so loud," he said.

"She was always so loud."

"She'll hate that we're young."

"She'll be *furious*," said Maki, and almost smiled. "She'll say we look like children. She'll say it's embarrassing. She'll say—" She stopped. Her face changed. "She was ten. When you—when the old you went. She sat on the coffin. She said she'd be strong." She looked at the paper in her fist. "I wasn't there. I was already gone. I never saw her grow up."

Yuta put his arm round her.

"You'll see her now," he said.

Maki did not say anything. She leaned against him, in the mud, against the fence post, with the paper in her fist, and looked at the field.



At the top of the slope, behind the fence, someone had been watching.

Not Gojo. Gojo had been caught at the top of the slope at the start of the hour by Anko, who had written him up in her notebook for the fourth violation of rule four that morning and marched him back to the house by the collar. *RULE FOUR. GOJO. AGAIN. (FOURTH TODAY.) (HE SAID HE WAS 'CHECKING THE ARCHITECTURE.')*

Someone else had stayed.

Mai stood at the fence at the top of the slope, alone, with her hands in the pockets of her coat. She had watched the whole hour. Nineteen times on the ground. The feint. The laugh. The kiss in the mud. The paper.

She did not go down. She did not say anything. She stood at the fence and watched her sister sit in the mud at the bottom of the field, with her head on Yuta's shoulder, holding a folded square of paper with a name on it that Mai had heard exactly once, at a low table, over crackers. A name from a life Mai had not been in, because, Mai knew without anyone ever telling her, she had not been there for it. She had stopped, somewhere, before it began.

She did not know how she knew that. She had never asked. Maki had never said.

Mai stood at the fence for a long time.

Then, very quietly, she took her right hand out of her pocket and looked at it. It was not shaking. She had not made a bullet that morning. For the first time since the bridge, she had decided not to.

She put it back in her pocket and walked back up to the house alone, before either of them could look up and see that she had been there.
