*When: Day 139, the third dating day, afternoon. Yuta and Maki came back from the south field at noon covered in mud; the five have been arguing in the big room since lunch.*

Maomao set the terms.

She set them at breakfast, on the hundred-and-thirty-ninth day, without looking up from her porridge, in front of the whole long table, in the flat voice she used for dosages.

"It will be the forest edge," said Maomao. "By the old stand of birch, past the north field, inside the line. I have Daphne's permission. There are roots there I need before the thaw takes them. It will take two hours. You will carry the basket." She ate a spoonful of porridge. "That is the date."

Down the table, Jinshi put his cup down very carefully.

"That's a herb-gathering trip," he said.

"Yes."

"You need the roots anyway."

"Yes."

"So it's not a date. It's work. You've turned the decree into *work*."

Maomao looked up from her porridge at last, and met his eyes across the long table, with an expression of perfect, polite, glacial calm.

"The decree says every couple must go on a date," said Maomao. "It says who plans it is up to the couple. I am planning it." She went back to her porridge. "I did not say you had to come."

The long table went very quiet.

Jinshi looked at her for a long moment.

"I'll carry the basket," he said.

"Good," said Maomao, and did not look up again.

At the arch of the fort, Umaru made a small mark on her slate under *MAOMAO / JINSHI (TEMPORARY)*. It said: *date = WORK?? ministry to review.*



It was work. For about an hour, it was exactly work.

They walked out past the north field, past the red stake, along the edge of the line where Anko's stakes ran, to the old stand of birch at the forest edge, white and bare in the thin light. Maomao walked in front, with a small trowel and a cloth bag and her sleeves tied back. Jinshi walked behind with the basket, which was large and empty and which he carried as if it were a ceremonial object. When they reached the birches, Maomao knelt in the wet earth at the foot of the first one and began to dig.

She did not talk to him. She talked to the roots.

"*There* you are," she said, to something pale and knotted that came out of the ground in her fingers. "Bitter. Good. You'll do for the cough." She put it in the cloth bag. She moved to the next tree. "And you. Don't hide. I can see your leaf." She dug. "Ah. *Ah*. You're the one that stops a heart if you boil you too long." She held it up to the light, delighted. "Beautiful."

Jinshi stood behind her with the basket and watched.

He had watched her do this before. In another world, in a palace, in the gardens, at night, when she thought nobody saw. Kneeling in the dirt with her sleeves tied back, talking to poisons as if they were old friends, with her face doing something it never did in front of anyone, which was light up.

He had never been allowed to stand this close.

"What does that one do?" said Jinshi.

Maomao paused, with the trowel in the earth.

She looked over her shoulder at him, for the first time since they had left the house. Suspiciously. As if he had asked a trick question.

"You don't care what it does," she said.

"I asked."

"People ask to be polite. Then they stop listening at the second sentence." She turned back to the root. "It's a sedative. Mild. In small doses. In large doses it makes your fingers go numb and then your lips and then your heart forgets what it's for." She dug it out. "Daphne says it grows here because the birches like it. I don't believe her. I think it grows here because it likes the birches."

"What's the difference?"

Maomao paused again.

"That," she said slowly, "is actually a good question."

So she told him.

She told him about the sedative, and why it grew by birches, and why she thought Daphne was wrong. Then about the next root, which was for chilblains, and the one after, which looked exactly like the one for chilblains and would kill you in an afternoon. Then about the moss on the north side of the third birch, which was not for anything, but was the most beautiful green she had seen since the palace. And at some point she stopped digging, and sat back on her heels in the wet earth with her hands black to the wrist, and talked. And Jinshi stood behind her with the basket, and listened to every sentence.

Not to the second sentence and then stopped. Every sentence.

She noticed, after a while. She stopped in the middle of a sentence about bark and looked up at him.

"You're still listening," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

Jinshi considered this.

"Because you look happy," he said. "When you talk about them." He shifted the basket. "You don't look happy very often. Where people can see."

Maomao looked at him for a long time, kneeling in the wet earth at the foot of a birch, with her hands black to the wrist and a root in her fingers that could stop a heart.

Then she looked back down at the root.

"Hold the basket lower," she said. "I can't reach."

He held it lower.

She put the root in.



On the way back, the path along the edge of the line was icy, where the shade of the birches had kept the frost. Maomao walked in front, with the cloth bag. Jinshi walked behind, with the basket, which was full now, and heavy.

At the iciest part, Maomao's foot went out from under her.

She did not fall. Her hand shot out sideways and caught something, and held on, and she stayed upright.

The thing she had caught was Jinshi's sleeve.

She stood on the ice for a moment, holding his sleeve, looking at the path.

"The path is icy," said Maomao.

"Yes."

"I am holding your sleeve because the path is icy."

"Yes."

"It is a practical measure."

"Of course," said Jinshi.

She did not let go.

They walked the rest of the icy part like that, slowly, Maomao in front, holding his sleeve, Jinshi behind, with the basket. The icy part was not long. It ended at the red stake, where the north field began, and the path was dry.

Maomao did not let go at the red stake.

She did not let go across the north field, either. Or past the last cottage. Or into the plaza. She walked all the way back to the real house, through the front door, past the vermilion columns, holding Jinshi's sleeve, looking straight ahead, with her face perfectly composed. Jinshi walked behind her with the basket and did not say a single word, in case saying a word made her notice.

At the door of the clinic, she let go.

She took the basket from him without a word and went in and put it on the bench and began unpacking the roots, very fast, with her back to the door.

Jinshi stood in the doorway.

"Thank you," he said. "For the date."

"It was work," said Maomao, not turning round.

"It was a very good date," said Jinshi.

Maomao's hands stopped moving over the roots.

She did not turn round. She did not say anything. But the back of her neck, under her tied-up hair, had gone pink.

Jinshi went away, quietly, before she could change her mind about anything.



That night, very late, Maomao went into her room beside the clinic. Jinshi's bed stood in the far corner, as far from hers as the walls allowed, where she had drawn it herself on the plan. Jinshi was asleep in it. On the strip of paper pinned to the foot of it, in her own small hard handwriting, it said:

*JINSHI (temporary). (renewed).*

She looked at it for a long moment in the dark.

Then she took the pencil from behind her ear and, under *(renewed)*, very small, wrote:

*(renewed again).*

She went to bed. She did not look at the corner again. She fell asleep in about four minutes, which was unusually fast for her, with her hands, which still smelled of birch earth and roots that could stop a heart, folded under her cheek.

In the morning, the fort's slate said, under *date = WORK?? ministry to review*, in Umaru's large crooked capitals:

*REVIEWED. COUNTS. (SHE HELD HIS SLEEVE THE WHOLE WAY HOME. ANKO SAW.)*

And under that, in Anko's small precise hand:

*RULE FOUR. ANKO. (I WAS ON PATROL. IT WAS ON THE PATROL ROUTE.) (IT COUNTS.)*
