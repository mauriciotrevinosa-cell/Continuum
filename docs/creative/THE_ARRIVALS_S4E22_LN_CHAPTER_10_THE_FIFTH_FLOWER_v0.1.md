## Chapter 16 — The Fifth Flower

*When: The night of Day 139, the last night of the decree, into the reset hour of Day 140. The five are in their kotatsu, unscheduled; Eren and Mikasa will walk to the red stake at dawn.*

Frieren planned it, which she never did.

She did not tell him. She told nobody. On the morning of the decree she had read the list, *MAU / FRIEREN*, at the top, in crayon, and looked at it for a long time with her serene face. Then she had gone up to the east room and sat in the reading chair with the grimoire from the mimic on her knees and not opened it, and thought.

She thought for three days. Mau had been in the clinic for the first of them, and on the bench under the ink tree for the second, with orders to do nothing. She had asked whether his head hurt, whether he wanted water, whether he had slept. They had spoken about all those things. Not yet about the field. She wanted to hear his answer when he had enough breath to give her one. And every time Frieren looked at him, sitting under the tree with the blanket over his knees and his black-and-red eye moving very slightly, very fast, reading things nobody else could see, she thought a little harder.

On the evening of the third day, the hundred-and-thirty-ninth, at dusk, she came out to the bench under the ink tree in the orange hoodie and stood in front of him.

"Get up," said Frieren.

"Maomao said—"

"Maomao said do nothing." She held out her hand. "We're going to do nothing. Somewhere else."



She took him to the river path.

He knew where they were going before they got there. Down past the washing court, past the slope where the fort sledded, past the last cottage, onto the path that ran along the river toward the weir, between the dark water and the bare trees. It was not far. It was inside the line. It was the path he had walked back down once, a long time ago, in the ten days. At dusk, alone, with his hair wet from the falls, because he had said he would, and because Gojo, in Yuta's stories, never got to walk back.

He had not been on it since.

Frieren walked beside him, in the hoodie, with her hands in the pocket at the front, which was the only pocket it had. She did not say anything. Neither did he. The river went past in the dusk, loud and grey with the thaw, and the trees were black, and the first stars were coming out over the hills.

Halfway to the weir she stopped, at a place where the bank was flat and the grass was coming through, pale and new, among last year's dead stems.

"Here," she said.

She sat down on the bank, on the new grass, with her knees drawn up. He sat down beside her.

They looked at the river for a while.

"You didn't hear me," Frieren said.

Mau turned toward her.

"In the field. When I called you."

He looked down at his hands. "I don't remember hearing you."

"I know."

The water ran past them. She pulled one sleeve over her fingers, then let it fall back.

"I saw your hands come together," she said. "And I was back there. With Yuta holding me down. Waiting for the light to stop."

Mau did not speak.

"You were standing up this time. You were moving. Everyone could see you." She looked at him. "And I still couldn't reach you."

"I'm sorry."

"I know that too."

She said it without anger. It made him listen harder.

"Yuta was frightened," Mau said, after a while. "Gojo wasn't hearing him. I asked Rimuru to take him out. I thought I could keep Gojo looking at me until they were clear."

"They were clear."

"Yes."

He rubbed his thumb over the side of one knuckle. The skin there was still tender.

"And then I stayed," he said.

Frieren watched his hand.

"I remember the field. Parts of it. But I also remember the street in the Trial. Where I had to find the next answer or die." He stopped rubbing his knuckle. "I wasn't making the difference anymore. I knew what came next, and I kept doing it."

"Even when it hurt you."

"Especially then." He looked at her. "That's what I learned in there. If it hurt, it wasn't finished. There was something else I had to do."

Frieren was quiet for a long time.

"I don't want you to stop helping Yuta," she said. "I don't want him left there."

"Neither do I."

"But when he was safe, you weren't. And you couldn't hear anyone telling you."

Mau nodded. Slowly.

"I wasn't thinking about you having to watch it," he said.

Her mouth moved a little. She looked back at the water before she answered.

"I wasn't only watching. I was trying to get to you."

He put his hand on the grass between them, open. She looked at it, then laid hers in it.

"I can't promise I won't get it wrong again," he said. "I don't know how to promise that honestly."

"Then don't."

He held her hand without tightening it.

"But I can tell Gojo what I remember. And Ciel. Before we do anything like that again. Not wait until I'm in it and hope I know when to stop."

"Tell me too," she said.

"Yes."

"Even if you think it'll frighten me."

He looked at her. "Yes."

She leaned against his shoulder. He stayed still while she found a comfortable place, and then rested his cheek against her hair.

"I wanted this to be a date," she said after a while.

"It can still be one."

"I don't know how to do dates," said Frieren.

Mau looked at her.

"I've been on—" She stopped. She thought about it. "I don't think I've been on any. In a thousand years." She looked at the water. "Himmel asked me once. To a festival. I said I was busy. I wasn't busy. I just didn't understand what he was asking." She was quiet for a moment. "I understand now. It's taken me a long time."

Mau did not say anything.

"So I don't know what you're supposed to do," said Frieren. "On a date. Everyone else did something. Wakana made a dress. Bocchi wrote a song. Okarun drew the stars." She pulled the sleeves of the hoodie down over her hands. "I didn't know what to make. I don't make things. I collect them." She looked at him. "So I thought I'd ask you to make something instead."

"Make what?"

"Flowers," said Frieren.



It was the one thing he could do.

That was what she meant. He understood it at once, sitting on the bank, and his throat went tight. There were so many things in him. The Infinity in its box. The Void. The Shrine. The wheel. The shadow that was a door. A hundred things that were half-built and could not be touched, and things that were only blueprints, and things nobody had a name for. All of them dangerous. All of them sealed. All of them a risk, every time, that might pull something else up behind them.

And the flowers. His own. Made with his own mana, the way he had failed to make them twice in the first weeks of his life, in a field with Frieren beside him, and had finally made, months later, at fourteen minutes into the first controlled trial, with every guardrail holding. The one thing in him that was entirely his, and entirely safe, and had never once frightened anybody.

She kept them. He knew she kept them. Four pressed white flowers, one for each controlled trial, between the pages of the old grimoire, which lived on the shelf in the middle of the floor now.

"Okay," said Mau.

He put his hand flat on the new grass between them.

He did not reach for anything sealed. He did not reach for anything at all. He just let his own mana come, the small warm ordinary amount of it that was his. He let it go down through his palm into the earth the way she had taught him, in the first weeks, very patiently, while he failed.

The grass under his hand moved.

A shoot came up. Pale. Then another. Then a bud, closed tight, green at the edges. Then it opened.

White. Five petals. Small, and plain, and perfect. The same flower he had made at fourteen minutes. The same flower in her grimoire.

Then another, beside it. And another. A small ring of them, round his hand, on the bank of the river in the dusk, white against the new grass.

Frieren looked at them for a long time.

"There," she said quietly. "That's the date."



It was then, looking at the flowers, that he saw it.

Not the flowers. Through the flowers. Into them.

He saw the mana in them. His mana. Running up through each stem like water in a glass tube, and out into each petal, and branching, finer and finer, into veins too small to see with any eye. He saw every vein. He saw how many there were. He saw the exact point in each petal where the mana thinned to nothing and the flower became only a flower. He saw the soil under them, and the water in the soil, and the slow pale threads of root going down. He saw the grain of each grain of earth.

And he could not stop seeing it.

He sat on the bank with his hand on the grass and his eye open, and the world opened under it like a book whose pages were too thin. Every line on every page showing through, all at once.

*Mau,* said Ciel, very quietly. *Mau. Look away.*

He looked away. At the river. That was worse. He saw the water. Every current. Every eddy. The cold of it and the speed of it and the stones under it, the silt carried in it, the exact weight of the water going past him in the dusk, every second, forever.

He looked at the sky. The stars. He saw how far.

He looked at Frieren.

And he saw her.

Not her face. He saw her face too, in the dusk, turned toward him, very still. But he saw past it. Through it. Into the thing that Frieren had kept folded down small inside herself for a thousand years. Her mana. The real amount. The amount she had suppressed for so long, so perfectly, that demons who had lived for centuries had looked straight at her and seen an ordinary mage. The amount he had never seen like this: not released for him, but visible through a suppression she had not lifted.

It was enormous.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. It was a sea. It went down and down and down, further than the river, further than the hills, further than anything, quiet and deep and cold and full of light, folded so small and so tight inside a small elf in an orange hoodie that he had never seen like this—

"Mau," said Frieren.

Her voice had changed.

He blinked. The sea went on behind his eye. He could not stop seeing it.

"You're looking at me," said Frieren, very quietly. "Not at my face."

Mau did not answer. He could not.

"You can see it," said Frieren. "Can't you." It was not a question. Her face had gone very still in the dusk. "All of it. What I keep down."

"Yes," said Mau. His voice did not work properly. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean to. I can't stop it, I'm—it's just *there*, I can see all of it, I'm sorry—"

Frieren did not move.

For a long moment, on the riverbank, with the ring of small white flowers between them, she did not move at all. And he understood, in a way he had not understood before, that he had just seen something she had not chosen to show him. Her suppression had not lifted; his sight had passed through it. That something in him had reached past every wall she had, the way it had reached past Gojo's blindfold, and taken it.

Then Frieren reached over and put her hand over his eyes.

Gently. Her small cold palm, flat across both of them. The way you would cover the eyes of someone who had looked at the sun.

The sea went dark.

He sat on the riverbank with Frieren's hand over his eyes and breathed.

"Don't be sorry," said Frieren quietly. She did not take her hand away. "I don't mind you seeing it." A pause. "I just didn't know you could." Another pause, longer. "You can see through it without me letting it go."

"Is it—was it all right? That I—"

"It's yours now," said Frieren. "If you can see it, it's yours." Her hand stayed over his eyes. "I just need you to tell me. When you can see things you shouldn't. I need you to tell me." Her voice went very quiet. "Because that's not you. That's not the flowers. That's something else."

*She is right,* said Ciel. *That was not you.*

Mau sat very still in the dark behind Frieren's hand.

"Okay," he said. "I'll tell you."

They sat there for a long time, on the riverbank, in the dusk, with her hand over his eyes and the ring of white flowers between them on the new grass. Then she took her hand away, slowly. He kept his eyes on the flowers, only the flowers, only the petals, and did not let them go any deeper. And it held. Just.

She picked one of the flowers. She did not say anything. She opened the front pocket of the hoodie and put it in, carefully, against her.

"The fifth," said Frieren.



They walked back in the dark. He kept his eyes on the path. Only the path.

The common room was still warm when they came in, and nearly empty. The fire in the cold hearth had burned down to embers. Mau sat on the bench in front of it, because his legs were tired, and Frieren sat beside him, and leaned her shoulder against his. They looked at the embers.

Two hundred and eleven.

He did not mean to count them. His eye did it. Two hundred and eleven embers, and the exact heat of each one, and which ones would go out first, and in what order, over the next hour. He knew it the way he knew his own name.

He did not say anything.

Frieren, beside him, felt him go still.

"Mau," she said.

"Two hundred and eleven," said Mau, very quietly. "In the hearth. I counted." He swallowed. "I didn't mean to count."

Frieren did not say anything for a long time.

Then she took his hand. And they sat on the bench in front of the dying fire together until the embers went out. They went in exactly the order he had seen, one by one, and neither of them said anything about it.



At the reset hour, Frieren woke the way she always woke now, an hour before dawn, when his body did.

She put her cold foot against his leg under the blanket. The way she always did.

"One—" she began.

She stopped.

His eyes were open. He was lying on his back, looking at the ceiling, awake, as he was always awake at this hour, waiting for the ceiling to change. That was not what stopped her. What stopped her was his eyes.

Both of them. Not the left one, black and red, the way it had been since the south field. Both. Open, in the grey snow-light from the east window. And they were blue.

Not the blue of Gojo's eyes, which were the colour of the sky on a clear day, high and bright. Darker than that. Deeper. The blue of the sea a long way out from shore, where it goes down further than anyone has ever been, quiet and cold and full of light. An ocean. In both his eyes. Looking at the ceiling.

And under the blue, very faintly, very fast, something was moving. Reading. As if every grain of clay in the ceiling were a word.

Frieren lay very still beside him, with her cold foot against his leg, and the word she had said every morning since the ugly days stopped in her mouth half spoken.

He turned his head on the pillow and looked at her. With the ocean.

"Frieren?" he said. "What's wrong?"

She did not answer.

She could not. She lay there and looked into his eyes, which were the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and the most frightening. And somewhere far down in her, under a thousand years of keeping things small and quiet and folded, the fear that had woken beside him so many mornings found a new shape.

*Ciel,* she thought, though Ciel could not hear her. *Ciel. Something's finished.*

And inside Mau, where only Mau could hear, very quietly, Ciel said:

*It is done building.*
