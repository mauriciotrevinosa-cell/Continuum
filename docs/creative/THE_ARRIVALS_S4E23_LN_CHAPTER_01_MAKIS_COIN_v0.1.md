*When: Day 140, the morning after the reset hour, into the night. The decree is over. Eren and Mikasa came back from the red stake at sunrise; Mikasa is still smiling at the hills.*

The house saw his eyes at breakfast.

He came down the main stair with Frieren beside him, and she had her hand in his, and she was holding it harder than she usually did. He crossed the common room to the long table. Rimuru, at the cold-hearth end, looked up to say *looked, quiet*, and did not say it.

The common room went still the way water goes still before a storm.

Mau stopped at the end of the bench. He knew why. Frieren had told him at the reset hour, lying beside him in the grey light with her cold foot against his leg and the word *one* stuck in her mouth. He had got up and gone to the little window of Senku's glass in the washroom and looked. In the dim light from the snow he had seen them looking back at him.

Blue. Not Gojo's blue. Darker. Deeper. The blue of the sea a long way out.

"Mau," said Rimuru carefully.

"I know," said Mau.

*It finished in the night,* said Ciel, through Rimuru, to the whole table, very quietly. *At the reset hour. What Analyze took from Gojo's eyes. It built them.* A pause. *They are not his. They are not a copy. They are what Analyze and the wheel made from the roadmap. In him.* Another pause. *They see what his see.*

Nobody said anything.

Then Gojo stood up.

He had been eating at the far end, beside Yuta, and he put down his spoon and stood up and walked the length of the long table without a word, past the fort, past the witches, past Seiko on her high seat. He stopped in front of Mau. He was so tall that he had to bend, a little, to look into his face. He did not take the blindfold off. He did not need to.

He looked for a long time.

"Huh," said Gojo, very quietly.

It was not the *huh* from the plaza. It was not bright. There was nothing in it at all.

"How much can you see?" said Gojo.

Mau thought about the river. The stars. The sea inside a small elf in an orange hoodie. Two hundred and eleven embers.

"Everything," said Mau. "If I look." He swallowed. "I can't stop looking."

Gojo did not move.

"Yeah," he said. "That's the problem." He straightened up. He turned round to the long table, to all of them, the whole house staring at the two of them. His voice was light again. Almost. "Okay. Change of plans. Lesson one starts today. Not next week. Today." He looked back at Mau. "Lesson one is *not looking*. It's the hardest one. It took me about ten years." A pause. "We don't have ten years. So we're going to do it fast, and it's going to be awful."

"Gojo-san," said Frieren.

Gojo turned his blindfolded face toward her.

"Can you fix it?" said Frieren. Very quietly. In front of everyone.

Gojo was quiet for a long moment.

"No," he said honestly. "Nobody can fix it. They're his now." He looked at her, small and white-haired and holding Mau's hand too hard. "But I can teach him to live with them. I'm the only person in two worlds who knows how." He paused. "That's not nothing."

Frieren looked at him for a long time.

"No," she agreed. "It's not nothing."



They argued about Maki's coin all morning.

Maki had said it on the south field, in the mud: *after the dates*. The dates were over. She came to the long table after breakfast with the folded paper in her hand and sat down across from Fern, and Fern looked at the paper and then at Mau's eyes and then at Maki, and said nothing for a long time.

"Not now," said Rimuru, gently, from the hearth. "Maki. Not with—"

"It's never going to be *not with*," said Maki.

She said it flatly. Without anger. She put the folded paper on the table in front of her, under her hand.

"I said I'd wait for ready," said Maki. "I'm ready. And then it'll be his eyes. And then it'll be the gate. And then the devil comes back. And then it'll be the road, or the hills, or whatever comes out of them." She looked down the table at Mau. "It's never going to be safe here. It's never going to be *not with*. If I wait for that, I'll wait forever. And she'll be standing in a room in another world for every day I wait." Her voice did not change. "She already lost us once. She sat on a coffin when she was ten. I'm not making her wait longer than she has to for a house that's never going to be quiet."

The long table was silent.

Yuta, beside her, put his hand over hers on the paper.

"I want her here," said Yuta quietly. "Before anything else happens. I want her to see us young."

"I second the motion," said Gojo.

He was beside Yuta again, with his spoon set down.

"I'd like to meet your family. They should get to meet their favourite uncle."

Maki looked at him.

"She decides that."

"Of course," said Gojo. "I'm simply making myself available."

Yuta let out a small breath that almost became a laugh. Gojo's shoulder touched his for a moment, then moved away.

Fern looked at the two of them for a long time. Then she looked at Mau, at the end of the bench, with his ocean eyes fixed very carefully on the grain of the table, because the grain of the table was the safest thing in the room to look at.

"Mau?" said Fern.

He did not look up from the table.

"It's her coin," he said. "And her granddaughter." He swallowed. "And if anything happens, it'll be me. Not her. Not the girl." He finally looked up, at Maki, very carefully, only at her face, not into it. "Bring her. Please. Before I'm worse."

Maki looked at him for a long moment.

"You're not going to be worse," she said.

"You don't know that."

"No," said Maki. "But I'm saying it anyway." She stood up with the paper. "Tonight."



She did it on the roof, at dusk, because that was where Yuta had done his.

Yuta was there. Mai was not. Maki had asked her, quietly, in the corridor, and Mai had looked at her for a long moment and said *no*. Not unkindly. *No. It's yours. I'll be at the step.* And then she had gone to her room, and shut the door, and not come out until dark.

So it was the two of them on the ridge of the real house in the blue dusk, with the gate glowing in the north field and the first stars out and the lamps coming on below. Maki sat with her knees up and her spear across them, the way she had sat at the bottom of the south field in the mud. Yuta sat beside her, close.

She unfolded the paper at last.

Yuta had never seen what was on it. He looked now. It was one word, in Maki's square hard handwriting, written on the fortieth day and carried every day since.

*Yuka.*

Maki looked at it for a long time.

Then she took the coin out of her pocket and closed her hand round it, with the paper still in her other hand. She did not close her eyes either. She looked at the sky over the black hills, the way Yuta had.

She thought of her granddaughter.

Not the coffin. She had not been there for the coffin. She had been gone already, in that life, a few years before. She thought of a small girl with her hair in two pigtails on a beach, screaming *GRANDPAAA* at the top of her lungs and throwing sand at a crab. Of the same girl, a little older, at the kitchen table, demanding to be taught to fight. Of a fist, very small, very fierce, punching the air at a training post. *I'm gonna get stronger and show them.* Of a small warm weight on Maki's lap, on a porch, in the evening, falling asleep against her in the middle of a sentence about how she was going to be the strongest sorcerer in the world, stronger than Grandpa, stronger than *anyone*.

She had not seen her grow up. She had seen her start.

*You are sure? Yuka Okkotsu.*

Maki sat on the ridge of the real house with the coin in her fist.

"Yes," said Maki. "I've been sure since she was born."

The coin went warm. Then warmer.

Then it was gone.

Maki opened her hand. Her palm was empty. She looked at it for a long time. Then she folded the paper with the one word on it, very carefully, along its old creases, and put it back in her pocket, where it had been every day since the fortieth.

Yuta did not say anything. He put his arm round her.

Maki did not lean into it, at first. She sat very straight on the ridge, looking at the sky. Then, after a while, she did. And they sat there, the two of them, in the blue dusk, young, in bodies that had not yet done any of the things they had done, waiting for a granddaughter they had last seen as a child.

"She's going to be so loud," said Yuta.

"She's going to be *furious*," said Maki. "She's going to say we look like children."

"We do look like children."

"She's going to say it's *embarrassing*," said Maki, and laughed, short and rough, and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, once, fast, so that nobody could see. "She's going to be so *embarrassed*."



At the foot of the stair, in the common room, Mau sat on the bench with his eyes shut.

He had been shutting them all afternoon. It was the only thing that worked. Gojo had spent three hours with him in the yard, trying to teach him *not looking*, and it had been as awful as Gojo promised. Mau had a headache that went all the way through to the back of his skull. And the only thing in three hours that had given him any rest at all was this: closing his eyes, and keeping them closed, and not opening them for anything.

Frieren sat beside him with her shoulder against his.

"Is it bad?" she said.

"Only when they're open."

"Then keep them shut."

"I can't keep them shut forever."

"No," said Frieren. "But you can tonight."

So he kept them shut.

And with them shut, in the dark behind his own eyelids, he felt it. Something far down, under everything, behind his breastbone. Turned. Not toward Gojo, this time. Not toward the gate. Toward the roof.

Toward a coin, warm, in Maki's fist. Then not toward the coin. Toward what the coin had reached. Somewhere very far away. In another world. In another time. A girl, standing in a room, and across the room from her, on a cord, something she was not wearing.

A ring.

The same ring. The same ring Yuta wore, on his hand, upstairs on the ridge. Two of the same ring. One here. One coming.

*Mau,* said Ciel, very quietly. *Something in you is reaching. I don't know for what.*

Mau sat on the bench with his eyes shut and his hand in Frieren's.

He did not say anything. But his free hand, on his knee, had closed. As if it were already holding something it had not yet been given.
