## Chapter 18 — Grandpa's Little Girl

*When: Day 141, the morning after Maki's coin. The fort on the step for the fourth time; Mau on the bench in the common room with his eyes shut, because open is worse.*

She came at the eighth bell, with Jinshi's key in the lock, as if she had been waiting politely on the other side of the door for somebody to open it.

The bell in the tower rang once, by itself. Jinshi turned the key. The door of Arrival House swung inward. And from the second witness room at the back, before anyone on the step had even stood up, came a voice. Loud. Young. Furious. With every word in it pitched to carry across a training ground.

"*WHERE AM I? WHAT IS THIS? WHO TOOK MY—oh. Oh, it's back. Okay. Okay. WHERE AM I?*"

On the step, in the middle, Maki closed her eyes.

"That's her," she said.

Yuta was already standing.



She was in the doorway of the second witness room by the time they reached it. A girl of about seventeen, tall for her age, with long dark hair tied back in a tail that had come half loose. She wore a fitted dark top with long sleeves, and a pale coat over it that hung to her knees, and the boots of someone who fought for a living. Her hands were up in front of her, open, in a stance, the way you hold your hands when you have woken up somewhere you did not expect and are going to hit the first thing that moves.

Round her neck, on a cord, outside her coat, hung a ring.

She saw them come in. Yuta first, then Maki behind him, then half the step crowding into the intake hall behind them. Her eyes went from face to face very fast, the way a fighter's do.

Then they stopped on Maki.

And on Yuta.

The stance fell apart.

Her hands dropped. Her mouth opened. Her eyes went very wide and very bright, and she stood in the doorway of the second witness room and stared at the two of them. A young man with dark hair and a ring on his hand and a thin pale scar across his forehead. A young woman with a spear and glasses and a face like a closed door. Both of them about her own age. Both of them looking back at her as if she were the only thing in the room.

"No," said the girl.

"Yuka," said Maki.

"*No*," said the girl again, louder. "No. No way. *No way.*" She pointed at Maki, then at Yuta, then at Maki again, and her finger was shaking. "You look like *children*!"

Behind them, in the intake hall, somebody on the step made a sound that might have been a laugh and might have been crying.

"You're *eighteen*!" said the girl. "You're—you're *my age*! You're not supposed to be my *age*! Granny, you had *grey hair*! You had a *cane*, you hit Tsurugi with it every Sunday, you *never* had—" She stopped. She was looking at Maki's face. "You never had—" Her voice cracked. "This is so *embarrassing*."

Maki started to laugh.

It was the short rough laugh, the one from the bottom of the south field in the mud. It came out of her all at once. And with it, at the same time, without any warning at all, tears. Maki stood in the intake hall of Arrival House with her spear in one hand, laughing and crying at once, in front of everyone. She did not wipe her eyes. She did not turn away. She just stood there.

"I *told* you," she said to Yuta, through it. "I *told* you she'd say it was embarrassing."

The girl stared at her.

"Granny," said the girl. "Are you *crying*?"

"Shut up," said Maki, and crossed the intake hall in three strides, and took her granddaughter by both shoulders, hard, and pulled her in, and held on.



The girl did not hug back at first. She stood rigid in Maki's arms, staring over Maki's shoulder at Yuta. At his face. Young. Dark-haired. With the thin pale scar across his brow.

She was looking at the scar.

Mau did not see this. He was in the common room of the real house, on the bench, with his eyes shut. Frieren told him afterward, at the long table, in a low voice, while Yuka was in the bath. She told him the girl had looked at Yuta's face for a long time over Maki's shoulder, with an expression nobody could read. As if she knew the scar, and did not know the face it was on.

"Grandpa?" said the girl.

Yuta stood in the doorway with his hands at his sides.

"Hi, Yuka," he said.

The girl looked at him. At his eyes. At his mouth. At the scar. She tilted her head, very slightly, the way you tilt your head at a photograph of someone you love, taken long before you were born.

"Your face is different," she said slowly.

Yuta's own face did something.

"I know," he said.

"You had—when I was little, you were old." She was frowning, the frown of someone working something out against her will. "The scar's the same. And when you smile—" She stopped. "But you're younger than I am."

"It's my face," said Yuta quietly. "From before I got old." He swallowed. "The world brought me back like this." He took a step forward. "But it's me. It was always me."

The girl looked at him for a long moment, still in Maki's arms.

Then she put her hand up, slowly, and touched the ring on the cord round her neck. The one that matched the one on his hand.

"You gave me this," she said. "On the beach. When I was six. You said it would watch over me until I was a real sorcerer." Her voice wobbled. "They took it away. After. They gave it to Tsurugi. They said I wasn't suited." She held it tighter. "I got it back. At the end. After everything. I *earned* it back." She looked down at it. "And last night I took it off. For the first time. To fix the cord. It was on the table in my room, across the room from me." She held it tighter. "And then there was a voice. Out of nowhere. And it said a name. Not mine. *Granny's.* *Maki.*" She looked from one of them to the other. "And then I was here. And it was round my neck."

*Continuum,* said Ciel, very quietly, inside Mau, on the bench in the common room, where only he could hear. *It brought the ring. It was not with her. It decided the ring was hers.*

Yuta did not say anything. He crossed the last of the space between them. He put his arms round both of them, Maki and the girl, and held on.

And Yuka Okkotsu, seventeen, from sixty-eight years in the future of a world that was not this one, stood in the intake hall of Arrival House with her grandparents' arms round her. Both of them young. Both of them her age. One of them with the wrong face. And she put her own arms up, slowly, and round them both, and put her face in Maki's shoulder.

"You were *gone*," she said, muffled. "Both of you. You were *gone*. I sat on the—I promised I'd be strong. I *was* strong. I was so *strong*, Grandpa, I fought an *alien*—"

"I know," said Yuta, into her hair. "I know you were."

"You *don't* know, you weren't *there*—"

"No," said Yuta. "But I know."

"You really are Grandpa's little girl," said Maki, very roughly, into her hair.

Yuka made a sound like something breaking, and held on harder.



The register girl wrote it down, when there was a moment to write anything down, with her face pink.

*Yuka Okkotsu. Arrived. By coin. The fourth.*

And in the margin, very small: *(the grandparents cried.)*



There was a moment, later in the morning, that Mau also did not see, and that Frieren told him about afterward, very carefully.

They brought Yuka across the plaza to the real house, with half the step trailing behind. At the door, under the vermilion columns, standing beside Diablo, who had opened it, there was a tall man with white hair and a blindfold, waiting.

Gojo had not come to the step. He had stayed at the house. Nobody knew why. He had said he wanted to be out of the way. He was standing in the doorway with his hands in his pockets as they came up the path.

Yuka stopped dead.

She stopped in the middle of the path with Maki on one side of her and Yuta on the other, and stared at the man in the doorway. At his white hair. At the height of him. At the shape of his jaw under the blindfold. At his mouth, which was smiling very slightly, and then, as she stared, stopped.

The colour went out of her face.

"Gojo?" she whispered.

The plaza went very quiet.

Gojo did not move.

Yuka looked at him. Then at Yuta, beside her, young and dark-haired with the scar. Then back at the tall white-haired man in the doorway. Her hand had gone to the ring on her chest.

"Your teacher?" she asked her grandfather.

Yuta nodded.

Gojo reached up and pulled the blindfold down, round his neck, and let her see his eyes. Blue, like the sky.

"He told you about me?"

"Yes." She looked at Yuta again. "But you were gone. You were already gone before—"

"Yeah," said Gojo, gently. "I missed a lot."

She stood looking at him for a moment longer. Mau would think about it afterward: the dead walking out of family stories, while the grandfather who had told them stood beside her young enough to have been in her class.

"Satoru Gojo," he said, holding out his hand. "Your grandpa's teacher."

Yuka took it. She shook it hard, the way her grandmother shook hands, and did not let go.

"You got him home," she said.

Gojo looked past her at Yuta.

"He got himself home," he said. "Did a lot of the difficult part without me."

"He used to throw me in the sea," she said, still holding on. Her voice wobbled. "On the beach. Every summer. He'd pick me up and throw me, and I'd scream, and he'd laugh when I came up. Really loud."

Gojo looked at the young man beside her. Yuta had turned his face away.

"I'd tell him to do it again," Yuka said.

Gojo was quiet for a long time.

Then he laughed softly, with his hand still in hers.

"Yeah," he said. "That sounds like him."



The last moment was the one Frieren told him most carefully of all.

In the common room, at the long table, when they brought Yuka in to sit down and eat something, she looked down the table at all of them. Wide-eyed. At the slime and the dragon and the elf and the demon and the fort. And then her eyes stopped on someone sitting at the end of a bench, a little apart, the way she always sat at first.

A young woman. Short dark hair. Glasses. A face like a closed door.

A face exactly like Maki's.

Yuka went very still.

"Who's—" she began.

"My sister," said Maki, beside her. Very quietly. "Mai."

Yuka looked at Mai. At the end of the bench. Mai looked back at her, with her flat bored expression, her hands in her lap.

And Frieren, watching, saw something cross the girl's face. Something she knew. A thing from her family's history, maybe. A name on a grave she had been taken to as a child, in a family plot, with Granny Maki standing very still beside it and not saying anything.

Yuka opened her mouth.

Maki, beside her, without looking at her, put her hand on Yuka's knee under the table. Not hard. Just there.

Yuka closed her mouth again.

She looked at Mai for a long moment more. Then she gave her a small, careful nod. The kind you give someone you have heard about all your life and never met.

"Hi," said Yuka.

"Hi," said Mai.

And that was all. That was all, for now.



On the bench in the common room, with his eyes shut, Mau heard all of it. Every word. The laugh in the doorway. *He got himself home.* *He used to throw me in the sea.* The small careful *hi*.

He sat with his eyes shut and Frieren's shoulder against his and listened to the long table make room for one more.

And far down, behind his breastbone, the thing that had been reaching since the roof reached further.

Toward the girl at the long table. Not toward her. Toward what she wore. A ring on a cord, round her neck. The same ring as the one on Yuta's hand, at the far end of the table. Two of one ring, in one room, for the first time in either world.

*Mau,* said Ciel. Her voice had changed. *Mau. Open your eyes. Something is—*

He opened his eyes.

He did not mean to. He did not mean to do anything.

The common room went white at the edges.

And then he was not on the bench any more.
