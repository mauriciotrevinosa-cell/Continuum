*When: Day 145, within the hour after the bell stopped. The fort is still in the refuge cellars; the witches are still standing at the bottom of the slope, because nobody has told them they can stop.*

She came at once.

That was not how it worked. Everyone in the house knew how it worked by now. The holder says *yes*, and the coin goes, and then you wait. A night. A morning. However long the Goddess decides. Seika had come the next day. Ram before dawn. Gojo at seven. Yuka at the eighth bell.

Shoko Ieiri came while they were still carrying him up the slope.

The bell in the tower over Arrival House, which had only just stopped ringing the seal pattern, rang once more. By itself. One deep round note, across the plaza and the yard and the south field. Everyone on the slope stopped.

Jinshi was the one who ran. He had been at the fence with the register girl and the staff of Arrival House, because that was where the protocol put them. And he turned at the sound of the bell, and looked at the empty building across the plaza with its door shut, and ran. Faster than anyone in the house had ever seen him run. He did not wait for the protocol, or for anyone to tell him.

He reached the door. He did not need the key. It was already open.

A woman was standing in the intake hall.

She was tall, and thin, and tired. She wore a long white coat over dark clothes, the kind of coat doctors wore in Yuta's world, open, with the sleeves pushed up. Her hair was brown and fell loose to her shoulders. There were shadows under her eyes so deep they looked painted on. She was holding, in one hand, a cigarette, unlit, halfway to her mouth, as if she had been about to light it in a dark room somewhere and the room had stopped being there.

She looked at Jinshi.

"Where," said the woman, "is he."

It was not a question. Her voice was flat and dry and very steady, and under it, so far down that you would only hear it if you had spent a lifetime in a palace listening for what people did not say, something was shaking.

"Who?" said Jinshi.

"Satoru Gojo," said the woman. "Someone said his name. Out of nowhere. In my office. At four in the morning. Four days after I—" She stopped. The cigarette in her fingers did not move. "Someone said *Satoru*. And I'm here. Wherever here is." She looked at him. "So where is he."

Jinshi looked at her for one second.

"There's a patient," he said. "Dying. Gojo-san called you for him. With a coin. Ten minutes ago." He stepped back from the door. "Gojo-san's with him. They're bringing him to the clinic now."

The woman stood very still.

Then she put the cigarette back in the pocket of her white coat, carefully, the way you put away something you are saving.

"Talk later," said Shoko Ieiri. "Show me the patient."



Gojo was carrying him.

He came through the door of the real house with Mau in his arms, wrapped in the sheet, with the black blindfold still across his eyes and the two thin lines of red still running from under it into his hair. Frieren was at his side with one hand on Mau's face. Maomao was in front, running, clearing the way to the clinic. Rimuru was behind, being half carried himself by Diablo, grey to the lips, blood on his upper lip.

They came down the corridor toward the clinic door.

And there was a woman in a white coat standing outside it.

Gojo stopped.

He stopped in the middle of the corridor with Mau in his arms, and his whole body went still. His eyes, without the blindfold, blue like the sky, went to the woman outside the clinic door. They stayed there.

She looked back at him.

For one moment, nobody in the corridor moved. Not Maomao, at the door. Not Frieren, with her hand on Mau's face. Not Diablo, holding Rimuru up. The tall white-haired man with his blindfold on someone else and his student's blood on his coat. And the tired woman in the white coat who had, four days ago in her own world, stood over his body in two pieces on a metal table and done the last thing a doctor could do for him.

Her face did not change. Not at all.

Then she looked down at the man in his arms.

"Is that the patient?" said Shoko.

Gojo did not answer for a moment.

"Yeah," he said. His voice was not right. "Yeah. That's the patient. He's—his eyes, they're—he's got—"

"Put him down," said Shoko. "In there. On the bed. Now." She turned to Maomao, at the door, who was staring at her. "You're the doctor here?"

"Apothecary," said Maomao.

"Better," said Shoko. "Tell me everything. Fast. I'll do the rest."



They worked for two hours.

Mau did not see any of it. He was not there. He was somewhere very far down, under the new walls, under the filter Ciel had built from the shape behind Gojo's eyes, in the dark behind the blindfold, holding on to the sound of a voice that had stopped talking. And then, after a while, to another voice. A woman's. Flat and dry and very steady, saying things he did not understand. *Pulse. Pressure. His heart's running like he's been sprinting for a week. Give me his hand. No, the other one.*

And then something warm.

It came into him through his hand, which someone was holding very hard. Not warm like Frieren's mana, which was like drinking from a well. Not like Yuta's cursed energy, folded inside out. Something else. Something that took the dark pressure of a curse and turned it round until it was not a curse at all, and put it into him, and *mended*.

Reverse cursed technique. The finished form. Not the scratch-closing light version that was all he had. The real thing. Pouring into him from someone who had spent her whole life doing this, every day, for everyone, without asking where they had been.

He felt his heart slow down.

He felt the lines of red at his temples stop.

He felt, very far away, the woman's voice say: *There. There. He's not going anywhere. Not today.*

And Maomao's voice, closer, very quiet, saying: *How did you—that's—you just put it back. Into him.*

And the woman's voice, tired, saying: *That's all I do. That's all I've ever done. Somebody breaks, and I put it back.*



Frieren sat on a stool beside the bed the whole two hours with his other hand in both of hers and did not say a word.

When it was over, Shoko sat back on the stool, and put her hands on her knees, and looked at the man on the bed. Breathing. Slow. Even. With the black blindfold still across his eyes. She looked at him for a long time.

Then she looked at Frieren, on the stool.

"He'll live," said Shoko. "He'll sleep for a while. A day. Two. His eyes—" She stopped. "I don't know anything about his eyes. I know they're Six Eyes. I know what Six Eyes do to a brain that wasn't born with them. I know someone built something round them to stop them." She looked at the blindfold. "Leave that on. Until he wakes up and someone who knows what they're doing says it can come off."

"Gojo," said Frieren quietly.

"Yeah," said Shoko. "Him."

She stood up.

She stood beside the bed for a moment, looking down at the patient. Then she turned round and went to the door, and opened it, and stepped out into the corridor.



He was there.

Of course he was there. He was sitting on the floor of the corridor with his back against the wall opposite the clinic door, exactly where he had sat all night twelve days ago with Diablo beside him. Diablo was not beside him now. Diablo had carried Rimuru upstairs to the loft above the closet in Mau and Frieren's east room. A small blue slime lay asleep on his own pillow there, snoring. Diablo sat on a stool at the foot of the loft ladder, keeping watch. Gojo was alone. His knees were drawn up and his arms were round them, and his eyes, without the blindfold, were open, blue, staring at the door.

He got up when it opened. Too fast. He nearly fell.

Shoko stood in the doorway and looked at him.

He was very tall, standing. She was not. She had to tilt her head back to look at him, and she did. She looked at all of him. The white hair. The coat, with his student's blood on it. The eyes, with nothing over them. The face that she had last seen four days ago, in her own world, on a metal table, in two pieces, with the eyes closed. She had closed them herself.

"You," said Shoko.

Gojo did not say anything. His mouth moved. Nothing came out.

"You," said Shoko again. Her voice was perfectly flat. Perfectly steady. "You were *dead*. You were dead. I *saw* you. I was the one who—" She stopped. "Four days ago. On a table. In two pieces. I closed your eyes. I wrote the report. I signed it." Her voice did not change. Her face did not change. But something was coming up underneath both of them, very fast, like water under ice. "I went home and I sat in my office for four days with the lights off. With a cigarette I couldn't light because my hands wouldn't stop. And then somebody said your name."

"Shoko—"

"And I'm *here*," said Shoko, over him, "wherever here is. With a slime and a dragon and an elf and a dying boy with your *eyes*. And the first thing they tell me—the *first thing*—is that you called me here with a coin, for him, because he was dying." She took a step toward him. "Because you came here, somewhere new, somewhere you'd never been, somewhere you were *alive* again. And the first thing you did was *fight*."

Gojo did not move.

"You arrived somewhere new," said Shoko, "and the first thing you did was fight."

Her voice broke on the last word.

Not much. Just once. Like ice cracking.

And then she was crying. Shoko Ieiri, who did not cry, who had patched up every sorcerer in Tokyo for years without once crying in front of any of them, was standing in the corridor of a timber house in another world, in her white coat, crying. She did not wipe her eyes. She did not turn away. She stood there with her hands at her sides, looking up at the man who had been dead four days ago, and cried, and kept talking through it, as if talking were the only thing holding her up.

"You *idiot*," said Shoko. "You absolute *idiot*. You couldn't even last a *week*. You couldn't last a *day* without throwing yourself at something. You never could. You never once in your whole stupid *life*—"

Gojo put his arms round her.

He did it carefully. The way you would hold something that had been broken once and mended. He bent, because he was so tall, and put his arms round her, white coat and all, and held on. And she let him. She stood in his arms in the corridor with her face against his bloody coat and went on crying and went on talking, muffled, *idiot, idiot, you absolute idiot*. And he held on and did not say anything at all.

After a long time, she stopped talking.

After a longer time, she stopped crying.

She did not let go.

"Four days," said Shoko, into his coat, very quietly. "I had four days of you being dead."

"I know," said Gojo. "I'm sorry."

"I had it all organised. The grief. I was going to be very good at it."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"I was going to *smoke*," said Shoko, "about it. For about a year."

Gojo laughed. It was wet and cracked and it came out of him all at once, and he put his face down in her hair.

"There's no cigarettes here," he said. "I checked."

Shoko went very still in his arms.

Then she reached into the pocket of her white coat, without letting go of him, and took out the cigarette. The one she had been holding when the room stopped being there. Unlit. A little bent.

She looked at it for a long moment over his shoulder.

"Then I'll save it," she said quietly. "For something worth it."

She put it back in her pocket.



In the clinic, on the bed, under the blindfold, very far down, Mau heard it.

Not the words. The sound. A woman's voice in the corridor, crying, saying *idiot* over and over. And a man's voice, very quietly, saying *I'm sorry*.

*Ciel,* he thought. Very slowly. Very far away. *Who's that?*

*The doctor,* said Ciel. Her voice was almost nothing. She had not rested in a day and a half. *The real one. She put you back.* A pause. *She is shouting at Gojo.*

*Good,* thought Mau.

*Yes,* said Ciel. *I thought so too.*

He felt Frieren's hands around his. He moved one finger against her palm.

"Mau?"

He could hardly get the words out. "Everything is okay."

Her grip tightened.

"Come here," he whispered. "On the bed."

She looked at the space beside him. Then at the doorway. Shoko had come back to check on him; she stood there for a moment, watching his breathing.

"Beside him," she said. "Gently. Keep his chest clear."

Frieren took off her shoes. She climbed in carefully and settled along his side, leaving the blindfold alone. His arm moved a little, trying to reach around her. She brought herself closer so he would not have to lift it.

"You don't have to talk," she said.

"You can." He swallowed. "Tell me again."

"What?"

"The war. Against the mimics."

She looked at his mouth, at the effort it had taken to say that. A small breath left her.

"You know that story."

"Tell me anyway."

She laid her head beside his on the pillow.

"It started," said Frieren, "a long time ago."

Her voice was unsteady at first. Then she found the ruin in the north, and Himmel pulling one boot, and Eisen holding the other. The tower with three mimics and no fourth chest. Her indignation returned very slowly, in the familiar places.

"Who builds a tower like that?" she asked him.

His mouth moved. "A monster."

"A monster," she agreed, softly.

She kept going. Through the centuries of people telling her to stop. Through the ninety-nine percent. Toward the round room where the mimic had finally held a grimoire as well as teeth.

"I was right," she told him. "And eaten. Those aren't opposites."

He made the smallest sound against the pillow. She stayed close enough to hear it.

By the time she reached the secret inside the grimoire, he was asleep. His breathing was slow and even. She finished the story quietly, with his hand still in hers.

The cloth remained over his eyes. The new walls still needed watching. For this little while there was a voice he knew, and someone beside him when he went to sleep. In his shadow, curled up with its white ear listening, lay a black dog with no other name but Ear.
