*When: Days 146 to 148, the days after. Shoko sleeps in the west wing's newest room, finished the week before and kept empty by Rimuru's rule; Wakana is laying out the next empty one. The gate is closed to everyone by Fern's word.*

Fern stopped everything.

She did it at the long table on the morning of the hundred-and-forty-sixth day, with the ledger open and her pen in her hand. Mau was still asleep in the clinic with the blindfold on and Frieren beside him in the bed, awake, holding his hand. Rimuru was still asleep as a slime on his pillow in the loft above Mau and Frieren's east-room closet, snoring, with Diablo keeping watch beside the loft ladder. The house came down to breakfast in ones and twos, grey-faced, not talking, and sat down, and did not eat. And Fern stood up at the head of the table and said it.

"Everything stops," said Fern.

Nobody argued.

"No taming," said Fern. "No more shikigami. Not the black one. Not any." She looked at Yuka, at the end of the bench between her grandparents, with the white dog asleep under the table at her feet. Yuka nodded, fast, without saying anything. "No lessons except Gojo's. The not-looking ones. Only those. Gently." She looked at Gojo, who was sitting very still at the cold-hearth end with no blindfold, because his was still on someone else. He nodded too. "No gate. Not for Mau. Not for a long time. Not until Shoko and Maomao and Ciel all say so." She paused. "And nobody—*nobody*—goes looking for anything Mau's missing. No ink near him. No rings near him." She looked at Okarun. "No Turbo Granny near him."

From Okarun's pocket, very small, Turbo Granny said: *I wasn't going to go NEAR him. It came near ME.*

"I know," said Fern. "That's why." She looked down the long table, at all of them. "Whatever is in him can see what it's missing now. So we don't put it in front of him. We don't ask it to want anything. We don't give it a reason." She closed the ledger. "We stop."

There was a silence.

Then, from the witches' end, Pride spoke. She was sitting very straight, with her hands flat on the table, open. She looked very tired. They all did. They had stood at the bottom of the south field for an hour with the light breaking round them and nobody had told them they could stop.

"May we sit down now?" said Pride.

Fern looked at her.

"You are sitting down," said Fern.

"Yes," said Pride. "We sat down at breakfast. Because breakfast is a thing one sits for." She paused. "But nobody has told us we may stop standing at the bottom of the field." Her hands, flat on the table, were trembling very slightly. "We have been waiting to be told."

Fern looked at the seven of them. At Sekhmet, lying on the bench, with her eyes open. Both of them. Still. At Satella with her card. At Echidna, who had not said a word since the south field, and whose face had not stopped looking like grief.

"You can stop standing," said Fern quietly. "Thank you."

Pride closed her eyes.

"Seven," she said, very softly, and the other six said it after her, one by one, and then, for the first time since the bell, they ate.



Wakana replaced the glass in the east window on the afternoon of the hundred-and-forty-sixth.

He did it alone. Nobody asked him to. He took the cracked pane out of the frame very carefully, the single straight line running across it from corner to corner, and carried it down to the workshop. He put it on the bench. And he did not throw it away. He leaned it against the wall at the back of the workshop, beside Senku's box marked *NOT YET (AGAIN)*, and looked at it for a long time. Then he went back up with a new pane, one Senku had made in the furnace that morning without being asked, and fitted it, and it was clear and whole.

The shelf was already back in the middle of the floor. Frieren had put it there. She had come up from the clinic at midday, for an hour, because Shoko made her. She had stood in the east room and looked at the shelf on its side, and the grimoire and the comb and the cup scattered across the boards, and the four older pressed flowers from the book in the corner where they had slid. She had picked those up first, one by one, then taken the fifth from the hoodie pocket. All five. Then she had stood the shelf up, alone, and pushed it back to the pale patch on the boards where it had stood since moving day, and put the flowers on it.

"Temporarily," she had said, to nobody, and gone back down.

And on the east windowsill, on Eren's stands, there was nothing.

The cars were gone.

Eren noticed. He came up in the afternoon to check the stands, because he checked them, because he had made them. He stood in the doorway of the east room and looked at the empty sill, the two stands with nothing on them and the space between where a grey stone should have been, and his face went very still.

Then Ori came in through the door from her room.

She had something in her arms. She had been holding it since dawn the day before, since an elf carried her down the main stair at the sound of the bell, through the whole hour in the refuge cellar with Umaru's arms round her and the ground shaking, and through the night after. She had not put it down. Not to eat. Not to sleep.

Two small cars. A red and white one and a red one. And between them, a grey stone from a tidepool.

She walked past Eren to the windowsill. She put the red and white car on the left stand. The red one on the right. She put the stone between them, exactly in the middle, where it had always been.

Then she stood there, looking at them, with her hands at her sides.

"I caught them," said Ori. "When the shelf went. I was in my door. I saw them fall." She did not turn round. "I caught them before they hit the floor."

Eren did not say anything.

He came to the sill and stood beside her, and looked at the cars on his stands, and the stone. Then, very carefully, he reached out and moved the left stand a hair's breadth to the left. Measured the gap with his thumb. Moved it back.

"Good catch," said Eren.

Ori did not say anything. But she did not move from the sill for a long time.



The crackers table met that night, though it was not its night.

Nobody called it. Nobody needed to. At midnight on the hundred-and-forty-sixth, Seiko came down the corridor to the clinic with the painted tin under her arm, slowly, the way she walked. Maki behind her with the brazier. Mai with the cushions, four and one very small. And Turbo Granny riding on the lid of the tin, saying nothing at all, for once.

They did not go in. Shoko met them at the clinic door and looked at the tin, and at the four of them, and at the tiny cushion, and did not say anything either. She just nodded, once, and went back inside. And they set the table up on the floor of the corridor, outside the door, where Gojo had sat all night once with Diablo. The brazier. The tin. The cushions. And waited.

Frieren came out after a while.

She had stayed beside him in the clinic bed since he had asked her up the morning before, leaving only when the doctor needed space or made her take a break. She had barely eaten. She had rested her head beside his and listened, without managing proper sleep. She came out into the corridor in the orange hoodie with her hair loose and her hands hanging at her sides, and looked down at the low table on the floor, and the four of them round it, and the empty cushion.

"It's not the night," she said.

"The table came to you," said Seiko. "That's allowed. Once." She opened the tin. "Sit."

Frieren sat.

She did not take a cracker. She did not take the tea Seiko poured. She sat on the cushion with her hands in her lap and looked at the brazier, and her face was perfectly calm, perfectly serene. The way it went when something was very wrong.

Nobody said anything.

Then Maki moved. Round the table, on her knees, to Frieren's cushion. She took Frieren by the shoulders. Gently. And turned her round. And put her face very close.

"Look at me," said Maki.

Frieren looked at her.

"Breathe with me," said Maki. "In. Out. That's all. Just that."

Frieren breathed.

In. And out. And on the third breath out, her face went. All at once. The way a wall goes when the last pin comes out. She did not make a sound. She put her forehead down against Maki's shoulder and shook, and shook, and Maki held her, on her knees on the floor of the clinic corridor, and did not say anything.

Mai, on the next cushion, put her hand flat on Frieren's back. Between her shoulder blades. And left it there.

On the lid of the tin, Turbo Granny looked away, at the wall, very hard, as if the wall were very interesting.

Seiko did not move. She sat at the head of the low table with her fan closed in her lap and watched the elf cry, the way she had watched a great many people cry in a long life, without looking away.

It went on for a long time.

When it was over, Frieren lifted her head. Her face was wet. She did not wipe it.

"I can't carry it," she said. Very quietly. "Any of it. Not tonight."

"Then don't," said Seiko. "Give it to the table." She held out her hand, palm up, over the tin. So did Maki. So did Mai. And on the lid of the tin, after a moment, so did Turbo Granny, one small paw, without looking round.

Frieren looked at the four hands.

She did not put hers on any of them. She put both of hers on all of them, at once, in the middle of the low table, over the tin. And left them there.

Then she went back into the clinic, climbed carefully into the bed beside him, and finally slept properly, with his hand in hers. And the table stayed in the corridor outside the door until dawn, keeping watch, the way the house had once kept watch outside a white wall.



Mau came fully awake on the hundred-and-forty-seventh day, at the reset hour, the way he always woke. The brief words after the treatment returned to him slowly: Frieren beside him, the tower, the mimics. Then he had slept again.

He did not open his eyes. There was something over them. Cloth. Snug. Knotted once at the back of his head. He lay in the clinic bed with the blindfold on and Frieren's hand in his, and did not move, and did not open his eyes under the cloth. Because he remembered. All of it. The walls, beautiful and thin. The wheel. The light. *Let me out.* A voice talking, and talking, about an airport and rice cakes and a boy on a train.

"Don't," said a voice. "Not yet."

Gojo's voice. Close. On the stool in the corner.

"I wasn't going to," said Mau. It came out like gravel.

"Liar. You were thinking about it." The stool creaked. "When you open them, it's going to be different. The slime and his voice built you new walls. With a filter in them. Mine. The shape of mine." A pause. "You're going to open your eyes and look at something, and for the first time it's going to be your choice what you see. Not theirs. Yours." Another pause. "It's going to feel like nothing. That's how you'll know it's working. It'll feel like nothing at all."

Mau lay still.

"Okay," said Gojo, after a while. "Now. Slowly."

Long fingers at the back of his head. The knot loosening. The cloth lifting away.

Mau opened his eyes.

The clinic ceiling. Clay, washed white, with a beam across it.

Just the ceiling. Not every grain of clay. Not the exact weight of the beam, or how long ago the tree it came from had died. Just a ceiling. He could feel, somewhere behind it, very far back, the eyes. Waiting. Ready to pull him deeper into what he looked at, if he let them. But he did not ask. And they did not show him.

He turned his head.

Frieren lay beside him in the clinic bed, awake, looking at him from the other half of the pillow.

He looked at her face. Just her face. Her eyes, green. Her mouth. The tired shadows under her eyes. He did not go through. He did not see the sea. He could have. He knew he could have. He looked at her face, and only her face, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

"Hi," said Mau.

Gojo kept his hand close to the blindfold. He waited until Ciel confirmed the filter was holding before he put it on the stool.

"That's enough for now," he said. "We test again with the doctor. No seeing how far it goes."

Mau nodded. He had wanted the ceiling to be enough. Now that it was, he was afraid to move his eyes away from it.

Frieren did not say anything.

Under the blanket, she put her cold foot against his leg. Gently. The way she did at home.

"One," said Frieren. Her voice was not steady. "Still here."

"Still here," said Mau.

In the corner, Gojo stood up, and folded his blindfold, and put it back on his own face, and went out without a word.



That evening, after Shoko and Maomao had checked him again, they let him go upstairs to the east room. Gojo helped him up the stair; Frieren walked beside them. The blindfold went back on for the journey. He was to rest, with the clinic close and the checks continuing.

Frieren got into their bed beside him. Above the closet, Rimuru was asleep on his own pillow. Through the connecting door, Ori could see that they were back.

The house was different after.

Nobody said so. That was the house's way. But Mau saw it, the way he saw everything now, carefully, only on the surface. He saw it the way you see that a room has been rearranged in the dark.

He saw Ori, at night, in the door between her room and the east room. Not coming in. Standing in the door, in the dark, very still, for a long time, after she thought they were asleep. He did not know what she was doing. Then, on the third night after the bell, he realised. She was counting. His breaths. Under her own. One, two. One, two. Until she was sure. Then she went back to bed.

He saw Umaru, at the toll booth, three times a day, with the fort's roster on a slate. Reading it aloud, under her breath, every name. *Thirty. Thirty. Thirty.* As if one might have gone missing between breakfast and lunch.

He saw Yuta on the roof, every night, not sleeping. Not on roof nights. Every night. And Maki going up every night at midnight with a blanket, and sitting with him until dawn, and neither of them saying anything.

He saw Rimuru.

He found him in his office on the hundred-and-forty-eighth day, in human shape, sitting at the desk with a sheet of paper in front of him covered in lines. Walls. Diagrams of walls. Crossed out and drawn again.

"They were mine," said Rimuru, without looking up. "The walls. The old ones. I built them. With her. After the containment." He put his finger on a line. "They were thin. I knew they were thin. Here. And here. After the south field. After the dog." He crossed the line out. "I knew. And I kept saying *they'll hold*." He did not look up. "They didn't hold."

"They held for months," said Mau.

"They didn't hold *this*."

"They held long enough," said Mau, "for Gojo to get here. And Yuka. And the doctor." He sat down on the edge of the desk. "If they'd broken in the winter, there'd have been nobody who knew what the eyes were. Nobody with a filter. Nobody who could put me back." He looked at the crossed-out lines. "They held exactly as long as they needed to. You built them to hold until help came. And help came."

Rimuru did not say anything for a long time.

Then he put his pen down, and put his face in his hands, and Mau sat on the edge of the desk beside him and did not say anything either.



And he saw Seika.

She was at the window of the common room on the hundred-and-forty-eighth morning, at breakfast, with her arms folded, in the ugly coat, when Momo dropped a pot in the kitchen.

It was the same big iron pot Momo had dropped on the fourteenth day, in the ugly days. It hit the stone floor like a bell.

And the whole long table flinched.

Not Mau. Mau did not flinch, this time. He was looking at his porridge, only his porridge, carefully. Everyone else. Forty people, all at once, turning toward the sound with their hands half raised. Mikasa's hand at her blades. Yuta with Rika's shimmer coming up round him and going down again. Frieren half out of her seat. Milim on her feet. Even the fort, behind its curtain, gone silent. All of them, every one, turning, for one heartbeat, not toward the kitchen, where the sound was.

Toward Mau.

Then the heartbeat passed, and Momo said *sorry, sorry, it slipped*, and everyone sat down and looked at their bowls and nobody said anything.

Seika, at the window, had not moved.

She stood there with her arms folded and her face completely flat, and looked at the long table. At forty people who had turned toward one man at the sound of a pot. Then at the man, at the end of the bench, looking very carefully at his porridge with his ocean eyes, pretending he had not seen them turn.

Mau looked up, once, and met her eyes across the room.

She did not say anything. She did not need to. He remembered what she had said to him on the south veranda, her first day, in the ugly coat. *People look at the person they're scared for.*

She had been here six weeks. She had watched the whole house turn toward him at the sound of a pot.

She understood it now. All of it. He could see that she did.

She looked away first.



On the hundred-and-forty-eighth afternoon, he went to the river.

Alone. Frieren let him. She looked at him for a long moment in the yard and then nodded and went back inside, and he knew what it cost her and did not say anything.

He wanted to see the flowers. That was all. The ring of small white flowers he had made on the riverbank on the last night of the decree, with his own mana, the one safe thing. He wanted to know if they had lived through it. Through the bell and the light and the snow that had fallen all at once.

He walked down past the washing court, past the slope, toward the last cottage, toward the river path.

And at the washing court, the women stopped singing.

He felt it before he saw it. The heat of them, the way he felt everyone now, all turning at once. Six women at the troughs, in the cold, with their sleeves rolled up. They had been singing, badly. They stopped. Two of them stepped back from the troughs. One put her hand on the arm of the woman next to her. Nobody moved toward him. Nobody moved away. They just stood there, with wet hands, very still, watching him come.

At the last cottage, a woman from the refuge put her hand on the shoulder of a child playing in the doorway and drew him back, gently, inside.

At the corner by the river path, Mikasa was standing. She was on watch. She was always on watch now, somewhere, every hour of the day, since the bell. She turned when she felt him coming, the way she turned toward anything coming. And her hand went to her blades. Not drawn. Just there. On the hilts. Ready.

Anko was at the red stake past the river path, on her rounds. She turned too. Her notebook was in one hand. Her other hand was inside her coat.

And Okarun, crossing toward the washing court with a basket of shirts, stopped dead in the path with his free hand clamped over the pocket of his coat. Over the small cat-shaped body inside it. Holding it very still.

Everyone ready.

Mau stopped walking.

He stood in the path between the last cottage and the river, in the thin afternoon light, and looked at them. Only at their faces. Only the surface. Mikasa with her hand on her blades. Anko with her hand in her coat. Okarun with his hand over his pocket. The women at the troughs with their wet hands. The child, drawn back into the doorway.

All of them ready. For him.

Mikasa saw his face.

Her hand came off her blades, very fast, as if they had burned her.

"Mau," she said. "I'm sorry. I didn't—I wasn't—it's just habit, I—"

"Sorry," said Okarun, at the same moment, taking his hand off his pocket. His face had gone red and then white. "Sorry, I wasn't, Granny was just—she's been jumpy, she's been—"

"I'm sorry," said Anko, from the stake, taking her hand out of her coat. Very quietly. "That was—I'm sorry."

At the troughs, one of the women said *sorry* too. Under her breath. Not to him. To the water.

Mau looked at all of them.

"Don't," he said.

They looked at him.

"Don't apologise," said Mau. His voice came out steadier than he expected. "Don't. Any of you." He looked at Mikasa, at her hand, still half raised, empty now. "You saw it. All of you. You were at the fence. Or in the cellars, feeling the ground shake. You heard the bell." He swallowed. "You'd be stupid not to be ready."

Nobody said anything.

"I'd rather you were ready," said Mau. "I'd rather you were ready and wrong a hundred times than not ready once." He looked at the child in the cottage doorway, peering out round his mother's skirt. "That's not something to be sorry for. That's just being smart."

He meant it. He was surprised how much he meant it. It hurt, all through him, a deep dull ache like a bruise. And he meant it anyway.

Mikasa looked at him for a long moment.

Then she put her hand back on her blades. On purpose, this time. Not hiding it. And nodded once.

"Okay," she said. "Ready, then."

"Ready," said Mau.

He walked on, past her, down the river path.

Behind him, after a moment, at the washing court, someone started to sing. Badly. Loudly. Louder than before. The voice of a large red-handed woman, Dessa's friend, at the end of the second trough, who did not look at him once. She sang the whole first verse alone, at the top of her lungs, very badly. Then the others came in, one by one, until the whole washing court was singing the song he had first heard on the eighteenth day, in the ugly days, the one about something he never could make out.

He did not turn round. But he walked a little straighter.



The flowers had lived.

The ring of small white flowers on the riverbank, halfway to the weir, on the new grass. Five petals each. Plain, and small, and perfect. The snow had fallen on them and melted. They were still there, a little bent, all of them but one, the one Frieren had picked.

He sat down on the bank beside them.

He did not look at them too hard. He did not look at the mana running up through the stems or the veins in the petals or the threads of root going down. He looked at them the way you look at flowers. White. Small. His.

He sat there until the light started to go.

Then he walked back up the river path, past Mikasa, who nodded, past the washing court, where they were still singing. Past the last cottage, where the child in the doorway, no longer drawn back, lifted one small hand and waved. Back to the house, where Frieren was standing at the door under the vermilion columns, waiting, with her arms folded inside the sleeves of the orange hoodie, pretending she had not been standing there the whole time.

"Well?" she said.

"They lived," said Mau.

Frieren did not say anything. She took his hand, and they went in.
