*When: The night of Day 149, and the morning after. The house has started, very carefully, to sound like itself again; Gojo's lessons are back to two hours a day, gently, and Mau is less terrible.*

She fell asleep on him in the reading chair.

It was not planned. Nothing about it was planned. It was the night of the hundred-and-forty-ninth day, four days after the bell, and the east room was warm, and the new glass in the east window was clear and black with the night behind it. Mau was in the reading chair, the one he had said no to, with a blanket over his knees. Frieren had climbed into it with him at some point after ten, without asking, the way she did, with the grimoire from the mimic, and settled sideways across his lap with her back against the arm and her head on his shoulder, and opened the book.

She was reading the frost spell again. She read it most nights now. He did not know why, and he did not look to find out.

He held the lamp. Out of habit. She had not decreed it. He just held it.

At some point after midnight, she stopped turning pages.

He looked down. Only at her face. Her eyes were closed. Her mouth was a little open. The grimoire had slid down her chest and lay open under one hand. She was asleep, all at once, the way she fell asleep when she had not slept properly for four days. And she had not, not since the bell. He knew. He had felt her lying awake beside him every night, listening to him breathe, the way Ori listened from the door.

He did not move. He put the lamp down, very slowly, on the shelf beside the chair, the one that was not the shelf in the middle of the floor. He put his arm round her. He leaned his head back against the chair.

He did not mean to fall asleep. He did.



She woke at the reset hour.

Mau did not know that at first. He was asleep. He knew afterward, when she told him, in a very small voice, in the grey light, with her face against his chest.

She woke the way she always woke now, an hour before dawn. All at once. Because some part of her was always listening for that hour.

And her face was wet.

Her cheek. Where it lay against his shoulder, against his shirt. Wet. Warm. Spreading.

And for one second, half asleep, in the dark, she was not in the reading chair in the east room of the real house. She was somewhere else. She was on the dead earth of the south field, with her forehead against a black blindfold, and two thin lines of red running out from under it into his hair. And her hands were wet with it. And it was warm. And it would not stop.

She made a sound.

Mau woke to it.

He had never heard her make that sound. Not in the south field. Not at the fence. Not in the plaza, or the Trial, or the reversion night, or any of it. It was not a scream. It was smaller than a scream and much worse. It was the sound of someone who has been holding something down for a thousand years and has just, in the dark, half asleep, felt it come up all at once and not been able to stop it.

She was off his lap. On the floor. On her knees, on the boards in front of the reading chair, with her hands up in front of her face. Looking at them. Turning them over. In the dark. Looking for red.

"*Frieren*—"

"It's wet," said Frieren. Her voice was wrong. High and fast and not hers. "It's wet, your shoulder, it's—where is it coming from, where is it, Mau, where is it coming *from*—"

She had her hands on his face. On his eyes. Feeling under them, at the corners, at his temples, in his hair. Looking for the lines. For the red. Her fingers were shaking so hard he could feel them rattle against his skin.

"Frieren. Frieren. Look at me."

"Your eyes, your—is it your eyes again, is it coming from under, is—"

"*Frieren.*"

He took her hands. Both of them. Off his face. He held them in his, and they shook, and he held on.

"It's not blood," said Mau.

She stared at him in the dark.

"It's not blood," he said again, very gently. "Feel." He turned her hand over, the one that had been on his cheek, and put it to her own face, to her own cheek, where it was wet. Then to his shoulder. To the damp patch on his shirt. "It's not warm any more. Feel it. It's cold. It's just—" He stopped. He almost laughed. He did not. It was not funny. It was the least funny thing in either world. "It's me. I fell asleep with my mouth open. It's—I drooled on you. That's all. That's all it is."

Frieren did not move.

She knelt on the floor in front of the reading chair in the dark with her hand on the damp patch of his shirt, and looked at it. At her own fingers on it. At the way it was cold. Not warm. Not spreading. Not red. Just damp. Just a man who had fallen asleep with his mouth open and drooled on the person asleep on his shoulder.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then her face crumpled.

She did not cry the way Shoko had cried in the corridor. She did not make any sound at all. Her face just went, all at once, the way a wall goes when the last pin comes out. And she put it down against his knees, where she knelt on the floor, with her hands still fisted in his shirt, and shook.

Mau slid down out of the chair onto the floor beside her and put his arms round her.

He did not say anything. He did not say *it's all right*, because it was not. He did not say *it's just drool*, because she knew that now, and it did not matter. He held her on the floor of the east room in the dark, with the reading chair behind them and the shelf in the middle of the floor and the cars on the sill. He did not let go. And she shook, and shook, and did not make a sound.

After a long time, she stopped shaking.

After a longer time, she said, into his chest, so quietly that he almost did not hear:

"I thought it was coming from your eyes."

"I know."

"I thought it was the field. I thought I was in the field again. I thought—" She stopped. "I could feel it on my hands. Warm. I could feel it on my *hands*, Mau."

"I know," he said. "I know. I'm sorry."

"Don't," said Frieren. Very fast. Fierce. "Don't be sorry. You drooled. You don't get to be sorry for drooling." Her hands were still fisted in his shirt. "I just—" She stopped again. "I just need to know where it's coming from. When something's wet. I need to *know*."

"It'll be drool," said Mau. "Nine times in ten. I drool. I didn't know. Apparently I drool."

Frieren made a sound against his chest. It was not quite a laugh. It was the place a laugh would go, a long time from now.

"You do," she said. "You always have. I've just never—" She stopped. "I never minded before."

At the reset hour, on the floor of the east room, she put her cold foot against his leg.

"One," said Frieren. Her voice was almost steady. "Still here."

"Still here," said Mau.

Through the door to Ori's room, which was open a crack, very faintly, someone who had been standing in it in the dark let out a long breath, and went back to bed.



In the morning, Frieren went to find Himmel.

She did not tell Mau. She did not tell anyone. She came down the main stair at dawn, while he was still asleep, at last, properly, in the bed, and went out through the front door under the vermilion columns. Himmel was there. Of course he was there. He was always there at dawn, at the gate, between his two red columns, looking at the road. Because he faced the road. Because that was where heroes faced.

He turned when he heard her. He smiled. Then he saw her face, and stopped smiling.

"Frieren," he said.

She came and stood beside him, between the columns, and looked at the road too. It went away north between the fields, pale in the dawn, toward the river and the hills.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"You went in front," said Frieren.

Himmel looked at her.

"Always," said Frieren. She did not look at him. She looked at the road. "On the journey. Every fight. Every dungeon. Every dragon. Every demon. Every time something came out of the dark, you were already in front of it before anyone else had moved." Her voice was very quiet. "For ten years. I watched you do it for ten years."

Himmel did not say anything.

"I thought I didn't mind," said Frieren. "At the time. I thought, he's the hero. That's what heroes do. They go in front." She folded her arms inside the sleeves of the hoodie. "I didn't understand what it was. To watch someone do that. Every time. And know that one time, they'd be in front of something they couldn't stop." A pause. "I understood after. When you were gone. I understood all of it at once. Fifty years too late."

"Frieren," said Himmel gently.

"He does it too," said Frieren. "Mau. He always goes in front. Every time. The plaza. The south field. Yuta. The dog. Every time something comes, he's already in front of it, before he's thought. Before anyone can stop him." Her voice did not change. "And this time it was *him*. The thing that came out was *him*. And he still went in front of it. He lay in the middle of the field with his fingers in the dirt, holding on, so it wouldn't get past him to us." She stopped. "I watched. From the fence. Milim held my wrist. And I watched him go in front of himself."

Himmel did not say anything for a long time.

"I can't stop him," said Frieren. "I've tried. For two years. I've tried everything." She looked at the road. "I want to. I want to so much I can't breathe. I want to tie him to the bed. I want to stand in front of *him*, for once." Her voice went very quiet. "And I can't. Because if he didn't go in front—"

She did not finish.

"Then who would?" said Himmel.

Frieren closed her eyes.

"Yes," she said.

They stood between the red columns in the dawn, looking at the road.

"I used to think about that," said Himmel, after a while. "On the journey. At night. When you were all asleep." He was not smiling. He was looking at the road the way he looked at it every morning, as if something might come up it. "I used to think: if I don't go in front, who will? Heiter would. He'd go in front with a bottle in one hand and a prayer in the other and get himself killed. Eisen would. He'd go in front and stand there like a mountain until something broke him." He paused. "You wouldn't. You'd stand at the back and kill everything that came, and never go in front of anything, because you were very old and very wise and you knew better." He almost smiled. "So it had to be me."

"I know," said Frieren.

"I didn't do it because I was brave," said Himmel. "I was terrified. Every time. I did it because I couldn't stand the thought of watching someone else do it." He looked at her. "That's the secret. That's the only secret heroes have. We go in front because we can't bear to watch from behind." He was quiet for a moment. "And then we never think about the people who have to watch *us*."

Frieren did not say anything.

"He thinks about you," said Himmel. "I've watched him. Two years. He thinks about you every time. He thinks about you while he's going in front." He paused. "I never did. Not properly. Not until much too late." He looked back at the road. "He does. That's the difference. He goes in front and he knows exactly what it costs the person at the fence. And he goes anyway."

"That's worse," said Frieren.

"Yes," said Himmel. "That's much worse. That's much braver."

She stood beside him in the dawn, between the columns he had asked for and Marin had painted red, so that anyone coming up the road would know at once that this was a place where great things happened. She looked at the road for a long time.

"I hate it," said Frieren quietly.

"I know."

"I hate it so much."

"I know," said Himmel. "So did Heiter. So did Eisen. So did you, I think, after." He did not look at her. "And you'll go on hating it. Every time. For as long as he lives. And you'll stand at the fence anyway. Because if you didn't, there'd be nobody at the fence for him to come back to." He paused. "That's what the people at the back do. That's your job. It's the hardest one there is."

Frieren did not say anything.

She stood between the red columns with her arms folded inside the sleeves of the orange hoodie, and looked at the road. And after a while she leaned, very slightly, sideways. Against Himmel's shoulder. The way she leaned against Mau's. The way she had never once leaned against Himmel's, in ten years on the road, when he was alive the first time.

Himmel went very still.

He did not say anything. He did not put his arm round her. He did not make it into anything. He stood there, the hero, facing the road, with the elf he had loved for a whole lifetime leaning on his shoulder for the first time, and let her.

"Thank you," said Frieren, after a long time.

"For what?"

"For going in front," said Frieren. "Back then. Every time." She straightened up. "I never said it."

Himmel looked at the road for a moment longer.

"You just did," he said quietly.

She went back inside, up the main stair, to the east room. Mau was still asleep in the bed with his mouth a little open. She climbed in beside him in the orange hoodie, and put her head on his shoulder, on the damp patch, and did not mind at all.
