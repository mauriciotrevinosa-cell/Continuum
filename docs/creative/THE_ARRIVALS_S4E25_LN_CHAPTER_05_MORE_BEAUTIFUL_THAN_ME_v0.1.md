*When: The evening of Day 151. Diablo has a sash now, ASSISTANT TO EVERYONE (OFFICIAL), and is wearing it with enormous dignity; the fort has scheduled a meeting it has not told anyone about.*

They were in the common room. That was the mistake.

It was the hundred-and-fifty-first evening, after dinner, and the long table had emptied into its usual corners. Nijika and Ryo were in the music room. The witches had gone to their north wing. Gojo and Shoko had gone out to the yard and were standing under the ink tree, arguing about something in low voices that made Shoko laugh once, short and surprised, and then stop. Seiko was asleep on her high seat with her fan open on her chest. The lamps were low. The fire in the cold hearth was red.

And Mau was on the bench by the hearth, with his back against the wall, and Frieren was on top of him.

Not in any way that meant anything. She had come and sat on the bench beside him after dinner with the grimoire from the mimic, and then, at some point, she had shifted. Then shifted again. Until she was lying along the bench with her head on his chest and her feet up on the far arm and the grimoire open on her stomach, reading. And his arm was round her. And neither of them had noticed that they were not in the east room, with the door shut, where they usually did this. They were in the common room, in front of the hearth, where anyone could see.

They had both forgotten, for the first time since the bell, to be careful about anything.

Frieren turned a page.

Then she stopped. She looked up at him, from his chest, with her head tipped back.

"Mau."

"Mm."

"Can I try something?" She closed the grimoire on one finger. "With your eyes."

He looked down at her. Only at her face. Carefully.

"What kind of something?"

"A spell," said Frieren. "A small one. A light." She held up one finger. "Very bright. Very close. Right in front of your eyes." She tilted her head. "If the filter isn't working, it'll hurt. The eyes will try to read all of it at once, and it'll be too much, and you'll feel it." A pause. "If the filter's working, it won't."

Mau looked at her for a long moment.

"You want to test Gojo's filter," he said. "With a spell. On my eyes. In the common room."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Frieren did not answer at once. She looked at his eyes. At the blue of them. The ocean.

"Because I need to know," she said quietly. "I need to know it holds. I can't ask Ciel every hour. I can't ask Gojo." She turned the grimoire over in her hands. "I need to see it myself. Once."

Mau looked at her face. Only her face.

"Okay," he said.



She held up one finger in front of his eyes. Close. A hand's breadth away.

Her lips moved.

A light came on at the tip of her finger.

It was small. The size of a seed. And it was bright. Brighter than the lamps, brighter than the fire, brighter than anything in the room. It was a point of pure white, so bright that the common room round it seemed to go dim. It hung at the end of Frieren's finger a hand's breadth from his eyes, and lit his face white, and every shadow in the room jumped.

Mau looked at it.

He felt the eyes, far back, behind the filter, wake up. Turn toward it. Ready. Ready to read it. Every particle of mana in it. The exact way Frieren had shaped it. The precise brightness, down to the last fraction. They were ready to take all of it in at once, the way they had taken in the river and the stars and the sea inside her.

He did not let them.

He did not have to *not* let them. That was the thing. He just did not ask. He looked at the light the way you look at a light. Bright. White. At the end of her finger.

And the eyes stayed where they were. Behind the filter. Waiting.

It did not hurt.

It did not hurt at all.

Frieren was watching his face. Very closely. Without moving the light.

"Well?" she said.

"Nothing," said Mau. His voice came out strange. "It feels like nothing. It's just a light."

Frieren did not say anything for a moment.

She looked at him. Not at the light. At his eyes, with the light in them. The tiny white point reflected in both of them, in the blue. In the dark blue. The blue of the sea a long way out from shore, with a single white star floating on it.

She looked for a long time.

Then she let the light go out. The common room went back to lamp-colour and fire-colour. And she did not take her hand away. She put it, very lightly, on his cheek, just under his eye. And looked into them.

"It's beautiful," said Frieren.

Mau looked down at her, lying on his chest, with her hand on his face, looking into his eyes.

"More than me?" he said.

Frieren did not answer at once.

She looked at him. At the ocean in his eyes. At the face round them. At the whole of him, lying on a bench in front of the hearth with his arm round her, alive. Six days after she had knelt on the dead earth with her forehead against a black blindfold and his blood on her hands.

"No," said Frieren.

Mau went still.

"No," said Frieren again, very quietly. "Because they're yours." Her thumb moved, very slightly, under his eye. "They're beautiful because they're yours. They're the most beautiful thing I've ever seen because they're in your face." She did not look away. "You're the most beautiful thing. Not them. You."

The common room was very quiet.

Mau did not say anything. He could not.

Frieren looked at him a moment longer, with her hand on his face. Then, slowly, with her serene expression, she turned her head on his chest.

To look at the room.

And stopped.



They were all there.

Not in the common room. Behind it. In the arch of FORT (EXTENSION), which was a bay off the north end of the common room, with its curtain drawn. Behind the curtain, which was not quite closed, which was open about a hand's width, there were faces.

A great many faces.

The fort. All thirty of them. In hoods. Crammed into the toll booth end of the fort in a heap, on cushions, on each other, on the toll booth platform. Umaru at the front, in her sash, with a slate in her hands. A slate that said, Mau could read it from the bench, in large crooked capitals:

*EMERGENCY MEETING: THE LOVEBIRDS (OUTSIDE THEIR NEST).*

And in the middle of the heap, sitting on the toll booth platform in human shape with a bowl in his lap, with his eyes closed and his head tilted as if listening to something very far away, was Rimuru.

Rimuru opened his eyes.

He looked through the gap in the curtain at the two of them on the bench by the hearth. At Frieren, with her head turned, staring at him. At Mau, frozen, with his arm still round her.

"—*you're the most beautiful thing,*" said Rimuru, very quietly, in a perfect imitation of Frieren's voice, to the fort, as if finishing a sentence. "*Not them. You.*" He paused. "And then she turned round." Another pause. "And she's looking at us." A longer pause. "Agent Bird, signing off."

There was a silence.

Then the fort, all thirty of it, made a sound. A single enormous involuntary sound, like a kettle the size of a house reaching the boil all at once. *EEEEEEEEEE.*

The curtain fell down.

The whole fort was visible now, in a heap on the cushions, thirty faces looking out at the bench by the hearth. Umaru was holding up her slate like a shield. Coco and Agott and Tetia and Richeh were in a pile under it, all pink. Suika had her hands over her mouth. Ori, at the edge, was looking at the ceiling with an expression of profound pain, as if she had told them this was a bad idea, many times, and been overruled. Mai, at the very back, had her face in her hands. Vamola was eating something.

And in the kitchen doorway, behind the long table, Momo was standing with Okarun and Aira and Kita and Bocchi, all of whom had very clearly been standing there for some time. Momo had a wooden spoon in her hand and was holding it in the air, forgotten, with soup dripping off the end of it.

Frieren lay on Mau's chest on the bench by the hearth and looked at all of them.

She went red.

Not pink. Not the faint pink at the tips of her ears that was the most anyone usually saw. Red. From the collar of the orange hoodie to the roots of her white hair, all at once, completely, like a lamp turned all the way up.

"*How long,*" said Frieren, very quietly, "have you been there."

"Since the spell," said Umaru, from behind her slate. Her voice was very high. "We were having a meeting. About you two. Because you were outside your nest." She lowered the slate an inch. "Your *nest* is the east room. You never do this outside the nest. It was an emergency."

"Nobody wanted to look," said Suika, through her hands. "In case you stopped. Like birds. If you look at birds they fly away."

"So we used Agent Bird," said Umaru.

Everyone looked at Rimuru.

Rimuru, on the toll booth platform, with his bowl, looked very slightly guilty.

"The thread," he said. "From the gate. In Mau's shadow." He gestured at the bench, at Mau's shadow on the floor in front of the hearth, thin and grey in the firelight. "I put it in on the second week. For the runs. Nobody ever took it out." He paused. "Ear sniffed it, after the taming. And let it stay." He looked at Frieren, scarlet on the bench. "I can see through it. Everything his shadow sees. So I was—" He stopped. "Narrating."

"Narrating," said Frieren.

"Quietly."

"*Narrating.*"

"Very quietly," said Rimuru. "Like a nature documentary. In my old world. About birds." He looked down at his bowl. "I'm sorry. The fort voted."

"Thirty to nothing," said Umaru, faintly.

"*Ori didn't vote,*" said Ori, from the edge, to the ceiling.

"Twenty-nine to nothing," said Umaru. "And one abstention. In protest."

Frieren lay there on the bench, scarlet, looking at all of them. At the fort. At Rimuru with his bowl. At the kitchen doorway, where Momo had at last noticed the soup dripping off her spoon and was looking at it in horror.

Then, very slowly, she turned her face back round, and pressed it into Mau's chest, and stayed there.

"Mau," she said, muffled.

"Yeah," said Mau.

"I'm going to Zoltraak the fort."

"Okay," said Mau.

"Slowly. Over a long time."

"Okay."

She did not move. She lay on his chest with her face pressed into his shirt, scarlet to the ears. He could feel her heart going very fast against him. And, very faintly, so faintly that only he could have felt it, he could feel her shaking. Not crying. The other thing. The thing she almost never did, where anyone could see.

She was laughing.

Mau put his arm back round her, in front of the whole fort and half the kitchen, and did not take it away.

At the arch of the fort, Umaru was writing on her slate in coloured chalk, very fast, with her tongue between her teeth. When she held it up, it said:

*MEETING CONCLUDED. FINDINGS: THE LOVEBIRDS ARE OKAY.*

And under that, smaller:

*(THEY'RE MORE THAN OKAY.)*

Out in the yard, under the ink tree, Gojo and Shoko had heard the fort's *EEEEEEEEEE* through the wall. Gojo was laughing so hard he had to hold on to the bench. Shoko stood with her arms folded in her white coat, looking at the lit window of the common room, where you could just see, if you looked, two shapes on a bench by the hearth and thirty more in a heap behind a fallen curtain.

"This house," said Shoko, "is insane."

"I know," said Gojo, wiping his eyes. "Isn't it great?"

Shoko did not answer for a long moment.

"Yeah," she said at last, very quietly, as if admitting to something. "It's pretty great."
