*When: The night of Day 152, a week after the bell. The fort has been forgiven, mostly; Frieren has not yet said by how much. Far to the east, past the hills where the farmers keep seeing lights, it is the same night.*

Gojo went out to the south field alone at midnight, because he could not sleep, and because something had been bothering him for a week.

He stood in the middle of the field, on the patch of dead earth where nothing would grow for a while. Where Maki had laughed, and Ear had been named, and a man had lain on his back with his fingers in the dirt while everything in him came out at once. The field was dark. The snow was gone from most of it. The stars were very bright over the black hills, the way they were bright here and never were at home.

He took off his blindfold and looked at his own hand.

A week ago, kneeling here, with Mau's head in one palm and the wheel turning overhead, he had felt something. In the middle of it all. When he took the blindfold off and looked straight into the light pouring out of a man on the ground, and bore it. Something in himself had moved. Not much. A hair. A crack. The way ice moves on a pond in the first warm week, before anyone can see it.

He had not had time to think about it then. He was thinking about it now.

He held up his hand.

He reached for Blue.

Blue had worked on the day the snowball hung in front of his coat. It had worked in the fight too. Then Rika and Maki had stopped them, and Continuum had finally finished measuring him. After that, reaching for it had been like a cough caught in the throat. Infinity, and nothing else. Not yet.

He reached for it now.

And it came.

Not much. Not the way it used to. Not the great inward-falling pull that could drag a building down into a point. A small one. A marble of blue, the size of a cherry, hanging over his palm in the dark, pulling very gently at the dead grass round his feet.

Gojo looked at it for a long time.

"Huh," he said, very quietly.

*More, as the world grows,* Ciel had said, explaining Continuum to him in the snow. *More, as the threat grows.*

He thought about a week ago. The wheel. The light. *Let me out.* The thing in Mau's chest that could see everything it was missing and had gone to get it.

He supposed that counted as a threat.

He let the Blue go. It went out like a candle.



And then he did something he had never done.

He had been the strongest for his whole life. Since he was a baby. He had never once had to reach for anything. Everything had always just been there, all of it, the whole of Limitless, open, waiting, the moment he wanted it. He had never had to *look* for more, because there had never been any more. He had been the ceiling. Everyone else was underneath.

He stood in the dark in the south field and closed his eyes and looked.

Inside. At Limitless. At the technique he had been born with and had used every day of his life. At its shape. Infinity. Blue. Red. Purple, when you put the two together. Unlimited Void. All of it. Familiar as his own hand.

And past it.

There was something past it.

He had never seen it. In his own world, he had never once looked. He had never needed to. But he was looking now, with eyes that had just watched a man's whole body try to become everything at once. And there, past the edge of everything he had ever known Limitless could do, was a direction.

Not a technique. Not yet. A direction. A way the thing he had been born with could go, that it had never gone. Like a road on a map that ran off the edge of the paper. Like a door at the end of a corridor in a house you had lived in all your life, which you had always somehow assumed was a wall.

He stood very still.

It was not closed. That was the thing. It was not locked. In his own world, he understood now, it had never been locked either. He had simply never looked, because he was already the strongest, and the strongest does not look for doors.

He opened his eyes.

He was laughing. He did not know when he had started. He was standing alone in the middle of the south field at midnight, with his blindfold in his hand and his blue eyes open, laughing at the stars. Not loudly. Quietly. Helplessly. The way you laugh when you find out something enormous and terrifying about yourself and the only possible response is to laugh.

"*There's more,*" said Gojo, to the dark field, to nobody. "There's *more*. There was always more." He put his hand over his face. "I never looked. I never once looked."



"You're glowing," said a voice.

He turned round.

Shoko was sitting on the fence at the top of the slope. On the top rail, where the house had stood and watched a week ago, where Milim had held a wrist. She was in her white coat, with her knees drawn up, and the unlit cigarette between two fingers. She had clearly been there for some time.

"I'm not glowing," said Gojo.

"You're a bit glowing," said Shoko. "Round the edges. Like a lamp in fog." She looked at him with her tired eyes. "What did you do?"

He walked up the slope to the fence and stood in front of her.

"I found a door," he said.

Shoko looked at him for a long moment.

"Don't," she said.

"Too late."

"Satoru."

"There's more," said Gojo. "Limitless. There's more of it. There was always more. Past the edge. I never looked, because I didn't need to. Because I was already—" He stopped. "This place lets you grow. Ciel said. The world lets you have more as it grows. And it's growing, Shoko. Something's coming. The kid's chest nearly turned inside out a week ago and something under it said *let me out*." He looked at his own hand. "And I just found out I've never been the ceiling. I was just the only one who never looked up."

Shoko did not say anything.

She sat on the fence with the cigarette in her fingers and looked at him. At his eyes, without the blindfold. At his face, which she had closed the eyes of on a metal table in another world, eleven days ago by her own count.

"You're going to go looking," she said. "Aren't you."

"Yeah."

"For the door. Past the edge."

"Yeah."

"You just got here. You were dead eleven days ago. You put a kid in a bed on your second day. And now you're going to go and find out how much stronger you can get."

"Yeah," said Gojo. He paused. "Slowly. Ciel says it comes slowly. A marble of Blue at a time." He held up his thumb and finger, very close together. "This much. Tonight."

Shoko looked at the space between his thumb and finger.

Then she started to laugh. Short and tired and surprised, the way she laughed in the corridor, the way she laughed when she did not mean to.

"You idiot," she said. "You absolute idiot." She shook her head. "You're happy about this."

"I'm *so* happy about this."

"You were the strongest in the world and you're happy that you're weak."

"I'm happy," said Gojo, "that I get to get *stronger*." He sat down on the fence beside her, on the post next to hers, which creaked. "Nobody ever let me do that. Not once. I was born at the top. There was nowhere to go." He looked up at the stars. "Now there's somewhere to go."

Shoko did not say anything for a while.

She sat beside him on the fence in the dark with her shoulder almost touching his. Not quite. Almost. The cigarette in her fingers. The stars very bright over the black hills.

"I had four days," she said at last, very quietly, "of you being dead."

"I know."

"I'm not having four days again." She did not look at him. "If you go looking for doors. Past the edge. If you go and get stronger and stronger until you're the ceiling again and then go and throw yourself at the next thing that comes out of the hills." Her voice was perfectly flat. "I'm not having four days again. You hear me?"

Gojo looked at her.

"I hear you," he said.

"Good."

She was quiet for a moment. Then, very slowly, she moved her shoulder the last inch. Against his. And left it there.

Gojo did not move. He did not say anything bright. He did not say anything at all. He sat on the fence at the top of the south field at midnight, with the woman who had closed his eyes leaning her shoulder against his. And for once in his life, he did not fill the silence.

"You're still a bit glowing," said Shoko, after a long time.

"Is it bad?"

"No," said Shoko. She held up the cigarette and looked at it in the starlight. Then she put it back in her pocket. "It's fine. It suits you."



Far to the east, past the black hills where the farmers keep seeing lights, past the low woods and the river towns and the roads that were not roads any more, it was the same night.

There was an old watchtower on a ridge there that nobody had kept for a hundred years. Half its roof was gone. In the half that was left, a fire was burning, low, in a ring of fallen stones. And round the fire, in the dark, sat the ones who had come.

There were not many. A dozen, perhaps. They had come over the months, one by one, from wherever they had been, the way the cold come to a fire. A captain of a city guard, in a coat with the badge cut off it, who had stopped sleeping in the spring and never started again. Two hired swords who did not talk. A hill-witch with grey eyes that could count sheep on a ridge a league away. Something from the deep woods with too many joints in its arms, which sat furthest from the fire and did not blink.

And three who had come over the bridge, the way the town had. Alone. Into fields and roads and strangers. And had found no step to sit on, and no house with a door that opened, and nobody who came looking for them with a lamp. They sat close together and did not look at the others, and the others did not look at them.

At the head of the fire, on a block of fallen stone, there was a seat that nobody sat in.

Beside it stood the one who kept the fire. Tall, in a long pale hood, with breath that came out white though the night was not cold. It had brought each of them here. It had found each of them, in the worst week of their lives, and said a few quiet words, and they had come. Nobody knew its name. Nobody had asked twice.

"Say it again," said the one who kept the fire. "All of it."

The hill-witch said it again.

"Seven dawns ago," she said. "From the high ridge. A light came up over the town. Over the south field, below their house. Like a second sun." She did not blink. "There was a wheel over it. Pale. Turning. And then shapes. A ball of blue and a ball of red going round each other. A great dark shape like teeth and stone, that cut the snow out of the air." She paused. "And then a man with white hair walked down into the middle of it. Into the light. On foot. And knelt." Another pause. "And it went out."

The fire cracked.

"He knew," said the one who kept the fire. "Before you came down off the ridge. He said he felt it." Its white breath curled. "Like a hand he had lost, closing, somewhere far away."

"Where *is* he?" said the captain.

He had not meant to say it so loudly. The fire went quiet round him.

"Three months," said the captain. "I've been here three months. I've eaten your bread and kept your watch and gone where you sent me. And not one of us has seen him. Not once." He looked at the empty seat. "Where is he?"

"Building," said the one who kept the fire.

"Building what?"

"A road." The hood turned toward him. "He had one, in his own world. A way through. He built it with the help of a thing that had a wheel over its head. A thing that learned." It paused. "He spent that road. Against the boy in that town. It burned up in the boy's light, with the wheel." Another pause. "He says it was only one road."

Nobody said anything.

"He says nothing stops him building it again," said the one who kept the fire. "He says this world lets things grow. He has felt it, since he came. Every month a little more. Things he could not do in his own world, he can almost do here." The white breath hung in the air. "He says the ceilings are higher here. For the boy. For the white-haired one. For all of them in their town." It paused. "And for him."

"Then we go now," said the captain. "Tonight. While the boy's still on his back. Before they grow."

"No."

"*Why?*"

"Because he says let them," said the one who kept the fire. "Let the boy grow. Let the white-haired one grow. Let them all grow." Its voice did not change. "He says it will be worth so much more."

The captain stared at it.

Then he looked away, into the fire, with his jaw set. He had come here because of a city. Because of a day, long enough ago now that the grass had grown over the stones, when something had walked into his city in broad daylight and cut it to pieces in an afternoon, and left nothing he loved standing, and people had started saying the word *Otherworlder* the way they said *plague*. He had come here to stop the town in the hills, where they said the Otherworlders were gathering. Where they said they were growing.

He did not know whose seat that was, at the head of the fire. Nobody had told him. Nobody was going to.

"Find more," said the one who kept the fire, to all of them. "Things that fear that town. There are a great many, now, since the city." The hood did not turn toward the captain. "Fear is easy to gather. Bring them. We have time." It paused. "He says they have given us time."

The fire burned down. The ones round it went out into the dark, one by one, the way they had come. The three from over the bridge went last, together, not talking.

The hill-witch stayed. She sat by the embers with her grey eyes on the west, toward the hills, toward a town she had watched from a ridge, where a light had come up like a second sun.

"The white-haired one," she said, at last. "Did he say anything about him?"

The one who kept the fire was looking at the empty seat.

"He said his name," it said. "Both halves. Slowly. As if tasting them." The white breath curled. "And then he laughed. For a long time."

"And then?"

The one who kept the fire did not answer for a moment.

"And then he said he wants his rematch."
