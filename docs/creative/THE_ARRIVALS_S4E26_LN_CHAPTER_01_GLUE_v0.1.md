## Chapter 31 — Glue

*When: Day 153, the morning after Gojo found his door, into the night of Day 154. Fern's halt holds: Gojo's not-looking lessons are the only lessons. Shoko has made the clinic hers between ten and four; Diablo's sash has been ironed twice; Wakana is cutting the new glass for the east window.*

Yuka was at the bottom of the main stair when he came down.

She was sitting on the last step, in a borrowed jumper of Maki's with the sleeves rolled four times, with the white dog lying across her feet and her chin in her hands. It was barely light. The common room behind her was still grey, and Momo had only just lit the stove. Yuka looked as if she had been there for some time.

She looked up when she heard him on the stair.

"Morning, Oppa," said Yuka.

"Morning," said Mau. He stopped on the step above her. "Have you been here long?"

"No," said Yuka.

The white dog yawned, enormously, in a way that said she had.

Mau went through to the long table. Yuka got up and came after him, with the dog at her heel. When he sat down at the end of the bench, she sat down beside him. Not across. Beside. On the side where Okarun usually sat. Okarun arrived a minute later with his hair on end, found her in his place, looked at her, looked at Mau, and went and sat with Momo without a word. Turbo Granny, on his shoulder, said that in her day young women did not steal a man's seat at breakfast. They stole it at dinner, when it counted.

Frieren came down ten minutes after that, in the orange hoodie, and sat on Mau's other side, where she always sat. She looked past him at Yuka. Yuka looked at her porridge.

Nobody said anything.

Yuka ate her porridge very fast, with her shoulder almost touching Mau's arm. When he got up to fetch the tea, she got up too. When he sat down, she sat down. When he reached for the honey, she passed it to him before he could get there. Frieren watched all of it with her serene face and did not say a word.

"Yuka," said Mau.

"Mm?"

"You don't have to pass me the honey."

"I know," said Yuka. She passed him the salt.



It went on all day.

She came out to the yard for Gojo's lesson and sat on the woodpile with the dog and her knees drawn up, and watched. The lesson was the same as every day. Gojo stood behind Mau and talked, and Mau looked at things without looking at them. A bucket. The ink tree. A crow on the fence. Just a bucket. Just a tree. Just a crow. Two hours of it. Gojo had said they would be the most boring two hours of Mau's life, and Gojo had been right. Yuka watched every minute as if it were the most interesting thing she had ever seen.

"We have an audience," said Gojo, halfway through.

"I'm not here," said Yuka, from the woodpile.

"She's not here," Gojo agreed. He tilted his blindfolded face toward Mau. "Look at the crow. Just the crow. Not its feathers. Not where it flew in from. Not what it ate this morning." A pause. "Okay, you're looking at what it ate this morning. I can tell. Your face does a thing."

"It ate a worm," said Mau.

"Don't *tell* me. That's the opposite of the lesson."

On the woodpile, Yuka laughed, and then put her hand over her mouth, as if she had not meant to.

She came to the workshop afterward, where Wakana had the new glass for the east window laid out on the bench on a bed of straw. She sat on an upturned crate in the corner while Mau held the pane steady and Wakana scored it. Wakana looked at the girl in the corner, and then at Mau, and raised his eyebrows very slightly. Mau shook his head very slightly. Wakana went back to the glass.

"She's your shadow," said Marin, in the doorway, delighted, with her arms full of blue cloth. "Mau, you've got a *shadow*. Like a duckling. Like a little—"

"He's already got a shadow," said Yuka, from the crate. Her voice was quiet. "That's the problem."

Marin stopped.

Yuka looked at the floor. At Mau's shadow on the boards, under the bench, long and thin in the light from the window. She looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked away, and asked Wakana what the straw was for. Wakana told her. And the moment went by, and Marin went out with her blue cloth, and nobody said anything else about shadows.

But Mau had seen her look.



She followed him to the bath corridor at the end of the day, and stopped at the door.

"Yuka," said Mau.

"I'll wait," said Yuka. She sat down on the bench outside, with the dog. "I'm just going to wait."

"It's the bath."

"I know it's the bath. I'm not *going in*." She went scarlet. "I'm going to sit here. On the bench. Which is public. It's a public bench."

From inside, Stark's voice said: "Is someone out there?"

"No," said Yuka.

Mau went in. When he came out half an hour later, in a robe, with his hair wet, she was still on the bench. So was Umaru. Umaru had come down the corridor at some point in her hood and sat at the other end of the bench, as far from Yuka as the bench allowed, and the two of them were sitting there in silence, not looking at each other, like two cats who have arrived at the same windowsill and are each waiting for the other to leave.

Mau looked at them.

"Hi, Umaru," he said.

"Hi, Mau," said Umaru. She did not look at Yuka. "I was passing."

"She's been passing for twenty minutes," said Yuka, to the ceiling.

"It's a public bench," said Umaru.

Mau walked back to the common room with both of them behind him. Yuka on his left with the white dog. Umaru on his right in her hood, three paces back, because the President did not follow anybody. Ear asleep in his shadow, under all of them. They went down the corridor in a procession, and at the cold-hearth end of the common room Gojo looked up from a cup of tea and laughed out loud.

"It's a *parade*," said Gojo.

"It's not a parade," said Mau.

"You've got two little sisters and a dog in your shadow. It's a parade. I'd *kill* for a parade." He put his chin in his hand. "Mine never followed me anywhere. They just went off and did things and I found out afterward." He said it lightly. It did not sound light.

Shoko, beside him, did not look up from her book. But her hand moved, very slightly, on the table between them, and lay flat beside his.



It went on the next day too. The hundred-and-fifty-fourth. The yard. The workshop. The long table. Yuka on the woodpile, on the crate, on the bench. And Umaru, now, every time, a little way behind, in her hood, not following.

Frieren watched all of it.

She did not say anything. She read in the reading chair, and sat beside him at meals, and put her cold foot against his leg at the reset hour, and did not say one word about the girl who had been at his elbow for two days. Mau knew Frieren. He knew what her face did when she did not like something. It did nothing. It went perfectly smooth and calm and serene, like a lake with no wind on it. And her face had been perfectly smooth and calm and serene for two days.

He did not know how to ask about it. So he did not.

But he noticed one more thing, on the second day. He noticed that whenever he stopped walking, Yuka looked down.

Not at the floor. At his shadow. Every time. Quickly. As if checking it was still only a shadow.



He woke at midnight on the hundred-and-fifty-fourth and could not get back to sleep.

Frieren was asleep beside him, on her side, with one hand fisted in the blanket. She had slept properly for three nights now. He did not want to wake her. He got up very quietly and went to the door for water.

He opened it.

Yuka was in the corridor.

She was sitting on the floor with her back against the wall opposite the east room door, with her knees drawn up and her head fallen sideways onto her shoulder. Asleep. The white dog lay across her feet, also asleep, with its nose on its paws. There was a blanket over her that she had not brought, tucked in round her shoulders.

And beside her, also on the floor, with her back against the same wall, awake, was Mai.

Mau stood in the doorway.

Mai looked up at him. She did not seem surprised. She had the look she always had, cool and a little tired, as if the world had disappointed her early and she had decided not to give it another chance to do so. She put one finger to her lips.

He came out and closed the door behind him. Very softly.

"How long?" he said, under his breath.

"Two nights," said Mai, just as quietly. "She came to my room the first night and asked which door was yours. She didn't want to ask her grandmother." Mai looked at the girl asleep beside her. "So I told her. And then I came out to see what she wanted it for." She paused. "This. Apparently."

Mau looked at Yuka. At the borrowed jumper. At the dog across her feet.

"Why?" he said.

"Ask her," said Mai. "Not me."

They sat there for a while. Or Mai sat, and Mau stood, and then, after a moment, he sat down too, on the floor on the other side of Yuka, with his back against the wall. The three of them in a row in the dark corridor. The white dog opened one eye, looked at him, looked at his shadow on the boards, and sniffed at it. Long and slow and careful, the way a dog sniffs at a door with another dog behind it. Then it put its nose back on its paws.

"You look better," said Mai.

"Shoko says I'm boring now," said Mau. "She says it's the best thing a patient can be."

Mai almost smiled.

She looked down at her own hands, in her lap. They were still. They were always still, now, in the evenings. But he remembered them shaking. In lamplight, a long time ago, on the night between, when she had told him about her one bullet a day. About what it cost. About the shaking after, that nobody saw because she made sure nobody did.

"I heard about the field," said Mai. "I was in the cellars with the fort. But I heard." She did not look up. "Everything at once, they said. Every pattern you've got. All of it, out of your body. At the same time."

"Yes."

Mai was quiet for a long moment.

"One bullet a day," she said. "And I shake after." She turned one hand over, and looked at the palm. "Yours was a lot of bullets, Mau."

He did not say anything. His hand went, without his meaning it to, to his chest. To where the inside pocket of his coat would have been, if he had been wearing it. Where her bullet lived, cold, beside the warm coin, every day since the night she set it down on the long table.

"Did you count it?" said Mai.

"I tried," said Mau. "In the clinic. When I could hold a pen." He looked down the dark corridor. "I opened the log to the next page. And I didn't know what to write. So I wrote what it was."

"What did you write?"

"*Day 145. Everything. Hands: still shaking. Helped: everyone.*"

Mai was quiet for a long moment.

"That's the right count," she said.

"I'm not going to tell you anything about it," said Mai. "I'm not your doctor. You've got a doctor now. A good one, from what I hear. She shouted at the strongest man in her world in our corridor." She almost smiled again. "I just wanted to say I know. What it looks like. After. When you build things out of yourself and the bill comes." She closed the hand. "I know."

"I know you know," said Mau. "You told me. On the night between." He paused. "I didn't forget."

Mai looked at him then. For the first time since he had sat down.

"No," she said. "I didn't think you would."

She got up. She did it quietly, the way she did everything, without a sound. She stood over the two of them for a moment, looking down at Yuka asleep against the wall with her mouth open.

"She thinks it's hers," said Mai.

Mau looked up. "What is?"

"Your bullets."

And she went away down the corridor, toward her room beside Yuta and Maki's, and did not look back.



He sat there with Yuka for a long time.

He did not wake her. He did not know what he would say if he did. He sat on the floor of the corridor in the dark, beside a girl he had known for thirteen days, who had come from the end of a life he had only heard about, carrying a ring that had crossed a world to find her. Who had sat on a bench in the common room and told him that pushing the dog down was more dangerous than letting it out. Who had stood with him in a circle in the south field and said *small, nothing big*. Who had heard Fern say *no taming* at the long table, two days after the field, and nodded very fast, and not said a word.

He thought about that. About the way she had nodded.

*Oh,* he thought.

Footsteps in the corridor. Light. Fast. A long shape in the dark, with a spear's habits in how she walked even without the spear.

Maki stopped in front of them.

She looked down at her granddaughter, asleep against the wall. At the blanket Mai had tucked round her. At the white dog. At Mau, sitting beside her on the floor. Her face did something complicated in the dark. Then it settled.

"There you are," said Maki, very quietly. To Yuka, who did not hear her. "Idiot."

She crouched. She got one arm under Yuka's knees and one behind her back and lifted her, blanket and all, as easily as lifting a cat, and the girl did not wake. She only turned her face into Maki's shoulder and mumbled something that might have been *Granny* and might have been nothing. The white dog got up, stretched, and stood at Maki's heel, waiting.

Maki looked at Mau over the top of her granddaughter's head.

"She's like her grandfather," said Maki. "When she thinks something's her fault, she sits outside doors." She paused. "He does roofs. She does corridors. Same thing."

"She thinks it's her fault," said Mau. "The field."

"I know."

"It wasn't."

"I know," said Maki. "You know. Everyone in this house knows." She shifted Yuka's weight on her shoulder. "She's seventeen. She doesn't know anything yet." Something moved in her face, very briefly. "She will. I watched her learn everything else."

She turned to go. Then stopped.

"She's going to ask you something tomorrow," said Maki, without turning round. "Something stupid first. Probably about the elf." A pause. "Then something real."

"How do you know?"

Maki looked back at him over her shoulder. In the dark, with her granddaughter asleep in her arms, she looked, for one second, very old. Not her face. Something behind it. Sixty years, folded up very small.

"Because I raised the one who raised her," said Maki. "That's how she does it. Stupid first. Then real."

She went away down the corridor with Yuka in her arms and the white dog at her heel.

Mau sat on the floor of the corridor alone for a moment longer. Then he got up, and opened the east room door very softly, and went back in.

Frieren was awake.

She was lying on her side in the dark with her eyes open, looking at the door. She had clearly heard all of it. She did not say anything. She lifted the edge of the blanket.

He got in. She put her cold foot against his leg, which was not the reset hour, which was not anything.

"Seventeen," said Frieren, very quietly, into the dark.

"Yeah."

Frieren did not say anything else for a long time. But her face, when he looked at it, in the dark, very close, was not smooth any more. It was not serene. It was just her face. Thinking.

"Go to sleep," she said at last.

He went to sleep.
