## Chapter 32 — Are You With the Elf?

*When: Day 155. Yuka woke in her own bed with no idea how she got there; Mai has said nothing to anyone; the fort has noticed that its President spent two days "passing" Mau and has not been told why.*

Yuka found her grandmother behind the barn, where Maki trained in the mornings now that the gate was shut.

Maki had a pole instead of her spear, and she was going through the forms she always went through. Slow, then fast, then slower than slow, so slow that it hurt to watch. The pole did not wobble. It never wobbled. Daphne's bees went in and out of their hives along the barn wall and ignored her completely, the way they had learned to.

Yuka stood at the corner of the barn for some time before Maki stopped.

"Out with it," said Maki, without turning round.

Yuka came round the corner. She was red already. She had been red since breakfast, when she had come down and found her place at Mau's elbow empty and sat between her grandparents instead, very straight, looking at nothing, while Yuta asked if she had slept well and she said *yes* in a voice that made Gojo, at the cold-hearth end, choke on his tea.

"Granny," said Yuka.

"Mm."

"Is Oppa—" She stopped. She started again. "Is Mau. With. The elf."

Maki lowered the pole.

She turned round and looked at her granddaughter for a long moment, leaning on the pole, with her glasses pushed up on her forehead and sweat on her neck.

"Yes," said Maki.

"Like *with* with?"

"Like *with* with."

Yuka absorbed this. "Since when?"

"Since before anyone else in this house worked it out," said Maki. "Including them." She wiped her neck with her sleeve. "Two years, give or take. They were the last to know."

"Oh," said Yuka. She looked at the bees. "Okay. Okay. That's—okay."

Maki watched her.

"Yuka," she said.

"What?"

"Don't."

"Don't *what*?"

"Whatever you're about to do," said Maki. "Whatever you're thinking. Don't get between them."

Yuka went scarlet to the roots of her hair. "I'm not—I don't *like* him like that! Granny! That's—*gross*, he's—he's *Oppa*—"

"I know you don't," said Maki calmly. "Does she?"

Yuka stopped.

Maki leaned the pole against the barn wall. She sat down on the chopping block beside it and looked up at her granddaughter, and her face was not her training face any more. It was something quieter.

"I'll tell you something about that elf," said Maki. "She's my friend. We stopped talking once, early on. Over something stupid. The whole house tiptoed for a week. It was the worst week I had here, and I've had weeks here with things coming out of the hills." She looked at her hands. "She's not going to hurt you. That's not why I'm saying it. She'd never touch you."

"Then why—"

"Because if she thought you were in the way," said Maki, "she'd step back."

Yuka did not say anything.

"That's what she does," said Maki. "She's a thousand years old. She's watched everybody she ever cared about grow old and die and go on without her. Somewhere in her head she's decided she's the one who leaves. Every year on her birthday she tries to slip out of the house before anyone notices, and every year the fort has to stand in the door." She paused. "If she thought you were in the way, she wouldn't fight you. She'd go quiet. She'd go smooth. And she'd step back, and back, and let you have the seat at breakfast, and the honey, and the elbow. Because she thinks that's what she's for." Maki looked up. "And it'd break something in both of them that I don't know how you'd fix."

Yuka stood very still by the barn wall.

"Her face has been smooth for two days," said Maki. "I've been watching it."

"*Oh,*" said Yuka. Very small.

"Yeah."

"I didn't—I wasn't trying to—"

"I know," said Maki. She stood up, and picked up her pole again, and turned back to the forms. "So go and tell him. Whatever it is you've been sitting outside his door about. Tell him. And tell her too, while you're at it." The pole moved, slow, slow, slower than slow. "And then sit on the other side of the table at breakfast for a while, idiot."



Mau was under the ink tree.

He was on the bench under it, in the yard, with his eyes closed. Not because he had to. Because it was restful. Because Shoko had said that the eyes were new and the filter was new and the walls were tired, and that resting them for an hour in the afternoon was what any sensible doctor would prescribe, and she was a sensible doctor, and she was prescribing it. So he sat on the bench under the ink tree with his eyes closed and listened to the yard. Senku and Suika in the lab. Momo in the kitchen. Gojo somewhere, laughing. The bees.

He heard her coming. Her steps, quick and then slow and then quick, the way you walk when you have decided something and keep undeciding it.

He opened his eyes.

Yuka stopped in front of the bench. The white dog sat down beside her and looked at him with its tongue out.

"Hi," said Mau.

"Are you with the elf," said Yuka, all in one breath.

Mau looked at her.

"Yes," he said.

"Like *with* with."

"Yes."

"Okay," said Yuka. "Okay. That's what Granny said." She stood there in the borrowed jumper with her fists closed at her sides. "How old is she?"

"Over a thousand."

Yuka stared at him. "How old are *you*?"

"Younger," said Mau.

"That's—" Yuka opened her mouth. She closed it. "That's a *lot* younger."

"She says two years is like a sneeze to her," said Mau. "So I'm trying to be a long sneeze."

Yuka made a sound that might have been a laugh. It came out wrong. She put her hand over her mouth.

Behind her, across the yard, the back door of the house opened.



Frieren came out.

She came out in the orange hoodie with her hands in the front pocket, and she walked across the yard toward the ink tree at an ordinary pace, with her serene face. Elves have long ears. Everyone in the house knew that. Everyone in the house had learned, at some point, in some embarrassing way, exactly how long, and exactly how much they could hear through a wall.

She did not hurry. She did not look at Yuka. She walked toward the bench as if she were simply going to sit on it.

Halfway across the yard, something small and pink landed in front of her.

"Guardian," said Milim.

Frieren stopped.

"I'm only walking," said Frieren.

"I know," said Milim. She did not move out of the way. She stood in the middle of the yard, very small, with her arms folded and her pigtails sticking out, and looked up at the elf with enormous seriousness. "Guardian duty. Nobody Zoltraaks anybody in the yard. It's in the constitution. Umaru put it in after the snowball."

"I wasn't going to Zoltraak anybody."

Milim looked down.

Frieren looked down.

Her right hand had come out of the hoodie pocket at some point. She did not remember taking it out. It was hanging at her side, and the tips of her fingers were glowing. Very faintly. A pale, steady, silvery light, the colour of the beginning of a spell.

Frieren looked at her own hand for a long moment.

She closed it.

Milim took her wrist. Gently. The way she had taken it at the fence ten days ago, with all her strength, when the field was full of light. Not with all her strength now. Just enough.

"I'm holding you," said Milim.

Frieren did not pull away. She stood in the middle of the yard with Milim's small hand round her wrist, and her face was perfectly smooth, perfectly calm.

"It's not the same," she said quietly.

"I know," said Milim. "I'm still holding you."



At the ink tree, Yuka had turned round.

She saw the elf in the middle of the yard. She saw the dragon holding her wrist. She saw the faint silver glow fading out of the elf's closed fist. She went white, and then red, and then a colour that did not have a name.

"I DON'T LIKE HIM LIKE THAT," shouted Yuka.

The whole yard stopped. In the lab, Senku's grinding stopped. In the kitchen, Momo's chopping stopped. Somewhere out of sight, Gojo stopped laughing.

"I don't!" said Yuka, very loudly, to the elf, across the yard. "I don't, I swear, he's—he's *Oppa*! He's like my brother! He's—it would be *gross*! It would be *so gross*! I'd rather kiss the *dog*—" The white dog looked up at her, offended. "—not you, sorry, I mean the *idea* of—"

"Thanks," said Mau, from the bench.

"*You know what I mean!*"

Frieren looked at her across the yard for a long moment.

Then her hand opened. There was nothing in it. Just her hand. She looked down at Milim, who was still holding her wrist.

"You can let go now," said Frieren.

Milim considered her very carefully. Then she let go, and stepped aside, and said, "Okay. But I'm watching," and went and sat on the edge of the well, swinging her legs, watching.

Frieren walked the rest of the way to the ink tree.

She sat down on the bench, on Mau's other side. Where she always sat. She put her hands back in the pocket of the hoodie. She did not look at Yuka. She looked at the yard.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Yuka stood in front of them, scarlet, with her fists closed.

"I'm sorry," said Yuka at last, to the ground. "About the honey. And the seat. And the—everything. I wasn't trying to be in the way. I didn't think. I just—" She stopped.

"Sit down," said Mau.

She sat down. On the end of the bench, beyond Mau, as far from the elf as the bench allowed. The white dog lay down across her feet.

And then it came out. The real thing. In a very small voice, looking at nothing.

"It was my fault," said Yuka. "Wasn't it."

Mau opened his mouth.

"The field," said Yuka, before he could. "I told you. On the bench. At breakfast. I said it was more dangerous to push it down. I made you go out and fight it. I stood you in the circle. And it made your walls tired. Ciel said. Everyone heard her. *They held, but they're so tired.*" Her voice cracked. "And two days later they broke. And everything came out of you at once. And you almost—" She stopped. "And Fern said *no taming*, and she looked right at me. And she was right to. Because I did it. I made it happen. If I hadn't come—"

"Yuka," said Mau.

"If I hadn't *come*—"

"No," said Frieren.

Yuka stopped.

Frieren was not looking at her. She was looking at the yard. At the well, where Milim sat swinging her legs. At the lab door, where Suika's watermelon had appeared, listening. At the ink tree, over all of them, with its dark leaves.

"If you hadn't come," said Frieren, "he would have done it alone."

Yuka looked at her.

"I know him," said Frieren. "I've known him a long time. Longer than it feels to him, and much shorter than it feels to me." She did not look round. "The dog was in his shadow. It was trying to get out. It was crying to get out, in its way, the way you said. And he can't bear things that cry." She paused. "He'd have gone out one night. Alone. Without telling anyone, because he wouldn't want to worry anyone. He'd have gone out to the south field in the dark and let it out and put his hand on its head. And there'd have been no circle. No witches at the edge. No Rimuru holding the walls. Nobody saying *small, nothing big*." Her voice was very even. "You made it a thing done in daylight, with everyone there. That's what you did."

Yuka did not say anything.

"And the walls," said Frieren, "were tired before you came. They've been tired for two years. He's been tired for two years." She was quiet for a moment. "It wasn't the dog that looked at the walls. It was the eyes. The eyes would have looked whether the dog was tamed or not. They'd read everything outside, so they looked in. That was always going to happen. The day you came, it was already coming." She turned her head at last, and looked at the girl on the end of the bench, across Mau. Her green eyes were very calm. "It wasn't yours."

"But Fern looked at me—"

"Fern looks at everyone," said Frieren. "It's how she counts us."

Yuka's face crumpled.

She did not cry loudly. She cried the way her grandmother cried, which Mau had seen exactly once: furiously, with her jaw set, as if the tears were happening to someone else and she disapproved of them. She sat on the end of the bench with her fists in her lap and cried at the ground.

Frieren looked at her for a moment.

Then, slowly, as if remembering how to do something she had not done in a long time, she reached across Mau and put her hand on top of Yuka's head. The way she had once put her hand on the head of a small dark-haired girl in a village, a long time ago, who had grown up to be Fern.

She did not pat. She just left it there.

"I've been blaming myself for things for a thousand years," said Frieren. "I'm very good at it. Much better than you." She paused. "So take it from someone who's better at it. This one isn't yours."

Yuka cried harder.

Mau sat between them on the bench, with Frieren's arm across his chest and Yuka crying on his other side, and did not say anything at all. There was nothing he needed to say. The elf had said it.



It took a while.

When Yuka had stopped, and wiped her face on Maki's sleeve, and the white dog had licked her hand four times, she sat up very straight on the end of the bench and looked at Mau.

"Can I ask one more thing?" she said.

"Yes."

"Can I be your little sister?" She went red again. "Not like—not *weirdly*. Just. Officially. Since I already call you Oppa. And I don't have—" She stopped. "My brother's back there. Tsurugi. We—" She stopped again. "It's complicated. With him. It's always been complicated." She looked at her own hands. "I'd just like one where it isn't. Here. If that's allowed."

Mau looked at her.

He opened his mouth to say *yes*.

"You'll have to ask Umaru," said Frieren.

Mau closed his mouth.

Yuka blinked. "Who?"

From behind the trunk of the ink tree, where she had very clearly been standing for some time, in her hood, Umaru stepped out.

"The position of little sister," said Umaru, "is *filled*."

Yuka stared at her. Umaru stared back. The hood was up. The face under it was very serious. She had a slate under one arm and the Ministry stamp in her other hand, and she looked, for all the world, like a small bureaucrat who has been waiting behind a tree for two days to deliver exactly this sentence.

"You're the one from the bench," said Yuka.

"I'm the President," said Umaru. "And the Minister of Fort Affairs. And his little sister. It's in the register." She paused. "I've got two older brothers. He's one of them." She looked at Mau. Something moved in her face under the hood, very quick, and was gone. "The other one's not here."

Mau did not say anything. He knew who the other one was. A man in an apartment in another world, cooking for nobody.

Yuka looked at Umaru for a long moment.

"I'm older than you," said Yuka.

"Seniority," said Umaru, "is by arrival date."

"*That's not how sisters work!*"

"It's how *this house* works," said Umaru. "I was here first. I carried blankets in the evacuation. I built the fort. I have a *constitution*." She held up the stamp. "But." She paused. "There's room for a second one. If she applies. In writing."

Yuka opened her mouth.

She closed it.

"What's the position called?" she said, suspiciously.

Umaru considered this with enormous gravity. She looked at Mau. At Yuka. At the white dog. At the ink tree.

"Little Sister," said Umaru, "Teleport Division."

There was a pause.

"...Okay," said Yuka.

Umaru wrote it on the slate in chalk, very carefully, with her tongue between her teeth. LITTLE SISTER (TELEPORT DIVISION): YUKA. PROBATIONARY. She stamped it. She handed it to Yuka, who took it in both hands and looked at it as if it were something precious, which it was.

"You have to come to the fort," said Umaru. "Probationary members have to come to the fort. It's the rule." She paused. "There's a cushion."

"I can bring the dog?"

Umaru looked at the white dog. The white dog looked at Umaru.

"The dog can be an honorary citizen," said Umaru. "Like Agent Bird."

And the two of them went off across the yard toward the house together, the President in her hood and the probationary little sister with the slate held against her chest, and the white dog trotting behind. At the well, Milim jumped down and ran after them, shouting that she wanted to be a sister too, of somebody, of anybody, that it wasn't fair that everyone had a division but her.

Mau watched them go.

Then he looked at Frieren, beside him on the bench.

She had her hands back in the pocket of the hoodie. Her face was not smooth. It was just her face. A little pink, at the tips of her ears.

"Your hand was glowing," said Mau.

"No, it wasn't."

"Milim had to hold your wrist."

"Milim likes holding wrists," said Frieren. "It's a hobby."

He looked at her. She looked at the yard.

"I thought she might be in the way," said Frieren, after a long time, very quietly. "For two days. I thought—" She stopped. "It doesn't matter what I thought."

"Your face went smooth," said Mau. "For two days. I noticed."

Frieren was quiet.

"You noticed," she said.

"I always notice."

She did not say anything to that. But after a while she took her hands out of the pocket of the hoodie, and leaned sideways, and put her head on his shoulder. Under the ink tree. In the yard. In the afternoon, where anyone in the kitchen window could see. And from the kitchen window, Momo very carefully did not look.

"I'm still going to sit on your other side," said Frieren. "At breakfast. That's mine."

"It's yours," said Mau.

"Good," said Frieren, and closed her eyes.
