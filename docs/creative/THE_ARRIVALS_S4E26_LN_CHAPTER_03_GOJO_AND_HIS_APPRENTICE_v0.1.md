*When: Day 156, and the afternoons after, into the night of Day 161. Shoko has prescribed four hours of blindfold a day and written it in Fern's ledger, where it is law. The fort sewed all night. Yuka has her first fort cushion; Gojo has not been told anything and is about to find out at breakfast.*

"Four hours," said Shoko. "A day. Every day. Until I say."

She said it at breakfast, at the long table, with her tea in one hand and Fern's ledger open in front of her, where she had written it the night before in very neat doctor's handwriting under Fern's *Everything stops*. Fern had read it, and initialled it, and so it was law.

Mau looked at the entry. *M.: blindfold, 4 hrs/day, rest of eyes and filter. Afternoons. No exceptions. —S.I.*

"I close my eyes in the afternoons," said Mau. "Under the ink tree."

"You close them for an hour," said Shoko. "And then someone talks to you and you open them. And then someone else talks to you and you open them again. And then a bee goes by." She drank her tea. "The eyes are new. The filter is new. The walls are tired. Closing them isn't rest. It's a lid you keep lifting." She put the cup down. "Satoru wears his because the eyes tire him. They've tired him since he was a child. Yours will tire you too, if you let them. So you're not going to let them."

At the cold-hearth end, Gojo had stopped with a rice cake halfway to his mouth.

"Wait," said Gojo. "Wait, wait, wait. Are you saying—"

"I'm saying he wears one."

"Like *mine*?"

"Like yours."

Gojo put the rice cake down. Very slowly. A smile spread across the lower half of his face that Mau had learned, in three weeks, to fear more than anything else in either world.

"Shoko," said Gojo. "Are you telling me I have an *apprentice*?"

"I'm telling you he has a prescription."

"That's what an apprentice *is*." He was on his feet. "Where are we going to get one? It has to be black. It has to be *exactly* the right black. Not a cheap black. Not a grey that thinks it's black. It has to have the right—"

"Already done," said Umaru, from the arch of the fort.

Everyone looked round.

Umaru was standing on the toll booth platform with a small folded square of black cloth held up in both hands, the way you would hold up a crown on a cushion. Behind her, in a heap, the fort looked as if it had not slept, which it had not. Wakana was asleep sitting up against the fort's back wall with a needle still in her hand. Marin was asleep across her lap. Coco had thread in her hair.

"The fort sewed it," said Umaru. "Last night. Wakana cut it. Marin designed it." A pause. "Marin wanted to put a rhinestone on it. The fort voted the rhinestone down."

"Twenty-eight to two," said Coco, sleepily.

"Who were the two?"

"Marin," said Coco. "And Milim."

"I wanted it to *sparkle*," said Milim, from somewhere in the heap.

Gojo crossed the common room in four strides and took the black cloth from Umaru's hands and unfolded it and held it up to the light. He turned it over. He ran it between his fingers. He held it up next to his own face, next to his own blindfold, and tilted his head.

"It's the right black," said Gojo, in a voice of deep emotion. "It's the *exact* right black." He turned to the fort. "Who matched it?"

"Wakana," said Umaru. "She took a thread off yours while you were asleep."

Gojo opened his mouth. He closed it. He looked at Wakana, asleep against the wall with the needle in her hand, with the expression of a man who has just found out he was robbed in the night and that it was the best thing that ever happened to him.

"I love this house," said Gojo.



He tied it on Mau himself.

In the yard, after breakfast, under the ink tree, with half the house watching from the back step and the other half watching from the windows and pretending not to. Gojo stood behind him and wound the black cloth round his eyes and knotted it once at the back of his head, snug, the way his own was knotted. The way it had been knotted in the south field, the first time, when there had been snow hanging in the air and blood on the cloth.

Mau stood still while he did it. He did not think about the south field. He thought about the cloth. It smelled of the fort. Of cushions and chalk and someone's tea.

"There," said Gojo. He stepped round in front of him. "Let me look at you."

Mau could not see him. He could hear him. The crunch of his boots in the yard. The way his voice came from slightly above.

"Oh, no," said Gojo softly. "Oh, no. Shoko. Shoko, come and look at this."

"I'm looking," said Shoko, from the back step.

"He looks *exactly* like me."

"He looks nothing like you."

"He looks *exactly like me* but *shorter* and with *worse hair*." Gojo's voice was full of a terrible joy. "He's my apprentice. He's my *mini-me*. Somebody get the Ministry stamp. I want this *registered*."

"I don't mind," said Mau.

"He doesn't mind!" Gojo was delighted. "Did you hear that? He doesn't mind! That's how you know he's the one!"

And that was how it started.



It went everywhere after that. Four hours every afternoon, and Gojo turned every one of them into a procession.

He took Mau round the settlement. The two of them, blindfolded, side by side, at a leisurely stroll, down the main street, past Arrival House, through the plaza, out to the edge of the fields and back. Gojo walked as if he could see every stone, because he could. Mau walked with his hand on Gojo's sleeve for the first ten minutes, because he could not. People stopped and stared. The register girl at Arrival House dropped her pen. Two farmers on the Muslin road took off their hats, uncertainly, as if the blindfolds were some sort of religious order they had not been told about.

"Smile," said Gojo, out of the side of his mouth. "Wave. We're a *brand*."

"I can't see who I'm waving at."

"Nobody can see who *I'm* waving at either. That's the mystery. That's what makes it work."

By the plaza, Mau had let go of the sleeve.

By the fields, he did not need it at all.

Gojo noticed. Of course he noticed. Halfway back up the main street, he stopped, and Mau stopped beside him without being told, and Gojo turned his blindfolded face toward him.

"How are you doing that?"

"Doing what?"

"Walking. Straight. You stopped when I stopped. You stepped over the rut by the water trough. You didn't even slow down." A pause. "You can't see. I checked. The filter's holding and the cloth's on and the eyes are behind both. You're not using them." His voice had gone very slightly careful. "So how are you doing it?"

Mau thought about it.

"I know it," he said.

Gojo was quiet.

"The street," said Mau. "I've walked it every day since we came. I know where the trough is because I've broken the ice on it every morning all winter. I know where the rut is because the cart got stuck there in the thaw and I pushed it out." He turned his head, toward the sound of the river, beyond the end of the street. "The house is easier. I built the house. I know every board in it. I know which stair creaks. I know where the shelf is in the middle of our floor. I know how many steps from the east room door to the common room." He paused. "Forty-one. Forty-three if Ori's left her boots out."

Gojo did not say anything for a long moment.

"Huh," he said at last, very quietly.

"What?"

"Nothing." He started walking again. After a while he said, in a different voice, not the brand voice, "I couldn't do that. In my world. Walk anywhere without the eyes." He paused. "I never built anything. I never had to know where anything was. I could always just see it."

Mau did not say anything.

"That's a good thing to have," said Gojo. "Knowing where things are because you put them there." He was quiet for a few steps. "Better than seeing."



On the sixth afternoon, Suika came out of the lab with the watermelon.

She came across the yard to the ink tree, where Mau was sitting in the black blindfold beside Gojo in his, the two of them on the bench in identical attitudes with their legs stretched out. The fort had started calling them *the Lighthouse* (because they stood very still and looked at nothing, Umaru said, and everyone stared at them). Suika stopped in front of them. She had her glasses on. She was holding the watermelon helmet in both arms, carefully, the way you would hold a pet.

"Mau-san," said Suika.

"Hi, Suika."

"Suika brought you something." She held out the watermelon. "For your eyes."

There was a pause.

"Ah," said Gojo, with enormous dignity. "Sorry. He already has eyewear. It's very exclusive. It's the right black."

"It's not eyewear," said Suika. "It's a *helmet*."

"It's a fruit."

"It's a *helmet* that *looks like* a fruit." Suika's voice was very firm. She turned back to Mau. She could see him. He could not see her. But he could hear her, and he could hear that something in her voice was not about the joke. "Mau-san. Can Suika tell you something?"

"Yes."

Suika sat down on the end of the bench, with the watermelon in her lap.

"Before Senku made Suika's glasses," she said, "Suika couldn't see. Not properly. Not ever. Everything was blurry. Colours and shapes. Suika could tell people apart by how they walked, but not their faces. Nobody knew. Suika wore the watermelon so nobody could see Suika not seeing." She put her hand on top of the helmet. "And then Senku made the glasses. And Suika put them on. And everything came in at once."

Mau was very still.

"Everything," said Suika. "Every leaf. Every face. The edges on everything. The stars." Her voice went small. "It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing. And it was too much. Suika cried for a whole day. Because it was so sharp. Because there was so *much* of it, all the time, and Suika couldn't make it stop."

Gojo had gone quiet on the bench.

"Sometimes," said Suika, "Suika still puts the watermelon on. Even now. Not to hide. Just to make the world soft for a bit. When it's too sharp." She held the helmet out again, toward Mau, toward the sound of her own voice. "So. In case the black one isn't soft enough. Suika can lend it. Any time."

Mau put out his hands. She put the watermelon in them. It was lighter than he expected, and the rind was smooth and cool, and it smelled faintly of the lab and of Suika's hair.

"Thank you," said Mau.

He meant it more than she would know. Or perhaps she did know. Perhaps that was why she had come.

"Can I try it?" said Gojo.

Suika looked at him with deep suspicion.

"For *research*," said Gojo.

Ten minutes later, the fort, looking out of every window it had, saw Satoru Gojo, the strongest sorcerer of his generation, sitting on the bench under the ink tree in the middle of the afternoon with a watermelon on his head, with his long legs stretched out and his hands folded on his stomach, perfectly content, while his apprentice sat beside him in the right black, and Suika stood in front of them both with her arms folded, looking deeply satisfied.

"It *is* soft," said Gojo, from inside the watermelon, in a voice of wonder. "Shoko. Shoko. It's *soft*."

"I'm not looking," said Shoko, from the back step, who was looking.

On Okarun's shoulder, crossing the yard, Turbo Granny stopped dead.

"*What,*" said Turbo Granny, "*is that.*"

"It's Gojo," said Okarun.

"I *know* it's the beanpole. I meant the *fruit*." She stared at the watermelon. "I've lived a very long time. I've haunted a tunnel and a lucky cat and a high school boy's family jewels. I have never seen a man wear a melon with that much *self-respect*."

"Thank you," said the watermelon.

"That wasn't a compliment."

"It sounded like one."

Turbo Granny made a sound of profound disgust and told Okarun to keep walking. But halfway to the house, she looked back over his shoulder. Twice.



That night, Gojo found Mau on the roof.

It was Yuta's roof, really. The ridge of the real house, above the east wing, where Yuta went when he could not sleep, and where every seventh night he and Mau and Rimuru climbed up with a blanket and talked about nothing. Where, in the snow, Rimuru had laughed for the first time. Mau had not meant to come up. He had just found himself on the ladder, and then on the ridge, sitting with his knees drawn up and his arms round them. The blindfold was off. It was night. The four hours were long done. He was looking at the stars over the black hills, just the stars, the way Gojo had taught him. Not their distances. Not their names. Just lights.

Gojo came up the ladder without a sound and sat down beside him on the ridge.

He had his blindfold off too. His eyes, in the dark, were very blue. Not the blue of the ocean. The other blue. The sky, at noon, in summer, in a place with no clouds.

They sat there for a while.

"I'm not going to be your teacher," said Gojo.

Mau looked at him. "You've been teaching me for three weeks."

"Lessons. Sure. Lessons, I can do. I'm a fantastic lesson-giver. Everyone says so." He was looking at the stars. "But I'm not going to be your teacher."

Mau waited.

"I had students," said Gojo. "In my world. Good kids. The best. Yuta was one of them. There were others." He paused. "I made them strong. That was the job, I thought. Make them strong. Make them so strong that nothing could ever take them from me. I was the strongest, so I'd make them the strongest after me, and then nothing would ever happen to any of them, and the world would be fine." He laughed, very quietly. It was not a happy laugh. "And then I went off and did my own thing. Because I was Satoru Gojo, and the world needed me in about nine places at once, and they were strong now, so they'd be fine."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I got locked in a box," said Gojo. "In the worst month of their lives. I was in a box, and they were out there, being strong without me. Because I'd made them strong. And it wasn't enough. Strong was never going to be enough." He looked at his own hands. "And before that—" He stopped. "Before that, my best friend went somewhere very dark. Right next to me. For a year. And I didn't see it. Me." He touched the corner of one blue eye with one finger. "With these. I could see everything in the world. I could see a fly land on a wall a mile away. And I didn't see him going."

Mau did not say anything.

"I saw everything," said Gojo, "except the thing that mattered."

Below them, in the house, a lamp went out. Then another.

"So I'm not going to be your teacher," said Gojo. "Teachers make you strong and then watch you go. I've done that. I'm very good at it. It's the worst thing I'm good at." He turned his head and looked at Mau, in the dark, with his sky-coloured eyes. "I'm going to be around. That's all. I'm going to be *around*. In the yard. On the roof. At the table. Annoying you. For as long as this place lets me." He paused. "That's the whole plan. I'm going to be the guy who's around."

Mau looked at him.

He opened his mouth. He did not know what he was going to say. Something had come up into his throat, something with a shape he recognised, a word with a shape, and he did not know if he was allowed to say it.

"Like a—" said Mau.

"Don't," said Gojo quickly. "Don't say it. If you say it, Shoko will hear it from wherever she is and she will never, *ever* let me forget it." He looked back at the stars. "She'll put it in the ledger."

Mau closed his mouth.

He did not say it. He sat on the ridge of the house he had built, beside the man who had looked into everything that came out of him and borne it, and did not say the word. It sat in his throat, warm, with its shape, unsaid.

"Okay," said Mau.

"Okay," said Gojo.

From the other end of the ridge, in the dark, where neither of them had looked, a voice said: "You never said that to us."

Gojo went still.

Yuta was sitting at the far end of the roof, by the chimney, with his knees drawn up, where he had been sitting since long before either of them came up. In the starlight the stitched scar across his forehead was a thin pale line. He was not looking at them. He was looking at the hills.

"No," said Gojo, after a long time. "I didn't."

Yuta was quiet.

"I'm sorry," said Gojo. It came out very simply. No brightness on it at all. "Yuta. I'm sorry. I should have. To all of you."

Yuta looked at his teacher for a long moment, across the length of the roof. He had lived a whole life since the last time they stood on a roof together. Sixty years. He had grown old and taught students of his own and buried people and watched a granddaughter grow up. He knew things about Satoru Gojo that Satoru Gojo did not know about himself.

"I know," said Yuta.

He got up. He came along the ridge, carefully, in the dark, and sat down on Mau's other side. So that Mau was in the middle. The three of them on the ridge of the house, in a row, looking at the hills.

"Say it to him, then," said Yuta quietly. "Properly. Every day. Since you didn't get to say it to us." He looked at the hills. "That's how you make it up. Not by being sorry. By being around."

Gojo did not answer.

But he stayed. All three of them did. Until the last lamp in the house went out, and the stars over the black hills were the only lights left, and Mau, in the middle, without meaning to, fell asleep sitting up, with his head tipped sideways onto his teacher's shoulder. The one who was not his teacher. The one who was going to be around.

Gojo did not move for a long time.

"Yuta," he said at last, very quietly. "He's asleep on me."

"I know."

"What do I do?"

"Nothing," said Yuta. "You do nothing. You just sit there." He almost smiled. "That's the whole thing. That's all it ever was."
