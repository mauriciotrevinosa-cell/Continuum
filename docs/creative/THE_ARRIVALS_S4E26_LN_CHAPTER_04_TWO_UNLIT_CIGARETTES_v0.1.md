*When: Day 157. Gojo has walked his apprentice through the settlement twice, blindfolded, and the register girl at Arrival House has dropped her pen both times. Wakana is laying out the next empty room in the west wing, and the beams for it are in the yard. Mau is bored, which Shoko says is medically excellent, and has decided to do something about it.*

The beams for the next empty room were in the yard, in a stack by the barn, where Stark and Eren had dragged them up from the river that morning. Rimuru's rule: one empty room, always, ready. Wakana had chalked the outline of it on the west wing floor already, a neat rectangle with a door and a window, and the beams were waiting to go up.

Mau had been looking at them all morning.

He was bored. He had not been bored in two years. He had been frightened, and exhausted, and in love, and half dead, and once, briefly, a slime. But not bored. And now he had been bored for twelve days. No taming. No gate. No lessons but the not-looking ones, which were the most boring thing ever invented by anyone in any world, and which Gojo made worse by enjoying them. Four hours a day in a blindfold. Afternoons on the bench. Shoko taking his pulse at breakfast and lunch and dinner and saying *boring* each time, approvingly, as if it were a gift.

So when Stark went in for a drink of water and left the end of a beam lying in the yard, Mau went over and picked it up.

Not the whole thing. One end. He was not stupid. He bent his knees and got his hands under it and straightened up, and the beam came off the ground, heavy and good and real, and for one moment he felt like a person who did things.

"Put it down," said a voice behind him.

He did not turn round. He knew the voice.

"It's a beam," said Mau.

"It's a beam your walls will feel," said Shoko. She was standing at the back step in her white coat, with her arms folded. "Everything you lift, they lift. Everything you strain, they strain. They're new. They're tired. They don't need to hold up a beam today." A pause. "Put it down."

"It's *one end*."

"Then it's one end your walls will feel. Put it down."

He looked at her. She looked back. Her face did not do anything at all. It was the face of a woman who has had this conversation with a great many young people who thought they were indestructible, and has opened some of them afterward.

Mau put it down.

"Thank you," said Shoko, and went back inside.



He went to the lab.

Senku was firing the kiln. A new batch of glass for the lantern project, and the lab was full of heat and the smell of hot sand and Senku's voice saying *ten billion percent* to something that was not cooperating. Mau stood in the doorway and watched him wrestle a crucible out of the fire with long tongs, and then went in, and took the other pair of tongs off the wall.

"I'll hold the—"

"Rule four," said Anko.

Mau stopped.

Anko was sitting on a stool in the corner of the lab, which she had never once done in two years, with her notebook open on her knee and her pencil in her hand. She did not look up. She wrote something.

"Rule four," said Mau slowly, "is about Ciel."

"There's a new rule four," said Anko. "In my notebook." She turned the page round so he could see it. In her small precise hand, at the top of a fresh page: *4. M. does not lift, carry, fire, hold, haul, climb or strain. Until the doctor says. —A.U.* Underneath, already, a line: *Day 157. The beam. (Shoko.)* And under that, newer, the pencil still sharp on it: *Day 157. The tongs. (Me.)*

"How did you know about the beam?" said Mau.

"I was on the roof," said Anko.

"Why were you on the *roof*?"

Anko turned the notebook back round and went on writing. "Rounds," she said.

Senku, with a crucible of glowing glass in his tongs, looked from one of them to the other.

"I'm just going to finish this," said Senku, "and not be involved."



He went to see the gate.

He did not mean to go in. He only meant to look at it. It had been shut for twelve days by Fern's word, and before that he had gone through it every week, and he missed it, the way you miss a road you used to walk. The blue hum of it. The light in the north field. He walked out past the end of the yard and along the path toward the north field, with his hands in his pockets, not doing anything. Not lifting. Not carrying. Just walking. There was no rule about walking.

He had got as far as the red stake when someone fell into step beside him.

"Kufufufu," said Diablo. "Mau-sama. What a pleasant morning for a walk."

Mau closed his eyes briefly.

"I'm just going to look at the gate," he said.

"Of course," said Diablo. He was wearing his butler's coat and his sash. ASSISTANT TO EVERYONE (OFFICIAL), in Umaru's crooked capitals, stitched by the fort onto a strip of red cloth that went from his shoulder to his hip. He wore it as if it were the highest honour of an ancient kingdom. "I would be delighted to accompany you. Unfortunately, the gate is closed. By Fern-sama. By decree."

"I know. I'm only going to look."

"Of course," said Diablo again. "But looking, as Gojo-sama has taught you, is the very thing we are being careful about." He smiled pleasantly. "So I thought, perhaps, instead, I might describe it to you."

Mau stopped walking.

"Describe it," he said.

"In detail," said Diablo. "Indoors. With tea." He bowed slightly. "I have looked at it every day for twelve days, on your behalf, so that you would not have to. It is very blue. It hums at a pitch somewhere between a large bee and a distant choir. On the third day a hare went past it and did not like it. On the seventh day the snow on its western side melted entirely." He paused. "I could go on. I have notes."

Mau looked at him.

"You've been looking at the gate," he said. "Every day. For me."

"I have been looking at a great many things for a great many people," said Diablo. "It is my position." The pleasant smile did not change. "Shall we go back? I believe Momo has made the small cakes. With the honey."

Mau looked at the north field. At the blue light of the gate, far off across the grass, humming. He could have walked to it. Diablo would not have stopped him. Not with his hands. That was the thing. Diablo would simply have walked beside him the whole way, pleasantly, describing it.

He turned round.

"Kufufufu," said Diablo, with deep satisfaction, and walked him home.



"Good," said Frieren.

She said it from the reading chair, without looking up from the grimoire, when he came into the east room and told her about the beam, and the tongs, and the gate.

"*Good?*"

"Good," said Frieren. She turned a page.

"They're *watching* me. All three of them. Shoko and Anko and Diablo. Separately. They're not even working together. They're just—everywhere. I can't pick anything up. I can't *walk* anywhere."

"You can walk to the bench."

"I can walk to the *bench*," said Mau, "and sit on it, and be *boring*."

Frieren looked up at him over the top of the grimoire. Her green eyes were very calm.

"Twelve days ago," she said, "you were on the dead earth in the south field with blood coming out from under a blindfold." She looked back down at the page. "Be boring."

He did not have anything to say to that.

He sat down on the end of the bed. After a while, she shifted in the reading chair, and moved her feet, so that they were in his lap. Cold. Even through the socks.

"They're not working together," said Mau, after a long time. "Yet."

Frieren turned a page.

"No," she said. "Yet."



They found each other that night. Of course they did.

It was past midnight, in the kitchen, where the stove was banked low and the room was dark and warm. Anko was there because Anko was always there at that hour. She did not sleep much. She never had. She sat at the end of the long kitchen table with her low lamp and her notebook, the way she had sat every night for two years, writing up the rounds.

Shoko came in at a quarter past. She had not slept properly since she came. Not because of the bed, which was good, or the room, which Rimuru had built to stay empty for exactly this. Because of the quiet. There was no hum of a city. No sirens. No pager. Her body kept waiting for something to call her, and nothing did, and so she lay awake, and at a quarter past midnight she gave up and came down for water.

She stopped in the doorway when she saw Anko.

Anko looked up.

The two of them regarded each other across the dark kitchen for a moment. Two tired women, in coats, at midnight. They had been in the same house for twelve days and had said perhaps twenty words to each other in all of it.

"Couldn't sleep?" said Anko.

"Never can," said Shoko.

"Me neither."

Shoko came in and sat down at the table. Not next to Anko. Across. She did not get her water. She sat there with her hands in the pockets of her white coat, and after a while, without seeming to think about it, she took one hand out. There was something in it. She turned it over in her fingers on the table, slowly, the way you turn a coin.

A cigarette. Unlit. A little bent.

Anko's pencil stopped.

She looked at it for a long moment. Then she put down the pencil, and reached into the inside pocket of her own coat, and took out a small flat tin, old and scratched, with the paint worn off the corners. She opened it. She took out what was inside and laid it on the table.

A cigarette. Unlit. Very straight. Very carefully kept.

The two of them looked at the two cigarettes on the table between them.

"*Oh,*" said Shoko.

"Yeah," said Anko.

"He told me there weren't any."

"He asked me," said Anko. "On his second day. Went round the whole house asking." She almost smiled. "I lied."

Shoko looked at her. And then she laughed properly again. The laugh came more easily now, as it had with Satoru on the fence a few nights before. Low and tired and helpless.

"Good," said Shoko.

"I came over with nine," said Anko. "In a crushed pack, in my coat. I didn't know where I was or if there'd ever be more. So I made them last. One on the first of the month. At midnight. On the night shift." She turned the tin over in her fingers. "Then a vampire walked through the door one night. A girl I used to know. I dropped one on the floor and lit another one straight after, because I needed it." She looked at the cigarette on the table. "After that there was one left. I've carried it ever since."

"What are you saving it for?"

Anko was quiet for a moment.

"Something worth it," she said.

Shoko looked at her across the table. Then she looked down at her own cigarette, the bent one, from her office, from four in the morning, from the night after she had closed a man's eyes on a metal table.

"Me too," she said.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Tea," said a voice, very softly, from the dark by the stove.

They both turned.

Diablo was standing there. Neither of them had heard him come in. He had a tray. On the tray were three cups and a pot, steaming, and a small plate of Momo's honey cakes. He set it down on the table between them, beside the two cigarettes, without looking at the cigarettes, and poured.

"I hope I do not intrude," said Diablo. "I find that I do not sleep either. I have never needed to." He set a cup in front of each of them. "It seemed a shame for there to be two people awake in the kitchen without tea."

He did not sit. Then, after a moment, as if remembering something he was still learning, he did. On the end of the bench. The third side of the table.

Shoko looked at him. Anko looked at him. He looked back, with his pleasant terrible smile.

"He tried to carry a beam today," said Shoko.

"He tried to fire a kiln," said Anko.

"He tried to visit the gate," said Diablo.

There was a pause.

"*All* today?" said Shoko.

"Before noon," said Anko.

Shoko put her face in her hands.

"He's going to be a nightmare," she said, through them.

"Yes," said Anko.

"Kufufufu," said Diablo, which was also yes.

Anko pulled her notebook toward her and turned to the page with the new rule four. She wrote, under the three lines that were already there: *Day 157. The gate. (D.)* Then she looked at it for a moment, and drew a line across the page, and wrote underneath, in very small capitals: *ROTA.*

Diablo leaned over to look.

"Shall I compile a schedule?" he said. "Rotations. Coverage. I am very good at coverage." He paused. "I stood outside one door for two years. I believe I could stand outside several."

"Shifts," said Anko. "Morning, afternoon, night."

"He'll hate it," said Shoko.

"He will be alive to hate it," said Diablo pleasantly.

Shoko looked at him across the table for a long moment.

"Why do you care?" she said. Not unkindly. With the flat clinical curiosity of a woman who opened things to see how they worked. "You're his brother's demon. Rimuru's. You told the whole house so, a week ago. You'd kill any of us for making Rimuru cry." She tilted her head. "So why him?"

Diablo was quiet for a moment.

"Mau-sama laughed on the roof once," he said. He folded his gloved hands on the table. "In the snow. It was Mau-sama and Yuta-sama and Rimuru-sama, on the ridge of the house, and Mau-sama laughed, for the first time up there. I heard it from the yard. And when Rimuru-sama came down the ladder, his face was doing something I had never seen it do in any world." He paused. "If Mau-sama dies, Rimuru-sama will cry. And I have made a very public promise about what happens to anyone who makes Rimuru-sama cry." The smile widened, very slightly. "So, logically, Mau-sama is not permitted to die. I would have to kill myself, and that would be very awkward for everyone."

Anko stared at him.

Shoko stared at him.

Then Anko started to laugh. Not loudly. Into her tea. With her shoulders shaking. And Shoko, across the table, put her hand over her eyes and laughed too, and Diablo sat between them on the end of the bench and looked very pleased with himself.

"That's the most insane reason I've ever heard," said Shoko, wiping her eyes.

"Thank you," said Diablo.

"What's yours?" said Shoko, to Anko.

Anko stopped laughing.

She looked down at her notebook. At the rota. At the new rule four. Then she put her hand flat on the open page, very still.

"I had my hand in my coat," said Anko.

Shoko waited.

"The day he first walked down to the washing court," said Anko. "After. Everyone turned. Mikasa had her hand on her blades. Okarun had his over his pocket. And I was at the red stake, on my rounds, and I had my hand in my coat." She did not say what was in her coat. She did not need to. "On him. Ready. Because that's what I'm for. I hunted things that came out at night, in my world. I'm good at it. When something comes that might be dangerous, my hand goes into my coat before I've thought about it." Her voice was perfectly level. "And he looked at us. All of us. With our hands ready. And I watched him see it."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"He didn't say anything," said Anko. "He just stopped walking. And looked. And I saw his face." She closed the notebook. "I'd rather be the one who stops him carrying a beam. I'd rather write him up every day for the rest of his life for holding a pair of tongs. I'd rather do that than ever be the one at the stake with her hand in her coat again."

Nobody said anything.

Then Shoko reached across the table and put her hand, very briefly, on top of Anko's, on the closed notebook. Just for a moment. Then took it back.

"Children," said Shoko quietly. "I patched up children for years. Fifteen. Sixteen. Satoru made them strong and I made them able to go back out, and between the two of us we were very, very good at sending children back out." She looked at the bent cigarette on the table. "Some came back. Some came back in bags, and I opened them, and wrote down what had happened to them, and signed it." She paused. "I'm not doing that here. Not with this one. Not with any of them."

"He's not a child," said Anko.

"When you've opened enough of them," said Shoko, "they're all children."



Mau came down for water at a quarter to one.

He stopped in the kitchen doorway.

Three people at the table in the dark. Anko with her notebook, very suddenly closed. Shoko with her hands in her pockets, very suddenly innocent. Diablo with a teapot, very suddenly pouring a fourth cup that had not been on the tray a moment ago. Two unlit cigarettes on the table between them, beside a plate of honey cakes, which nobody tried to hide.

They all looked at him.

"What's this?" said Mau.

"Nothing," said Shoko.

"Rounds," said Anko.

"Tea, Mau-sama?" said Diablo.

Mau looked at the three of them. At the closed notebook. At the fourth cup, steaming, already in front of the empty place on the bench. At the two cigarettes. He thought about the beam, and the tongs, and the red stake, and the gate.

He did not ask anything. He came in and sat down in the empty place, and picked up the cup, and drank.

While he drank, Shoko reached across, without seeming to think about it, and put two fingers on the inside of his wrist. The way she did at breakfast, and lunch, and dinner. She held them there, counting, looking at nothing. He let her. Her fingers were cool.

"Boring," said Shoko, after a while, quietly, and took her hand back.

He finished the tea. He said good night. He went back upstairs to the east room, where Frieren was asleep in the bed with one hand in the blanket. He got in beside her. He lay in the dark and looked at the ceiling. Just the ceiling.

*Yet,* he thought.

Downstairs, at the kitchen table, Anko opened her notebook again, and turned to the rota, and began to write in names.

Shoko and Anko put their cigarettes away. One in a scratched tin. One in the pocket of a white coat.

"I'm keeping mine intact," said Shoko. "I'll ask Rimuru whether Ciel can analyse it. Paper, tobacco, all of it. If we ever try making more, we ought to know what we're trying to make."

Anko put the lid on her tin. "Let her look at mine too."

"When they've rested," said Shoko.

Anko nodded.

Saving them.
