*When: Day 158. Anko's rota went up on the kitchen wall at breakfast, and Mau read it in complete silence; Diablo has the night shift. In the clinic, Shoko, Maomao and Kusuri have discovered each other. Kaneki is on the charcoal kilns again, nights, the way he has been all winter.*

The clinic had become a different place since the doctor came.

It had been Maomao's for two years. A long low room at the side of the real house, with clay walls washed white and a beam across the ceiling, shelves of jars and bundles and dried things that nobody else was allowed to touch, and a smell of bitter roots that got into your clothes. Maomao's bench under the window. Maomao's cot by the stove. Maomao's rules, chalked by the door, which began *1. Do not eat anything on the shelves* and ended, eleven rules later, *12. Especially the mushrooms.*

Now there were two benches, and a second cot, and a second set of rules, chalked underneath the first in very neat doctor's handwriting, which began *1. If you are bleeding, say so before you say anything else* and ended, after three rules, *4. Do not lie to me about how much it hurts. I will know.*

And there were three women in it, most mornings, instead of one.

"You're insane," said Shoko.

She said it with deep approval. She was leaning on the end of Maomao's bench with her tea, watching Maomao roll up her own sleeve, take a small grey mushroom from a dish, cut a sliver off it with a knife, and press the cut side very carefully against the inside of her forearm. Where there was already a row of small faint marks, old and new, like a ladder.

"I'm thorough," said Maomao, without looking up. She was watching the skin under the mushroom. "If it reddens in a quarter of an hour, it's the one from the river bank. If it doesn't, it's the one from the birches. They look the same. One of them stops your heart."

"And you're testing which on *yourself*."

"Who else would I test it on?"

Shoko looked at her for a long moment.

"I've worked with a lot of people who did insane things to their own bodies," she said. "On purpose. For good reasons. Most of them were sorcerers." She drank her tea. "You're the first one who was keeping notes."

Maomao glanced up at her. Something moved at the corner of her mouth that was very nearly a smile.

At the other end of the room, by the stove, Kusuri was stirring a small pot that smelled of beeswax and something green. She had been coming to the clinic since the second week of the winter, with her hand creams and her salves and her small corked bottles of things she would not explain, and Maomao had tolerated her the way a cat tolerates another cat it has decided is acceptable. Shoko had watched her for three days. On the fourth she had gone and stood beside the stove and asked, very politely, what was in the pot.

"Secret," said Kusuri, happily.

"I'm a doctor."

"Still secret," said Kusuri. "It's for hands. People here have terrible hands. All that wood and rope." She tapped the spoon on the rim. "Yours too."

Shoko looked at her own hands. They were dry, and cracked across the knuckles, from the cold and the water and the soap.

Kusuri held out a small pot, still warm.

After a moment, Shoko took it.

At the door, at a quarter past eight, Jinshi appeared, as he did every morning. With the Arrival House key on its ribbon, and the register girl's day list, and the face of a man who had come to deliver a piece of paper and would have stayed all morning if anyone had let him. Maomao did not look up from her mushroom. She held out her free hand. He put the list in it.

"Thank you," said Maomao. "Go away."

"Of course," said Jinshi. He did not go away for a moment. Then he did.

Shoko watched him go. She looked at Maomao.

"Temporary," said Maomao, to her forearm.

"Sure," said Shoko.

The skin under the mushroom did not redden. Maomao wrote it down.



Shoko noticed the young man on her fourth evening.

He walked past the clinic window every day at dusk. Always at the same time, always going the same way, out toward the charcoal kilns at the edge of the timber yard, where the night shift worked. A quiet young man in a dark coat, with his hands in his pockets and his head down. He never looked in through the window. But every evening, as he passed the clinic door, his steps changed. Just slightly. Just for three or four paces. Faster, and further from the wall. The way some people walked past a dentist's.

By her thirteenth evening, she had seen it thirteen times.

On the fourteenth, she went out after him.



The kilns were at the far edge of the timber yard, three low humps of earth and turf with smoke coming out of the vents, and a shelter of poles and bark beside them where the night shift kept its tools. Kaneki was alone. He was raking the embers in the mouth of the middle kiln with a long iron rake, slowly, in the red light, with his sleeves pushed up. There was a small pot on a flat stone at the edge of the fire, and two cups upside down on the shelf in the shelter.

He did not look round when she came up. But his shoulders changed.

"You walk past my window every evening," said Shoko.

"It's on the way," said Kaneki.

"You walk past my door like it's going to bite you."

Kaneki raked the embers.

"Most people," said Shoko, "who walk past a clinic like that have a reason. Usually a bad one. Usually a doctor." She stood at the edge of the firelight with her hands in the pockets of her white coat. "I'm not going to ask you what it is. I just wanted you to know I'd noticed. So you didn't have to keep wondering whether I had."

Kaneki stopped raking.

He stood there in the red light with the rake in his hands for a long moment. Then he leaned it against the kiln.

"The last doctor who had me on a table," he said, "put someone else's organs in me."

Shoko did not say anything.

"Without asking," said Kaneki. "I was unconscious. A steel beam fell on me and a woman I'd gone on one date with, and when I woke up, she was dead and some of her was inside me." His voice was very even. "He did it on purpose. To see what would happen. I was the experiment." He looked at the fire. "So. Doctors."

The kilns ticked and breathed in the dark.

"Well," said Shoko at last. "I only open the dead."

Kaneki looked at her.

"And I close them properly afterward," said Shoko. "With very neat stitches. Nobody's ever complained."

There was a pause.

Then Kaneki laughed. It surprised both of them. A short, startled sound, out of a face that did not do it often. He put his hand over his mouth as if he could catch it.

"That's terrible," he said.

"I know. I've had a lot of practice being terrible." She came a step closer, into the light, and sat down on the end of the bench in the shelter, uninvited. "I'm not going to ask you to come in. I'm not going to ask what you eat, either. You don't, at the table. I've noticed. Twelve days."

"Thirteen."

"Thirteen," said Shoko. "I'm not asking."

Kaneki looked at her. "Why not?"

"Because you'd tell me if it mattered. Because you've lived in this house two years and the people here know, I think, or know enough." She looked at the cups on the shelf. "And because I spent my whole life in a school full of people whose bodies were complicated. One of them was a panda. More or less. I don't scare easily."

Kaneki did not answer that. But he took the pot off the flat stone, and took one cup down from the shelf, and turned it over, and poured. Black coffee, thick, bitter, steaming in the cold. He held it out.

Shoko took it.

He took down the second cup. He looked at it for a moment, in his hand. Then he put it back on the shelf, upside down, the way it had been, and did not pour for himself.

Shoko noticed. She did not ask about that either.

She drank. It was the worst coffee she had ever had in her life and she had worked night shifts in a hospital.

"This is terrible," she said.

"I know," said Kaneki. "I make it like that on purpose."

"Why?"

He picked up the rake again.

"Because it's the same every night," he said. "Same measure. Same water. Same pot. Same cup." He raked the embers. "When everything else in your body is something you didn't ask for, it helps to have one thing your hands do that you know."

Shoko held the terrible coffee in both hands and looked at him over it for a long time.

"You were at the fence," she said. "Weren't you. In the field. When it all came out of him."

Kaneki's rake stopped.

"I wasn't there," said Shoko. "I came after. But I heard. Everyone's told me some of it." She paused. "You saw something."

Kaneki was quiet for a long moment.

"Among the shapes," he said. "All the things coming out of him. The light, and the patterns, and the shrine. There was something red." He did not look at her. "Out of his back. For a second. Segmented. Like a tail made of muscle, or a spine on the outside. It came out and went back in, so fast that I don't think anyone else saw it."

"And you know what it was."

"It was mine," said Kaneki. "Or something like mine. Something he took from being near me, once, the way he takes things." He put the rake down again. "I knew it was in there. I felt it, a long time ago, in the bad hours, when the Noise was loud. I told him then. I didn't know it could come *out*."

Shoko did not say anything.

"He's going to be afraid of his body now," said Kaneki. "Not of the wheel. Not of the eyes. Everyone's watching the wheel and the eyes. Of his *body*. Of not knowing what's in it. Of waking up and not knowing what it's going to do." He looked at his own hands, in the red light. "I know what that's like. I know exactly what that's like. I don't know if anyone else here does."

"Then go and tell him," said Shoko.

Kaneki laughed again, shorter. "I'm not good at telling people things."

"Neither am I," said Shoko. "I usually write them in a report and leave it on someone's desk." She finished the terrible coffee, every drop of it, and set the cup down on the bench. "But I'm not the one who knows this part." She stood up. "You are."

She went back across the timber yard toward the house, with her hands in her pockets, without looking back.

Kaneki stood by the kiln for a long time.

Then he picked up the cup she had left, and rinsed it, and put it back on the shelf. Upside down. Beside the other one.



Diablo had the night shift.

Anko's rota said so. It was pinned to the kitchen wall in her small precise hand. MORNING (A.U.), AFTERNOON (S.I.), NIGHT (D.), with a footnote that said *(M. may sit. M. may walk. M. may not lift, haul, climb or carry. See rule four.)* Mau had read it at breakfast in complete silence while the whole long table watched him read it, and then he had sat down and eaten his porridge and said nothing at all, and Gojo had laughed so hard he had to leave the room.

So when Kaneki came to the kitchen door at ten o'clock that night and asked if Mau could come out to the kilns for an hour, it was Diablo he asked.

Diablo considered him gravely.

"Mau-sama may sit," said Diablo. "Mau-sama may not rake."

"He won't rake."

"Mau-sama may drink," said Diablo. "Mau-sama may not shovel."

"He won't shovel."

"Kufufufu," said Diablo. "Then I will be at the edge of the timber yard. In the dark. Not listening." He bowed. "Kaneki-sama."

Kaneki looked at him for a moment. Nobody had ever called him *-sama* in his life.

"Thanks," he said.



They sat on the bench in the bark shelter, by the middle kiln, in the red light.

Kaneki made the coffee. The same way. Same measure. Same water. Same pot. He poured two cups, this time, and gave one to Mau, and kept one.

Mau took a mouthful and nearly spat it into the fire.

"That's—"

"I know."

"That's *awful*."

"It's always awful," said Kaneki. "That's the point."

They sat. The kilns breathed. Out at the edge of the timber yard, somewhere in the dark, Diablo was not listening.

"I saw it," said Kaneki, after a long time. "In the field."

Mau went still.

"Something of mine," said Kaneki. "Out of your back. For a second." He did not look at him. "I don't think anyone else saw it."

"I felt it," said Mau. His voice was very quiet. "I felt it go. Out and back. Like a hand opening and closing that wasn't a hand." He held the awful coffee in both hands and looked at the fire. "I didn't know it was in there. Not really. I knew there were things. I didn't know—" He stopped.

"What else is in there," said Kaneki.

"Yeah."

"Ciel says some of it isn't even finished. Some of it isn't even *formed*. Things I don't know about. Things I don't have names for." Mau looked at his hands. "I lie in bed and I think about it. What's in me that I don't know about. What it could do. If I got angry, or frightened, or—" He stopped again. "Frieren sleeps next to me."

Kaneki was quiet.

"When it first happened to me," he said at last, "I couldn't eat. I couldn't look at my own hands. I thought every time I got angry, something would come out of my back and kill someone I loved." He drank his coffee. "Sometimes it did."

Mau looked at him.

"I'm not going to tell you it won't," said Kaneki. "I'd be lying. You'd know. You see everything." He set his cup down on the bench between them. "I'm going to tell you what I did instead."

"What did you do?"

"I made coffee," said Kaneki. "Every night. The same way. Awful. On purpose. So there was one thing my hands did that I knew. One thing in the whole day that was mine, and that I'd chosen, and that I could do exactly the same, and that never surprised me." He looked at the pot on the flat stone. "It doesn't fix anything. It's just a place to stand. When the rest of your body's a stranger, you need one place to stand where you know where your feet are." He looked at Mau. "Find your coffee."

Mau looked at the cup in his hands.

"I don't like coffee," he said.

"Nobody likes this coffee," said Kaneki. "Find something else."

Mau sat there for a long time, by the kiln, thinking.

He had the reset hour. *One. Still here.* That was a place to stand. That was hers and his. But it was not his hands. He thought about what his hands did that he knew. That he had chosen. That never surprised him. That was his.

He put the cup down.

He held out his right hand, palm up, in the red light of the kiln. He had not done this since the bell. Since before. Since the river, and the fifth flower, and Frieren on the bank. He had been afraid to. Afraid of what might come out of his palm instead, now that everything had come out of him at once.

But the flowers were the one safe thing. Everyone said so. Ciel said so. The light RCT and the flowers, and nothing else.

He breathed out.

And on his palm, small and slow, a flower opened.

It was tiny. The size of a thumbnail. White, with five petals, plain and small, like the ones on the riverbank. It sat on his palm in the red glow of the kiln and did not do anything else. It did not grow. It did not turn into anything. It did not open a door. It was just a flower.

Mau looked at it.

His hand was shaking. Not the way Mai's shook. A different way. He let it shake. The flower stayed.

Kaneki looked at it for a long moment.

"That's a good coffee," he said quietly.

Mau laughed. It came out wet. He closed his hand very gently round the flower, not crushing it, and held it against his chest, and sat there by the kiln with his eyes shut, laughing a little, with his hand shaking and the flower inside it.

Kaneki picked up his awful coffee and drank it, and looked at the fire, and did not say anything else at all. There was nothing else to say. That was the whole of it. That was all he had.

It was enough.



From the clinic window, across the dark timber yard, Shoko watched them. Two shapes on a bench in the red light of the kilns. One of them holding out an open hand. The other leaning close to look at what was in it, very carefully, the way you lean over a candle, as if it might blow out.

She watched until the hand closed.

Then she sat down at her bench and opened the notebook she kept for her patients. She turned to a fresh page. She wrote, at the top, *K.* And underneath, after a moment:

*Not a patient. Not asking.*

And underneath that, smaller:

*Makes terrible coffee. On purpose. Good doctor.*
