*When: Day 159. The rota is on its third day and Mau has stopped arguing with it. In the Witch Hat room, Coco is drawing the outer lattice in ink, for the first time, now that the ink tree gives; at the north wing it is the week of Echidna's tea, and the ninth song is still owed.*

Coco drew the lattice in ink on the hundred-and-fifty-ninth morning.

Not in water. She had drawn it in water eighty-one times on the atelier floor, a long time ago, in the deep of the winter, with a drop of water on the end of a pen, until the last ten were right. Water was for practice. Water dried. Water forgave you.

Ink did not forgive you.

She had a square of pale stone on the table in front of her, the size of her hand, smooth, from the river. She had a pen. She had the small glass bottle from the end of the row on the Witch Hat shelf, which had been empty for two years except for a stain at the bottom, and which was full now, dark and real, because the ink tree in the yard gave a bottle a week from the notch in its bark.

She had been sitting in front of the stone for a quarter of an hour without drawing anything.

"You're going to dry out the nib," said Agott, from her alcove, without looking up from her own work.

"I know."

"Then *draw*."

"I *know*," said Coco.

Across the room, Tetia was on her bed with her chin in her hands, watching, with the bright anxious expression of someone who very badly wants something to go well for a friend. Richeh was on her own bed, under the window, mostly asleep, with a half-finished glyph on a scrap of paper on her stomach that she had been drawing for a week and would probably finish when she felt like it. Qifrey was at the door. He had come up from the slide checks to see. He did not come in. He stood in the doorway with his arms folded and his eyes on his student, and did not say anything, because there was nothing a teacher could say at a moment like this that would not make it worse.

Coco dipped the pen.

She drew.

It went the way it went in water. That was the strange thing. Her hand knew it. Eighty-one times in water and the last ten right, and her hand had not forgotten any of it. The outer lattice. Every line of the binding, reversed. Every angle that closed, opened. The shape of an undoing, spreading out from the centre of the stone in fine dark lines, until it filled the square almost to the edges.

She lifted the pen.

The ink sat on the stone, wet and black.

Nothing happened for a moment.

Then the stone changed. Not much. Just a little, at the surface. Where the lines touched it, the grey went a shade lighter, a shade softer, like a thing that had been clenched for a very long time unclenching very slightly. A faint sound came off it. Not a crack. A sigh.

Tetia sat up on her bed.

"Did it—"

"It did something," said Coco. Her voice was very small.

Agott had put down her own pen. She came out of her alcove and stood behind Coco's chair and looked at the stone over her shoulder for a long time, with her arms folded and her face severe.

"It's right," said Agott at last.

Coco did not move.

"Every line," said Agott. "I checked. It's right." She paused. Then, as if it cost her something: "It's very good, Coco."

On the bed under the window, Richeh opened one eye. "Is it done? Is she done? Can I go back to sleep?"

"You *are* asleep," said Agott.

"Then I'm doing very well," said Richeh, and closed the eye.

At the door, Qifrey had not moved. But his arms had come unfolded.



It worked on stone.

She tried it on glass in the afternoon, with Wakana's offcut from the new east window, and it worked on glass. A faint softening, a sigh. She tried it on ice, on a bird of frozen water that Tetia shaped for her in a bowl in the yard, very carefully, with a drainage glyph and a cold glyph and her tongue between her teeth. It worked on the ice bird. The bird sighed and went soft at the edges and then, very slowly, was water again, in the shape of a bird, for a moment, before it lost the shape and was only water.

Everyone in the yard who saw it clapped. Momo, from the kitchen door. Senku, from the lab, who said *ten billion percent* in a voice that was trying not to be impressed. Gojo and Mau from the bench under the ink tree, both blindfolded, both clapping at the wrong moment because neither of them could see it, Gojo very loudly, Mau a beat late because he was listening to everyone else to know when.

It worked on stone and glass and a bird made of ice.

It would not work on a person.

Coco knew that. She had known it since the corridor, in the snow, with the pale book in Echidna's hands. *Because the person was alive. Inside. Still. In a way the stone was not.*

She sat on the edge of the well with the wet bowl in her lap, where the ice bird had been, and everyone went back to what they had been doing, and she looked at the bowl for a long time.



Echidna's tea was on the hundred-and-fifty-ninth evening. It always was, that week, in the north wing, in the room with the long low table and the cushions and the seven cups, and the door to the room with no name standing a little open at the back, with the library behind it.

Coco went with Qifrey. She brought the stone.

Echidna looked at it across the table for a long time without touching it. Then she picked it up, and turned it over in her pale fingers, and looked at the lines from every side. The other witches watched. Pride, very straight. Wrath, with her bandaged hands folded. Sloth, half lying on her cushion, half watching. Satella, at the end, quiet.

"In ink," said Echidna.

"Yes."

"The outer undoing. Complete." She set the stone down. "The woman in the other world did this. She drew it, and drew it, and it worked on stone and on glass and on water frozen into the shape of a bird. You have done in one winter what she did in a lifetime." She looked at Coco. There was something in her face that was not quite approval and not quite hunger. "And now you have reached the place where she stopped."

Coco did not say anything.

"The centre," said Echidna.

"I know."

"You cannot draw the centre without knowing the shape the binding took inside the one it bound. Exactly. The order. The way it moved through them." Echidna turned her cup slowly on the table. "You cannot know that from outside. No book holds it. No spell reads it. It is different for every living thing." She paused. "You would have to know it from inside."

"I know," said Coco again. Smaller.

Echidna looked at her for a long moment.

"There is a thing in this house," said Echidna, "that learns from the inside."

Qifrey went very still beside Coco.

"You saw it," said Echidna. Her voice did not change. She said it the way she said everything, quietly, precisely, as if reading from a page. "Over the south field. Fourteen days ago. A wheel. Pale. Turning. Every turn, a wall." She lifted her cup. "I have read about such things. In the library behind that door, there are three books that mention a shikigami that adapts. That turns its wheel, and with each turn, learns the shape of what is hurting it. From inside. Until it is no longer hurt." She drank. "I do not know where it came from. But I watched it turn, from the bottom of the slope, with my sisters, while we held the light. And I thought: *that* is the shape of the thing she never found."

"No," said Qifrey.

He said it very quietly. Everyone at the table looked at him.

Qifrey did not raise his voice. He had his hand flat on the table beside his cup. His face was perfectly pleasant. His eyes were not.

"I did not suggest anything," said Echidna mildly. "I observed."

"Observe something else," said Qifrey.

The room was very quiet. At the end of the table, Satella had lowered her eyes. Wrath's bandaged hands had tightened. Pride looked from Qifrey to Echidna and back, with the expression of a woman who has seen many arguments at many tables and is deciding whether this one is going to become a problem.

Echidna looked at Qifrey for a long moment.

Then she inclined her head. Very slightly.

"As you wish," she said. "I withdraw the observation." She set down her cup. "But I do not unmake it. It was true when I said it. It will still be true tomorrow." She turned to Coco. "You will decide. Not him. Not me. You. That is how it is with the things we want most." She folded her pale hands. "That is the only thing I have ever been sure of."

Coco did not say anything at all.

She sat at the long low table with the stone in front of her and her hands in her lap. She did not look at Qifrey. She did not look at Echidna. She looked at the lines on the stone, the fine dark lattice, opening outward, with nothing in the middle. And she thought about a wheel turning over a field full of light. About every click of it, a wall. About Mau on the dead earth with his fingers in the dirt and blood coming out from under a blindfold. About Fern at the long table the next morning, with the ledger open, saying *everything stops*.

She thought about her mother.

About a figure of clear crystal in a room in another world, in a house by a stream, with its hand still lifted, the way it had been lifted on the last afternoon, reaching for something. About a coin in the inside pocket of Qifrey's coat, on the left side, over his heart, that had been waiting there for a long time. That would bring her mother here, if Coco asked. Crystal and all. Hand still lifted.

To a house where nobody yet knew how to undo it.

Except, perhaps, one thing. Which turned, and learned, and broke walls.

Coco stood up.

"Thank you for the tea," she said, to Echidna, very politely. "I have to go now."

She picked up the stone and went out.



Qifrey found her in the Witch Hat room an hour later.

She was at the table under the candle with her private notebook open in front of her. The page with the two lattices, the outer one and the small one nested inside it, with the empty centre. And beside the empty centre, very small, the word she had written on a snowy night a long time ago, and looked at for a long time, and not crossed out.

*Mau?*

She was not crossing it out now either. She was writing underneath it. Qifrey stood in the doorway and did not come closer. He did not need to read it. He could see her hand moving, slowly, carefully, a child's careful letters.

When she had finished, she put down the pen and sat back.

"I'm not going to ask him," said Coco.

Qifrey did not say anything.

"Not now," said Coco. "Not while Fern says everything stops. Not while his walls are tired. Not while he has to wear a blindfold four hours a day and Diablo walks behind him and Shoko takes his pulse at every meal." She was looking at the page. "I saw it. The field. I was at the window. I saw what happens when the wheel turns." Her voice was quite steady. "I'm not going to ask him to turn it. For me. Not while it can do that."

She was quiet for a moment.

"I asked him once," she said. "In the winter. On the atelier floor, the day the ink came back. I showed him this page, and I asked if he thought he could be the one who finds the missing piece." She touched the word in the margin. "He said he didn't know. He said if he was, he'd do everything he could." Her voice did not change. "And he would. Everything. That's exactly why I can't ask him now."

"Coco," said Qifrey quietly.

"Mother's waited this long," said Coco. "She's crystal. She doesn't know how long it's been. She doesn't know anything." Her voice wobbled, very slightly, and then was steady again. "She can wait a bit more. Until Fern says. Until he's not tired." She paused. "Until I find another way. Or until there isn't one and he's strong enough and *he* says yes. Not because I asked. Because he wants to."

Qifrey came into the room then.

He crossed to the table and stood behind her chair and put his hand on her head, very lightly, the way he had not done since she was much smaller and had first come to the atelier with ink on her fingers and her whole world turned to crystal behind her. He did not say anything. He did not trust himself to.

He looked down at the page.

Under *Mau?*, in her careful letters, she had written:

*Not until Fern says.*

And under that, smaller:

*And not unless he wants to.*

And under that, smaller still, so small he almost could not read it:

*Find another way first.*

Qifrey stood behind her chair with his hand on her head for a long time. In his coat, on the left side, over his heart, the coin sat where it had always sat. Warm. Waiting.

He had been a great many things, in his life, in his world. He had wanted things very badly. He knew exactly what it was to have the shape of what you wanted held out in front of you, across a table, by someone who did not care what it cost. And to want it anyway.

He looked at what his student had written, and was prouder of her than he had ever been of any spell.



Mau was in the corridor outside the Witch Hat room when Qifrey came out.

He was sitting on the floor with his back against the wall, the way Yuka had sat outside the east room. In the black blindfold. Not because it was one of his hours. Because he had put it on himself, at the end of the corridor, so that he would not be able to see her door. He had his knees drawn up. He did not seem to be doing anything. He was just sitting there in the dark corridor, blindfolded, listening.

Qifrey stopped.

"How long have you been there?" he said quietly.

"A while," said Mau. "Diablo said I could sit." He turned his blindfolded face toward the Witch Hat door. "I heard her come back from the tea. She was walking the way you walk when something's happened." He paused. "I didn't come in. I didn't look." He touched the cloth over his eyes. "I couldn't, anyway."

Qifrey looked down at him.

At the young man on the floor of the corridor, blindfolded, who had died four hundred and thirty-seven times in a white place, and come back, and remembered. Who had a wheel asleep in his shadow that turned and learned and broke walls. Who had sat outside a child's door in the dark because he had heard her walk past and known something was wrong, and had not come in, and had not looked.

"She's all right," said Qifrey. "She made a decision. A good one."

Mau nodded.

"Whatever it is," he said. "When she's ready. When Fern says." He did not move. "She can ask me."

"I know," said Qifrey. "She knows." He paused. "That's why she won't. Yet."

Mau was quiet for a moment, in the blindfold.

"Okay," he said.

Qifrey left him there. When he looked back from the end of the corridor, the Witch Hat door had opened a hand's width, and Coco was sitting on the floor just inside it, on her side of the wall, with her back against it. Not talking. Not asking. Just there. With Mau on the other side, blindfolded, also just there.

Neither of them said anything.

They sat like that, on either side of the wall, until Diablo came down the corridor with a lamp to say it was late, and Coco closed the door, and Mau did not take off the blindfold until he was round the corner.
