*When: Day 160, and the night after. Ori has not slept through a night since the bell. Milim, her trial-run roommate, still floats in her sleep, and Ori still ties the red ribbon round her ankle and the other end to the bedpost so she doesn't drift into the corridor. Gojo, Shoko and Yuka are all in rooms of their own now; the house has stopped counting its new people and started counting its old ones.*

Ori counted his breaths.

She did it every night. She had done it every night since the bell, sixteen nights now, standing in the door between her room and the east room in the dark, with one hand on the frame. The door was always a hand's width open. It had been since the move, since Frieren said *draw a door* and Rimuru drew one. Ori's side, Milim asleep and floating a hand's breadth above the bed, tethered by the red ribbon. Their side, the reading chair and the shelf in the middle of the floor and the cars on the sill, and the bed, and the two shapes in it.

She counted.

In. Out. One. In. Out. Two.

She did not know why she counted. She only knew that on the first night, the night of the bell, when he had been in the clinic with the blindfold on and nobody was in the east room at all, she had stood in this door and looked at the empty bed and not been able to breathe. And on the fourth night, when he came home, she had stood here again and heard him breathing, and the counting had started by itself. And it had not stopped.

Some nights she counted to three hundred. Some nights to a thousand. Some nights she counted until the reset hour, when Frieren's foot moved under the blanket and Frieren said, very quietly, *One*, and Mau said, *Still here*, and then Ori could go back to bed and sleep for an hour before dawn.

She did not think anyone knew.

Frieren knew.



At breakfast on the hundred-and-sixtieth morning, Ori's hair went the wrong colour.

It was only for a moment. She was reaching for the bread, across the long table, and she was tired, so tired that the room had a soft grey edge to it, and something in her slipped. Like a foot on a wet stair. Her hair, which was the colour it always was, went for one instant pale, and then dark, and then a colour that was not a colour at all, a flicker like a lamp guttering in a draught. And then it was her hair again.

Nobody saw.

Almost nobody. Down the table, Shoko had stopped with her cup halfway to her mouth.

Ori took the bread and sat back and ate it without looking at anyone. Her hands were not quite steady. She finished, and got up, and took her plate to the kitchen, and went out through the back door into the yard, very fast, without saying anything.

Shoko put her cup down.

She looked down the table at Maomao.

"Her hair," said Shoko, very quietly. "Just now. It—"

"I know," said Maomao. She had seen it too. She was looking at the back door. "It does that."

"Hair doesn't *do* that."

"Hers does," said Maomao. She put down her spoon. "She wasn't born. She was made. By the Goddess, the one who answers the coins. In a forest, out of nothing, to see what love was." She said it flatly, the way she said everything, as if reading a label on a jar. "She didn't have anything before that. No family. No world. No memories. When she first came here she flickered all the time. Every time she was frightened, or confused, or didn't know who she was. Like a lamp." She paused. "It stopped. Last year. She got sure of herself." She looked at the back door again. "It hasn't happened in a year."

Shoko was quiet for a moment.

"What does it mean," she said, "when it comes back?"

"It means she's not sure again," said Maomao. "About something."

Shoko took out her notebook. She wrote, under a fresh heading, *O.* Then she sat and looked at the letter for a long time and did not write anything else, because she did not know what to write. She did not know this patient. She did not know anyone in this house, not really, not yet. Fifteen days. She had known Satoru since they were both fifteen, and she was still learning things about him.

At the other end of the bench, Frieren had already got up.



Ori was at the river.

She was sitting on the bank, halfway to the weir, on the new grass, in the middle of the ring of small white flowers that Mau had made on the last night of the decree. They were still there. A little bent. All of them but one. She sat in the middle of them with her knees drawn up and something in her hands. A small grey stone, from a tidepool. It had sat on the east windowsill for a long time between the two cars. She had taken it that morning without asking.

She heard Frieren coming. She did not look round.

Frieren sat down beside her on the grass. Not in the ring of flowers. At the edge of it. With her hands in the pocket of the orange hoodie. She looked at the river.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"You count," said Frieren.

Ori's hands tightened on the stone.

"At night," said Frieren. "At the door. His breaths." She did not look round. "Since the bell. Sixteen nights."

"You heard."

"Elves," said Frieren. "Ears."

Ori did not say anything for a long time.

The river went past. Somewhere upstream, at the weir, Stark and Eren were shouting about a log.

"I don't have a before," said Ori.

Frieren waited.

"Everyone here has a before," said Ori. "Even if they don't want it. Even if it hurts. They have a world, and people in it, and a coin they can hold and think of someone." She turned the stone over in her fingers. "Gojo came because Yuta loved him. Shoko came because Seiko gave Gojo her coin. Yuka came because Maki knew her name. Every day this month, someone's before has come through the door." She paused. "I don't have one. Before is a place I didn't have."

"I know," said Frieren.

"All I have is after," said Ori. "And all of my after is here. In this house. In that room. In the door between." Her voice was very steady. "He was my first. The first thing I ever saw that I wanted to look at. The first person I knew. Everything I am started with him finding me in a forest." She stopped. "And on the night of the bell, I stood in that door and looked at the empty bed. And I thought: if he doesn't come back—"

Her hair flickered.

Pale. Dark. Not-a-colour. Gone.

Frieren saw it. She did not say anything about it.

"If he doesn't come back," said Ori, very quietly, "I don't know what I am."

The river went past.

Frieren was quiet for a long time. She sat at the edge of the ring of flowers with her hands in her pocket and looked at the water. When she spoke, her voice was very even.

"You'd have had me," said Frieren.

Ori looked at her.

"If he hadn't come back," said Frieren. She did not look round. "You'd have had me. You'd have had Fern, and Milim, and the fort, and the whole of the long table. But you'd have had me." She paused. "And I'd have had you."

Ori did not say anything.

"I've lost a great many people," said Frieren. "I'm very old. I've lost everyone I travelled with, and everyone I taught, and everyone who taught me. I know exactly what it is to stand in a door and look at an empty bed." She was quiet for a moment. "And the thing I have learned, in a thousand years, is that what you are does not stop when they stop. It goes on. It's terrible. It goes on anyway." She turned her head at last and looked at Ori, sitting in the middle of the flowers with the stone in her hands. "You're not only his after, Ori. You're yours. You've been yours for a long time. Since the day you told me you lived *with* us, not next to us, and I told you to draw a door."

Ori's eyes were very bright.

"And if the worst thing had happened," said Frieren, "I'd have stood in that door with you. On my side. And you'd have stood on yours. And neither of us would have known what we were for a while." She looked back at the river. "And then we'd have found out. Together. Because that's what we'd have had left."

Ori put her face down on her knees.

She did not cry loudly. She never had. She sat in the ring of white flowers on the riverbank with her forehead on her knees and the stone in her hands and shook, a little. Frieren did not touch her. Frieren sat beside her, at the edge of the flowers, with her hands in her pocket, and was there. That was all. She was there.

After a while, Ori's hair stopped flickering.

After a longer while, she lifted her head.

"Can I keep counting?" said Ori. Her voice was rough. "Not because I'm frightened. I don't think I'm frightened any more. Just—" She stopped. "Sometimes. Some nights. Because I like knowing."

Frieren considered this.

"You can say it too," she said.

Ori looked at her.

"At the reset hour," said Frieren. "When I say *one*. You can say it too. From your side of the door." She paused. "You don't have to come in. It's your side. That's what the door's for. But you can say it."

"Say what?"

"Whatever comes next," said Frieren.

She got up. She brushed the grass off the hoodie. She looked down at Ori, sitting in the middle of Mau's flowers with the tidepool stone, and something moved in her serene face, very small, very quick.

"Put that back on the sill when you're done," said Frieren. "Between the cars. He'll notice it's gone."

"He won't look."

"He'll notice," said Frieren. "He always notices." And she went back up the bank toward the house.



Ori put the stone back on the sill that afternoon. Between the cars. Exactly where it had been.

Mau noticed. He came into the east room in the evening and stopped in front of the window and looked at it for a moment, the stone between the 1988 McLaren and the 2004 Ferrari, sitting a fraction to the left of where it had sat that morning. He did not ask. He did not look harder than that. He just looked at it, the way you look at a stone, and then he turned round and looked at the door between the rooms, which was open a hand's width, as always.

He went and stood in it.

Ori was on her bed, on her side, with Milim floating asleep above the next bed and the red ribbon taut from her ankle to the post. Ori's eyes were open. She was looking at him.

"Hi," said Mau.

"Hi."

"You moved the stone."

"I put it back."

"You put it back a bit to the left."

Ori almost smiled. "You looked."

"I noticed," said Mau. "It's different." He leaned on the frame, on his side of the door. "Frieren told me. That you count."

Ori went still on the bed.

"I'm not angry," said Mau. "I wanted to say—" He stopped. He did not know how to say it. He tried again. "I don't have a before either."

Ori looked at him.

"I don't remember anything," said Mau. "From before I woke up here. Nothing. No family. No world. Nobody I could hold a coin and think of. When the coins came, I didn't know who I'd ask for." He looked at the floor between them. At the line where his room ended and hers began. "Everyone I love is already here. That's what I said. Everyone I've ever loved is in this house. In that room. And in this one." He looked up. "I didn't have a before, Ori. I only had after. Same as you. That's why I found you. I think. In the forest. I think something in me knew what it was to start from nothing."

Ori sat up.

"So count," said Mau. "If you want. Every night. As long as you want." He paused. "I'll breathe."

Ori did not say anything for a long time.

"Okay," she said at last.

"Okay."

He went back to his side of the door. He did not close it.



At the reset hour, an hour before dawn on the hundred-and-sixty-first day, Frieren woke the way she always woke now. All at once. Listening.

Mau was breathing beside her. Slow and even. Asleep. And from the door between the rooms, open a hand's width, so faint that only elves could have heard it, someone else was breathing too. Awake. Counting.

Frieren moved her foot under the blanket. Cold. Against his leg.

He woke. He always woke for that.

"One," said Frieren, quietly, into the dark.

There was a pause.

And from the door, from the other side, from a girl lying on her bed with a red ribbon in her hand and a dragon floating asleep above the next bed, very quietly, a little uncertain, as if trying out a word she had not been sure she was allowed to say:

"Two."

Mau lay very still in the dark.

Then he smiled. He could not help it. He turned his head toward the door, toward the hand's width of darker dark where the girl on the other side was listening.

"Still here," said Mau.

"Still here," said Frieren, at the same moment.

Silence. The house asleep round them. The east window, with its new clear glass, grey with the first of the dawn.

And then, from the next room, from somewhere about a hand's breadth above the second bed, in a sleepy, cheerful, enormously self-satisfied mumble, from someone who had not been awake for any of it:

"*Three.*"

There was a pause.

Ori made a sound that was not quite a laugh. It was the place a laugh would go. Frieren pressed her face into Mau's shoulder and shook. And Mau lay on his back in the dark between the two of them, the elf and the door, with a dragon floating somewhere beyond, and laughed until the window was white.
