*When: Day 162, seventeen days after the bell. Shoko wrote two words in the ledger last night and Fern has not read them yet. The fort's roster says thirty, three times a day, and so does the house's. Anko's rota has never once been needed after dark. In the low woods, it has started to smell of wet paper again, and nobody has noticed yet.*

Fern read it at breakfast.

She had the ledger open on the long table in front of her, as she did every morning, with her pen beside it and her tea going cold. The page with *Everything stops* at the top, in her own small upright hand, seventeen days old. Under it, the doctor's entries, one a day, in very neat handwriting. *M.: pulse 61, slept 7 hrs, walls (per Ciel) holding. Boring.* *M.: pulse 60, slept 8 hrs, walls holding. Very boring.* *M.: lifted a beam (one end). Stopped. Boring otherwise.*

And at the bottom, last night's, in the same neat hand, two words, underlined twice:

*Boring enough.*

*—S.I.*

Fern looked at it for a long time.

The long table had gone quiet round her. It did that, in the mornings, when Fern looked at the ledger for longer than usual. Everyone had learned to feel it. Down at the cold-hearth end, Gojo had stopped talking in the middle of a sentence. Shoko, beside him, was drinking her tea and looking at nothing, with the face of a woman who knew exactly what Fern was reading because she had written it.

"Ciel," said Fern.

Mau blinked. He had not expected that.

"I'd like to hear it from her," said Fern, without looking up. "If she'll say it."

In his head, very clearly, Ciel spoke. Not to him. Through him. He felt her ask, and he nodded, and said it aloud, because that was the rule. Her words in his mouth.

"*The walls are not tired today,*" said Mau, for her. "*They are only walls. I have checked them every hour for seventeen days. The filter holds. The wheel is asleep. The dog is asleep. Nothing is reaching for anything.*" A pause. Then, in a slightly different tone, still hers: "*He is extremely boring. I would like that on the record.*"

Gojo made a sound into his tea.

Fern picked up her pen.

She did not cross out *Everything stops*. Mau watched her not do it. She left it there, in her small upright hand, at the top of the page, so that anyone who opened the ledger in a year or ten years would see it and know that it had happened. Under the doctor's two words, she drew a line across the page. Under the line, she wrote. Slowly. In the same small upright hand.

*Some things start.*

She set down the pen. She looked up at the long table.

"The gate opens," said Fern. "Today. The party goes in this afternoon. The usual list." She looked at Maki, who sat up very straight. "Gojo's lessons continue. The blindfold goes to two hours a day." She looked at Shoko, who nodded once. "Mau may lift." A pause. "One end."

"*Anything?*" said Mau.

"One end," said Fern. "Of things Anko approves."

At the end of the table, Anko was already writing. *Rule four, amended. M. may lift ONE END. (A.U. approves which.)*

"No taming," said Fern. "Still. Not yet." She looked down the bench at Yuka, between her grandparents, with the white dog under the table.

Yuka nodded. Not fast this time. Not guiltily. She nodded slowly, with her chin up, and grinned, very slightly, at Mau across the table. And Mau grinned back.

"And the rota?" said Diablo pleasantly, from the door.

"Stays," said Anko, without looking up.

Mau put his head down on the table.

The long table laughed. It laughed the way it had laughed at Diablo's meeting, the helpless enormous laugh, but easier, now. Not the first laugh after something terrible. Just a laugh. The fort cheered behind its curtain, because the fort cheered at most things. Milim stood on the bench and shouted that the gate was open, the gate was *open*, as if nobody had heard. Senku said *ten billion percent* at nobody in particular. Fern picked up her cold tea and drank it and did not smile, quite. But the corner of her mouth moved.

*Some things start.*



The gate party went in at noon. Maki first, with her spear, scarlet with happiness. Mikasa behind her. Yuta, Stark, Rem with her flail, Aira. The blue light of the gate in the north field went brighter as they stepped into it, and humming, and they were gone, and the north field was empty and humming and blue, the way it had been every week before the bell.

Gojo stood at the red stake watching them go, with his hands in his pockets.

"I'm on the list," said Gojo.

"You're on the list for next week," said Fern, beside him.

"I've been in this world a *month*. I've never seen the inside of a dungeon. I'm the strongest sorcerer of my generation and I've never seen a *mimic*."

"Next week," said Fern.

Gojo looked down at her. Fern looked straight ahead at the gate.

"Is it because I'm the strongest?" said Gojo. "Is that why? Because you're worried I'll break it?"

"It's because you'll show off," said Fern, "and Maki will hit you, and then I'll have to write it down."

Gojo opened his mouth. He closed it. Behind them, on the path, Shoko laughed out loud.



It was an ordinary afternoon after that. That was the thing. It was the first ordinary afternoon in seventeen days, and everyone in the house seemed to know it, and nobody said so.

Wakana and Marin hung the new glass in the east window. Wakana on the ladder, Marin on the floor holding it steady, both of them very careful and very quiet, until the pane went into its frame with a soft click and was simply a window again. Marin put her hand flat on the small of Wakana's back as he came down the ladder, and left it there, and neither of them said anything about it. Nobody in the east room said anything about it either. Frieren, in the reading chair, turned a page.

In the music room, before the afternoon session, Kita sat behind Bocchi on the floor and did her hair. Two plaits. Slowly. Bocchi sat very still with her guitar across her knees and her face pink, and Ryo watched from the drum stool and said nothing, for once, and Nijika pretended to tune something.

At the clinic door, at a quarter past four, Jinshi came with the evening list. Maomao held out her hand without looking up. He put the list in it. She did not say *go away*. She said, "There's tea. If you want." And then went back to her mortar as if she had said nothing at all. Jinshi stood in the doorway for a long, astonished moment. Then he sat down on the end of the second bench, very carefully, the way you sit down beside a cat that has just decided to let you, and Shoko, at the other bench, poured him a cup without a word, and did not look at Maomao, and Maomao did not look at her.

On the bench under the ink tree, Okarun sat down beside Mau.

He did not say anything for a while. Turbo Granny was on his shoulder, very small, in her lucky-cat body, with her arms folded.

"Man," said Okarun at last. "You okay?"

"Yeah," said Mau.

"Like, really?"

"Really."

Okarun nodded. He looked at his hands. "Last time," he said. "When you went missing. I came looking for you. I didn't think, I just went." He paused. "This time Fern locked us in the cellars. All of us. The bell rang and she had the doors shut before I'd even stood up." He looked at the yard. "I couldn't come. I wanted to. I sat in the cellar with my hand on my pocket and I couldn't come."

"You held her still," said Mau. He nodded at Turbo Granny. "I saw. Afterward. At the washing court. You had your hand over her so she wouldn't—" He stopped. "You were looking after something. That counts."

Okarun looked at him.

"She bit me," said Okarun. "In the cellar. Twice."

"You deserved it," said Turbo Granny.

"I was *protecting* you."

"You were *squashing* me." She looked at Mau. Something in her small cat face changed, very slightly. "He was shaking, the whole time. In the cellar. Like a leaf. Because of you." She sniffed. "Barely acceptable, the pair of you."

Okarun went red. Mau looked at him.

"Thanks," said Mau.

"Shut up," said Okarun, and bumped his shoulder, and they sat there.

In the big room, in the five's kotatsu, under the blanket, where nobody could see unless they lifted it, Karane was holding Hakari's hand. She had been holding it, under the blanket, more or less every afternoon since the red stake on the last dating day. Nobody had said anything about it. Hakari talked more than ever, louder than ever, about anything and everything, so that nobody would notice how still her left hand was. Across the kotatsu, Nano had her schedule out. Under the column for afternoons, in Shizuka's careful writing, beneath Nano's, there was a new line, repeated every day for three weeks:

*Further observation. (S.)*

Nano had not crossed it out. Nano crossed out everything that was not on schedule. She had not crossed this out.

Kusuri, at the end of the kotatsu, put a small pot of hand cream on the table in front of each of them, and said nothing, and smiled.



At sunset, Nazuna woke up.

She came down from the loft over Anko's bed the way she always came down at sunset, which was through the window, flying, in a long lazy curve over the yard, with her hair everywhere and her coat open and her face pale and cheerful and completely unrepentant about having slept through the entire day. She landed on the back step. She came into the common room yawning. She looked down the long table, where dinner was just being laid, and saw a plate at the cold-hearth end with two of Momo's honey cakes on it, and took one, and ate it in two bites.

"Hey," said Gojo. "That's—"

"You had two," said Nazuna, through it.

"I had *two* because I *wanted* two—"

Nazuna took the other one.

Gojo reached for it. His hand stopped an inch from hers. Not because of Infinity. Because Anko had come in from her rounds and was standing in the door with her notebook, looking at both of them with an expression of deep weariness.

"Nazuna," said Anko.

"He had two," said Nazuna, with her mouth full.

Anko looked at her old friend for a long moment. Then she sat down at the end of the table and opened her notebook and wrote something, and Nazuna, behind her, leaned over her shoulder to read it upside down, and laughed so hard she choked on the honey cake, and Anko did not look round and did not smile and very slightly, at the corner of her mouth, did.



At dinner, Gojo stood on the bench.

He did it in the middle of the meal, with his cup in his hand and his blindfold on, while the long table was at its loudest. He stood up on the bench at the cold-hearth end, unfolding to his full ridiculous height, and banged his cup on the beam overhead until the noise died.

"A toast," said Gojo.

"Get down," said Shoko.

"A *toast*," said Gojo, "to my apprentice."

Mau put his hand over his face.

"Who was *boring*," said Gojo, "for *seventeen days*. Which, for a sorcerer of his calibre, is a feat beyond anything I personally have ever achieved. Who has been prescribed by a *doctor* to be *less* interesting. Who wears the right black. Who knows how many steps it is from his door to this room. Forty-one. Forty-three if Ori's boots are out." He raised his cup. "To the Lighthouse. To my apprentice. To *some things starting*."

"*Some things starting*," said the long table, raggedly, and drank, and laughed, and the fort banged its cups on the toll booth platform.

From her high seat, over the top of her paper fan, Seiko looked down at the tall white-haired man standing on her bench with his cup in the air.

Then she looked across the long table. At Frieren.

Frieren was already looking at Seiko.

The two of them regarded each other across the length of the common room for a long, perfectly still moment. The old woman with the fan who judged everyone. The elf who had watched everyone for a thousand years. Neither of them said anything. Neither of them needed to. A whole conversation passed between them, without a word, over the top of Gojo's head. It began with *that man* and ended somewhere near *well*, and in the middle there was a great deal of quiet, deep, mutual assessment.

"Hmph," said Seiko, at last, and lifted her fan.

"Mm," said Frieren, and went back to her soup.

Gojo, still on the bench, felt it. He turned his blindfolded face from one of them to the other.

"Did I just get judged?" he said.

"Yes," said Shoko. "Get down."



After dinner, Eren and Mikasa walked to the north end, the way they did most evenings now, and stood at the place where the street ran out into the fields and the road began. Mikasa looked at the road. Eren looked at the hills. Neither of them said anything. Once, Mikasa glanced at him, and he did not see it. Once, he glanced at her, and she did not see it. And they stood there until the sun had gone down behind the black hills and the first lamps were coming on behind them, and then they walked back together, not touching, half a step apart. The half step that was always there.

At the vermilion columns, at the gate of the real house, Himmel was standing in the dusk, facing the road, the way he always stood. Mau came out with two cups of tea and gave him one.

Himmel looked at it, surprised. Then took it.

They stood between the red columns and drank, and looked at the road. It was very quiet.

"She's sleeping better," said Himmel, after a while.

"Yeah," said Mau.

"Good," said Himmel.

That was all. That was the whole of it. Mau did not know what Himmel knew. Himmel did not say. They stood there, the hero and the other one, between the columns, with their tea, facing the road, in the last of the light. After a while Himmel shifted his weight and put one hand on his hip and lifted his chin slightly, toward the horizon, with his cape stirring in the evening wind.

"Nice pose," said Mau.

Himmel turned his head. "You didn't look."

"I heard your cape," said Mau.

Himmel stared at him. Then, slowly, he started to laugh. And Mau, who had spent two years being careful round this man, and had never once known what to say to him, laughed too.



Fern was at the long table, alone, when the common room had emptied, with the ledger open and the lamp turned low.

Mau came down for water. Of course he did. He stopped by the table.

Fern did not look up. "You were boring for seventeen days," she said.

"I know."

"I counted."

"I know you did."

She turned back a page. *Everything stops.* She looked at it for a moment. Then she turned forward again, to *Some things start*, and to the list under it, which was longer now. The gate. The lessons. *One end.*

"Don't make me write it again," said Fern. Very quietly.

"I'll try."

"Don't *try*," said Fern. She looked up at him then. Her violet eyes were very steady and very tired. "*Don't.*"

Mau looked at her.

He did not say *okay*. He did not say *I promise*. He knew her too well. She would write it down.

"Umaru offered me a division," said Fern, after a while. She looked back down at the ledger. "This afternoon. In the fort. She said there was Little Sister, Teleport Division, and she was First, and there was room. She asked if I wanted one."

"What did you say?"

"I said no," said Fern.

"Why?"

Fern turned a page. She ran her finger down the column of names, the long column, everyone in the house, every room, every bed, every person the house had in it. She did it the way she did it every night. Counting.

"Because I'm not your little sister," said Fern. "I'm not anyone's little sister. I'm the one who keeps the ledger." She paused at a name. His. She left her finger there for a moment. "Somebody has to count you all."

Mau did not say anything.

He put his hand on the top of her head. Once. Lightly. The way Frieren did it, sometimes, when Fern was not expecting it.

Fern went completely still.

Then she went pink. All the way to her ears.

"Go to *bed*," said Fern.

He went to bed.



Anko did her rounds at midnight.

She always did. North field, red stake, river path, the last cottage, the edge of the line. The gate was humming in the dark, blue and quiet, with the party back through it hours ago, muddy and loud, Maki scarlet, Yuta behind her with a towel. The house was asleep. The fort was asleep. Somewhere above the second bed in the room beside the east end, a dragon was floating at the end of a red ribbon.

Anko walked the line with her hands in her coat pockets. In the left one, her notebook. In the right one, a small flat tin, old and scratched.

At the edge of the low woods, she stopped.

She did not know why, at first. She stood very still in the dark at the place where the fields ran out and the trees began, near the place where, six weeks ago, Mikasa and Eren had killed something small on the road that ran past the low woods. Something child-sized, covered in faces all turned away, muttering what the river towns said about the settlement in the hills. *Go back where you came from.* Two cuts. It had left nothing. It had smelled of wet paper.

Anko breathed in.

Wet paper.

She took out her notebook. She did not turn on her lamp. She crouched at the edge of the woods and looked at the ground in the starlight, the way she had looked at a great many grounds in a great many dark places in another world. And there, in the soft earth under the first trees, there were tracks.

Not child-sized.

She looked at them for a long time.

They went in under the trees. They came out again. They went along the edge of the woods, slowly, keeping just inside the dark, as if whatever had made them had walked the whole length of the line and looked at the settlement and the lit windows of the real house and the blue light of the gate. And then turned back into the woods.

The same walk. She knew it. She had watched the first one walk, on the road, before Mikasa's blades. Shambling and patient and wrong.

The same walk. A bigger body.

Anko stood up.

She thought about the bell, seventeen days ago. About a light that had come up over the south field like a second sun, that people had seen from every road and river town for miles. About the women at the troughs who had stepped back from Mau when he walked by. About the refuge mother drawing her child inside. About the things people said, in the river towns, about the settlement in the hills, and how much louder they would be saying them now. About what a devil was made of.

*Devils aren't normal,* she thought. *Killing one isn't the end.*

She opened her notebook to SIGHTINGS. Past page three, the merchant's bear-that-was-not-a-bear and the lights in the hills and the shadow man at the end of the boy's field. To a clean page. Page four. She wrote, in her small precise hand, by starlight:

*Day 162. Low woods. Tracks. The same walk. Bigger.*

*Smells of wet paper.*

*It came back.*

She closed the notebook.

In her right pocket, her fingers found the scratched tin. She held it for a moment. She did not take it out.

*Not yet.*

She turned round and walked back along the line toward the house, toward the lit window of the kitchen where Diablo would have the tea on, faster than she had walked out.

Behind her, in the low woods, something that smelled of wet paper watched her go.
