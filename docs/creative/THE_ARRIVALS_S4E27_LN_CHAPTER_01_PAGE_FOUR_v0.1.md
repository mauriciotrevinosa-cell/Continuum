*When: Day 163, the morning after Fern wrote "Some things start". The gate party came back from its first run in seventeen days muddy and loud and is still talking about it. Mau may lift one end. Anko has not slept.*

Anko put the notebook down on the long table in front of Fern, open, and stood back.

She did not say anything. She did not need to. It was breakfast, on the hundred-and-sixty-third morning, and the long table was full and noisy, the way it was noisy now that some things had started. Maki was telling the fort, for the third time, about the room in the gate with water in it. Gojo was complaining that he had not been allowed to see it. Momo was ladling porridge. And Anko came in from the yard, from the night, with mud on her boots and her coat still buttoned, and walked the length of the common room without stopping, and put her notebook down in front of Fern, and stood back.

The long table went quiet from the head down, the way it did.

Fern looked at the page.

It was SIGHTINGS. Everyone knew that notebook. Page four. Clean, except for four lines in Anko's small precise hand, written by starlight, a little crooked.

*Day 162. Low woods. Tracks. The same walk. Bigger.*

*Smells of wet paper.*

*It came back.*

Fern read it twice. Then she turned the notebook round, so that it faced down the table, and slid it a little way along the board, so that the people nearest could see. And the people nearest read it, and passed it on, without anyone saying to, until it had gone the whole length of the long table and come back. Every hand that touched it went still for a moment.

Mau read it when it reached him. *Bigger.* He looked at the word for a long time.

Frieren, beside him, did not read it. She was watching his face.

"How much bigger?" said Mikasa.

"The tracks were a man's length apart," said Anko. "Last time they were a child's." She was looking at Mikasa, not at anyone else. "It walked the whole line. Just inside the trees. Looking at us." She paused. "Then it went back in."

Mikasa stood up.

She did it without hurry, the way she did everything that mattered. She stood up from the bench and put her spoon down beside her bowl, and her hand went to the hilts at her hip. The new blades. The gate's blades, grey and strange, that the shop had sold Fern for a sack of crystals in the spring. And under them, still, out of habit, the old ones, with the nick in the left.

Eren stood up a moment after her.

He did not look at her. He looked at Fern. "We killed it," he said. "Two cuts. It didn't leave anything. Not a bone. Nothing." His jaw was set. "We killed it."

"I know," said Fern.

"Then how is it—"

"I don't know," said Fern. She closed the notebook and held it out to Anko. "But I'd like to know before it decides to come out of the trees."

At the far end of the table, someone else stood up.

Himmel. He stood up at the far end, where he always sat, facing the door, and put one hand on his sword. The sword everyone in the house knew was not the hero's sword, and had never been, and had cut down a demon king anyway.

"Things in the woods that wear faces," said Himmel. His voice was very calm. "In my world, they were always worse than they looked." He looked at Mikasa. "I'd like to come."

Mikasa looked at him for a moment. Then she nodded, once.

"Me too," said Gojo, half out of his seat.

"No," said Fern.

"I didn't even say—"

"No," said Fern. "You'd go in front. And you'd show off. And you can't make Red." She did not look at him. "Mikasa. Eren. Himmel. Anko, to show the way. Daylight. Back by dark." She opened the ledger and began to write. "If it's more than you can do with steel, you come back. You don't stay to find out."

Mau had not moved.

He sat at the end of the bench with his hands on the table on either side of his bowl. He felt something lift its head, very slightly, far back behind the new walls. Not the wheel. Something smaller. The part of him that had always stood up first. That went in front. That had stood up in the plaza and the south field and the river and every time something came out of the dark.

It wanted him to stand up.

He looked at Fern.

Fern looked back at him, over the ledger. She did not say anything. She shook her head. Very slightly.

Mau looked at her for a long moment.

Then he nodded, and picked up his spoon, and went on eating his porridge.

Under the table, Frieren's hand found his and closed round it, hard. She did not look at him. She looked straight ahead, at the bowl in front of her, and held his hand under the table so tightly that it hurt.

At the cold-hearth end, Shoko had put down her tea and was watching him with her tired, clinical, completely unsurprised eyes. When he glanced at her, she gave him one small nod. *Good.*

Gojo, beside her, had sat back down. He was looking at Mau too. His blindfolded face did not do anything. But after a moment he said, quietly, to nobody, "Huh," and did not say anything else for the rest of breakfast.



They went in at noon. Daylight, all the way through. Mikasa had insisted.

"Last time we came back through at dusk," she said, at the red stake, checking the edges of both pairs of blades with her thumb. "That was a mistake. It came out of the shadows. We're not giving it shadows."

Eren did not say anything. He buckled his blades on, and watched the south road.

Himmel had come out in his travelling cloak, with his sword at his hip and his hair brushed, because Himmel brushed his hair before every fight, in every world, on principle. Anko walked in front with her notebook. They went down the south road between the fields, past the place where the merchant had first said *monster town*, and down into the river valley, and the low woods rose up ahead of them, bare and grey, with the new green just starting at the tips of the branches.

At the edge of the trees, Anko stopped.

"Here," she said. She crouched. She pointed.

The tracks went into the soft earth under the first trees. A man's length apart. Each one deep, and shapeless, like a foot that had not decided what a foot was. And all round each one, pressed into the mud, very faint, were marks. Small marks. Like the impressions of hands, or mouths, or the edges of very small faces.

Himmel crouched beside her and looked for a long time.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Worse."

They went in.

The woods were quiet. That was the first thing. It was noon, and there was sun coming through the bare branches in long pale bars, and new green, and the smell of wet earth and thaw. There should have been birds. There were no birds. There was no wind. The road went between the trunks, straight, and the four of them walked down it, Mikasa on the left and Eren on the right and Himmel just behind, and Anko a step behind him with her hand inside her coat.

Halfway through, they heard it.

Not faint, this time. Not like water under ice. Louder. A great many voices, from the trees on the left, overlapping, muttering, the way a market sounds from the next street. And Mikasa could make out the words almost at once.

*...monster town...*

*...they don't belong, they never belonged...*

*...did you see it, did you see the light...*

Mikasa's hands were on her hilts.

*...a sun, a second sun, over their field...*

*...the shepherd saw it from the hills, the light came up like morning, at dawn, a second dawn...*

*...the one with the shadow...*

*...the one with the shadow made a SUN...*

*...the grass died where it fell, the ground's still dead, my cousin's boy went to look...*

*...he'll burn the valley...*

*...why do they let him live...*

Eren's face had gone very white and very still.

"It's new," said Mikasa, under her breath. "The words. They're new."

"They're saying it about the field," said Eren. "About the bell. They saw it."

"Everyone saw it," said Anko quietly, behind them. "From every road."

The muttering stopped.

All at once. Every voice. As if a door had shut on the market. The woods were completely silent.

Then the trees on the left moved.

It came out onto the road twenty paces ahead of them, into a bar of noon light, and stood up.

It stood up a long way.



It was taller than Himmel. Taller than any of them. It was the same grey, the grey of old mud, and the same lumpy shape that was almost a shape, with legs that did not bend the way legs bent and arms that were mostly mouths. But it was not child-sized now. It was the size of a man, and then, as it straightened, larger than a man, so that it had to stoop slightly under the branches.

And it was covered in faces.

More of them. Far more. Hundreds. Small faces the size of a hand, overlapping like scales, all over it, front and back and sides. Men and women and old people and children. And most of them, the way they had been last time, were turned away. Looking off to the side. Not meeting anyone's eyes. Muttering.

But not all of them.

Here and there, scattered across its body, some of the faces had turned. A dozen. Twenty. Thirty. They were not looking away any more. They were looking straight at the four people on the road. Wide-eyed. Unblinking. Their mouths were not muttering. They were very still, and very sharp, and they were looking.

"Oh," said Himmel softly, behind Mikasa. "Oh, I see."

The thing looked at them. All of its looking faces at once.

And it said, in every voice it had, the turned ones and the turned-away ones together, quite clearly:

*Go back where you came from.*

And then, in a smaller set of voices, only the ones that were looking, as sharp as a blade:

*Before he burns us.*

It came at them.



It was faster than last time. Much faster. It crossed the twenty paces in the time it took Mikasa to draw, and reached for Eren with an arm that was mostly mouths, and Eren cut.

The steel went in. The way it had last time. Deep, into the thing's shoulder, soft.

And the wound closed.

Not healed. *Closed.* New faces pushed up out of the cut, small and grey and muttering, crowding in from both sides like people closing a gap in a crowd, and the cut was gone, and the arm came on. Eren threw himself backward and it missed his face by a hand's breadth, and the mouths on it snapped at the air where he had been.

Mikasa was already behind it.

She cut twice. Crossing. At the neck. The way she had cut it last time, with the gate's grey blades, which went through the grey stuff like a needle through cloth.

The neck closed too.

Faces. Pushing up. Muttering. Turning away.

"*Mikasa*—"

It turned on her. Its whole body turned, every face on it, and the looking ones fixed on her, all of them, sharp and wide, and it swung at her with both arms. She went under it, the way only she could go under anything, and came up on the far side with both blades out. And it turned again, faster than anything that size had any right to turn.

Himmel stepped in.

He did not shout. He did not pose. He stepped in from the side, into the gap between the thing and Mikasa, with his sword already moving. And he did not cut at its neck, or its shoulder, or its arms.

He cut at a face.

One of the looking ones. On its flank, low, a woman's face, wide-eyed and still. His sword went through it, a short neat stroke, and the face split.

And it did not close.

The thing screamed. All of it. Every voice. Not outrage, this time. Pain. It reeled back, and the turned-away faces round the cut were muttering faster, frightened, and the place where the looking face had been stayed open, a dark wound, steaming.

"The ones that look," said Himmel. He was already moving again. His voice was perfectly calm. "Not the others. The ones that look at you. Cut those."

Mikasa did not ask why. She did not need to. She saw it the moment he said it, the way she saw everything in a fight, all at once.

The turned-away faces were the mutter. The ones that only said what everyone was saying, and looked away from it, and did not quite mean it. They were soft. They closed. They did not matter.

The looking faces were the ones that meant it.

She went for them.



It took a long time.

Afterward, the four of them could not agree how long. Mikasa said ten minutes. Eren said an hour. Anko, who had kept count in her head the way she counted everything, said twenty-three minutes, and wrote it down, and underlined it.

It was not two cuts.

They went round it, the three of them with blades, in the long bars of noon light, in the mud, cutting the looking faces one at a time. A man's face, on its shoulder. A child's, on its back. An old woman's, on the inside of one arm. Every one they cut stayed open, and steamed, and the thing screamed, and got slower. And the turned-away faces round each wound muttered faster and faster, frightened, *don't belong, don't belong, don't belong*, and pressed in, and could not close the gap.

Anko did not fight. She stood at the edge of the road with her hand in her coat and called out the looking faces as they turned, the way a spotter calls out targets. *Left flank, high. Back, two, together. Under the right arm.* Her voice never rose. Twice it swung at her and twice Eren was there before it reached her, and the second time its arm of mouths caught his shoulder and tore his coat and he did not stop.

By the end, there were three looking faces left.

Then two.

Then one. A child's face. In the middle of its chest. Wide-eyed, very still, looking straight at Mikasa.

The thing was down on what passed for its knees in the mud. It was smaller now. It had been getting smaller with every looking face they cut, the way a fire gets smaller when you take away the wood. It was not taller than Himmel any more. It was not taller than Eren. It knelt in the road, steaming from thirty wounds, and its turned-away faces were all still muttering, very fast, very quietly, frightened, *don't belong, don't belong*.

And the child's face in the middle of its chest looked at Mikasa.

*He made a sun,* said the child's face. Very small. Very clear. *Over the field. I saw it. Everyone saw it. He's going to burn everyone.*

Mikasa looked at it.

"No," she said. "He isn't."

She cut.

The face split.

And the thing came apart. All at once, the way it had last time, on the road, into a heap of grey lumpy stuff, steaming in the noon light. All the turned-away faces in it went slack. Their mouths stopped moving, one by one, all over the heap. It began to get smaller. Less. The way a puddle gets less on a warm day.

It smelled of old wet paper.

The four of them stood round it in the road and watched it go. A man's size. A child's. A cat's. A handful. A smudge in the mud. Nothing.

Nothing left. No crystal. No bones. Their blades were clean.

Eren was breathing very hard, with one hand pressed to his torn shoulder. There was blood coming through his fingers, not much, from where the arm of mouths had caught him. He was looking at the empty place on the road with an expression Mikasa had seen on his face before, in another world, at a wall, a long time ago.

"We killed it," he said. "Last time. We *killed* it."

"I know," said Mikasa.

"It came back *bigger*." His voice cracked. "Because they kept saying it. Didn't it. That's what it is. It's what they say. And they saw the light, and they're saying more, and it got bigger." He stared at the mud. "So what happens when they say more?"

Nobody answered.

Himmel had sheathed his sword. He stood at the side of the road with his hands at his sides and looked at the place where it had been for a long time. His face was very grave. Not the hero's face. The face underneath it.

"In my world," he said at last, quietly, "the things in the woods that wore faces were always worse than they looked." He paused. "This one was exactly as bad as it looked." He turned and looked back up the road, north, toward the settlement they could not see. "That's what frightens me."



They told the long table that night. All of it.

The words. *The one with the shadow made a sun.* The looking faces and the turned-away faces. Himmel's stroke. Twenty-three minutes. *He's going to burn everyone.* And how it had got smaller with every looking face, and left nothing, and smelled of wet paper.

Maomao cleaned Eren's shoulder and stitched it and said it was nothing, and then looked at the wound for a long time and said it was a very strange nothing, because the edges looked like teeth. Shoko looked at it too and agreed, and the two of them stood over Eren's shoulder murmuring to each other in a way that made him go pale.

Rimuru asked Ciel. And this time Ciel had something to say, because Anko had brought back a scrap of torn coat from Eren's shoulder with a grey smear on it that had not yet faded, and Rimuru had held it under the relay.

"*It is not the same body,*" said Rimuru, for her, in her flat clear voice. "*The first one is gone. Completely. There is nothing of it left.*" A pause. "*But this one is the same shape. The same pattern. The same...*" Another pause, longer. "*The same fear. Grown.*"

"Fear," said Fern.

"*It is made of something like what the cursed spirits in Yuta's world are made of,*" said Rimuru. "*But not the same. It is made of what people are afraid of. When they are afraid of the same thing, together, for long enough, it takes a shape.*" He paused. "*It came back because they kept being afraid. It came back bigger because they are more afraid.*" He paused again, and this time it was Rimuru, not Ciel, who added, very quietly: "Since the bell."

The long table was very quiet.

"So killing it doesn't work," said Eren.

"*Killing it works,*" said Rimuru, for Ciel. "*Today. It stopped it. Today.*" A pause. "*It is not the end.*"

Mau was at the end of the bench.

He had listened to all of it. He had sat very still through all of it, the way he sat still when something behind his breastbone lifted its head. And when Mikasa said *the one with the shadow made a sun*, he had felt every face at the long table not look at him.

"It's about me," he said.

The common room went quiet again.

"It's what they say about the light," said Mau. His voice was level. "About the field. About me. It said *the one with the shadow* last time, and now it says *he made a sun*." He looked at his hands on the table. "It's afraid of me. They're afraid of me. And it's getting bigger because they're—"

"No," said Mikasa.

He looked up.

She was sitting across the table from him, very straight, with her new blades on the bench beside her. Her face was perfectly calm. Her eyes were not.

"It said *the one with the shadow* last time too," said Mikasa. "Before the bell. Before the field. Before any of it. In the spring. When you hadn't done anything at all except carry water and fix the roof and sit with Frieren by the river." She did not look away from him. "It's never been you. It's them. It's what frightened people say about someone they've never met. It would say it about anyone." She paused. "It's not afraid of you. It doesn't know you. It's afraid of a story."

Mau did not say anything.

"I cut its face today," said Mikasa. "The last one. A child's. It looked at me and said *he's going to burn everyone*." Her voice did not change. "And I said *no, he isn't*. And then I cut it." She picked up her spoon. "That's all I'm going to say about it."

She went back to her soup.

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

Then Himmel, at the far end, raised his cup very slightly toward Mikasa. Just slightly. And Eren, beside her, with his stitched shoulder, looked at the side of her face for a long time, as if he had never quite seen it before.

Under the table, Frieren's hand was in Mau's again. She did not hold it hard this time. She just held it.



Anko did not sleep that night either.

She went up to the roof, after the rounds, the way she sometimes did, and sat on the ridge above the north wing with her knees drawn up and her coat round her, looking south. Toward the river valley and the low woods, where there was nothing to see. She had her notebook open on her knee. Page four, with a new line under the old ones.

*Day 163. Killed again. Twenty-three minutes. Cut the faces that look.*

And under that, after a long time, smaller:

*It'll come back.*

After a while, something landed on the ridge beside her. Lightly, the way a cat lands. Nazuna, awake, as she always was at this hour, with her coat open and her hair everywhere and her pale cheerful face, eating an apple she had very clearly taken from somebody.

She sat down beside Anko without asking, the way she had sat down beside her on a hundred rooftops in another city, a long time ago, before everything.

"You look terrible," said Nazuna.

"Thank you."

"You look like you did the year you didn't sleep." Nazuna bit the apple. "The year you were looking for me."

Anko did not answer that.

They sat on the ridge together for a while, looking south. Two women who had spent their lives awake at night, in a city full of things that came out in the dark. One of them had hunted those things. The other one was one.

"It's going to come back," said Anko at last, quietly.

"Probably," said Nazuna. She finished the apple and threw the core off the roof, a long way, into the dark. "Things do." She lay back on the shingles with her hands behind her head and looked up at the stars. "I'll take the north field tomorrow night. You take the south. We'll swap at three."

Anko looked down at her.

"You don't do night shifts," she said. "You do *nights*. It's different."

"I'm doing a night shift," said Nazuna. "Tomorrow. And the night after." She closed her eyes. "Somebody has to watch the hills while you watch the woods. And I can see in the dark better than you." She smiled, with her eyes shut. "I always could."

Anko did not say anything.

But she closed the notebook, after a while, and lay back on the ridge too, beside her, with her hands in her coat pockets. In the right one, the scratched tin. In the left one, nothing.

They lay there, the hunter and the vampire, on the roof of a house in another world, and watched the stars over the black hills, until the reset hour came and went.
