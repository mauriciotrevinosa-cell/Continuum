*When: Day 169, a week after the gate reopened, six days after the low woods. Eren's shoulder is stitched and he is pretending it isn't. Nazuna has kept the north field every night since, and Anko the south. The gate changed at dawn yesterday, and Gojo's "next week" has arrived.*

Gojo came out to the red stake dressed as if he were going to a festival.

Nobody knew where he had got the clothes. A long dark coat with a high collar, which was his, from his own world, and under it something Marin had very obviously made for him in the night, out of the leftover blue cloth from the dating days, with too many pockets. His blindfold was on. His hair was brushed straight up. He had a bag over one shoulder that turned out, when Fern made him open it, to contain six of Momo's honey cakes and nothing else.

"Provisions," said Gojo.

"You'll be inside an hour," said Fern.

"Six cakes is an hour of provisions."

Fern looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the list in her hand. GATE PARTY, DAY 169. *Yuta. Maki. Stark. Frieren. Gojo. Milim (Guardian, at the back).* In Fern's small upright hand, under Gojo's name, there was a second line, newer, added that morning: *(Does not go in front.)*

"Rules," said Fern.

"I know the rules."

"Rules," said Fern again. And the whole party, standing round the red stake in the cold bright morning with the blue gate humming forty paces beyond it, said them back to her, in the ragged chorus of people who had said them every week for months.

"One. Nobody goes in alone."

"Two. If anyone says out, everyone comes out. At once. No arguing."

"And the last one. To leave no one inside."

That one was Mau's. It was the last line of the contract he had written before the first run, in the spring, the day he chose to wait outside: five rules on the back of Fern's list, signed by every one of them but him. The last one was the only one anyone ever said aloud. *To leave no one inside.* He was not allowed in today; not since the field. He was standing at the red stake with his hands in his pockets and Diablo a pace behind him, pleasantly, on the rota, watching them go.

"And six," said Umaru, who had come out to the stake in her hood with the Ministry slate, as she did every week, to read out her guide.

*RULE SIX: MIMICS,* said the slate. *THIS MEANS YOU.* And under it, in fresh chalk, in a different colour:

*(THIS ALSO MEANS GOJO.)*

Gojo looked at the slate. He put his hand on his chest.

"I'm hurt," he said.

"You will be," said Umaru, "if you open one."

Shoko was at the stake too. She had come out with her tea, and her white coat, and her one unlit cigarette in the breast pocket where it always was now. She did not say anything to Gojo while the rules were read. She waited until the party was turning toward the gate. Then she said, without raising her voice:

"Satoru."

He turned round.

"Don't show off," said Shoko.

Gojo put his hand on his chest again. "I would *never*."

Nobody said anything. The whole party, and Fern, and Umaru, and Mau, and Diablo, looked at him in complete silence.

"I would *sometimes*," Gojo amended, and went through the gate.



The gate had changed at dawn the day before. It always did, every seventh dawn. This time it opened onto a forest.

Not a real forest. A forest made of stone. Great grey trunks of rock rose up out of a floor of moss and broken flagstones, so tall that their tops were lost in a ceiling of mist, and between them, growing out of the stone the way mushrooms grow out of wood, were crystals. Blue and pale and faintly humming, the size of a hand, the size of a man, clustered in the roots of the stone trees, lighting the whole place a dim underwater blue.

Gojo stopped dead just inside the gate.

"Oh," he said.

He took the blindfold off.

He stood there in the blue light with his bare blue eyes, turning slowly round, looking at all of it. At the stone trees. At the crystals. At the mist. And floating in the air in front of him, about twenty paces off, where everybody saw it in their own language, a window. A pale square of light, hanging in the air, with writing on it.

*GATE: THE STONE WOOD. NORMAL. CHANGES IN 6 DAYS.*

*PARTY: 6. SETTLEMENT BALANCE: 412.*

"It's a *game*," said Gojo, in a voice of pure joy. "It's an actual *game*. There's a *menu*. Yuta. Yuta, there's a menu."

"I know," said Yuta. He was smiling. He had been waiting all week to watch his teacher see this. "There's a shop, too."

"There's a *shop*?"

"At the gate," said Maki, walking past him with her spear on her shoulder. "Outside. You can't buy anything. You haven't got any crystals." She did not look back. "Put the blindfold back on, you'll tire your eyes. And keep up."

Gojo put the blindfold back on. Then he took it off again to look at the window one more time. Then he put it back on, and kept up.



The first things they met were stone hounds.

They came out of the roots of the trees in a pack, low and fast, made of the same grey rock as the trunks, with crystal teeth and eyes that glowed the same pale blue as everything else. Five of them. They came across the moss without a sound.

Stark stepped in front of Frieren with his axe, the way he always did. Maki dropped her spear into her hands. Yuta's cursed energy came up round him like a dark film. Milim, at the back, said "*Ooh*," and did not move, because Milim at the back was the rule, and Milim kept the rules about Guardians even when she did not keep any others.

And Gojo stepped forward.

"Gojo," said Yuta sharply. "*Not in front.*"

"I'm not going in front," said Gojo. "I'm going *beside*." And he did. He stepped up beside Stark, a pace to the left, not ahead, with his hands in his coat pockets. The first hound reached them and leapt at his throat, and stopped. In the air. A hand's breadth from his collar. Hanging there, slower and slower, its crystal teeth closing on nothing, forever.

"Infinity," said Gojo, pleased. "Still works in here."

Stark's axe took the hound out of the air in one stroke. It burst into blue sparks and a single small crystal that dropped onto the moss.

"Thanks," said Stark.

"Anytime," said Gojo. And then, as the second hound went for Maki, he took one hand out of his pocket and held it up, and a small marble of blue appeared over his palm. The size of a cherry. No bigger. It pulled. Gently. And the hound, mid-leap, swerved a hand's breadth to the left in the air, toward it. Straight onto the point of Maki's spear.

Sparks. A crystal.

Maki looked at him over her shoulder.

"That," she said, "was useful."

"I'm very useful," said Gojo.

"You're not usually," said Maki. "Usually you just do everything yourself and everyone stands around." She pulled her spear back. "That was better."

Gojo did not say anything to that for a moment. Then he let the marble of Blue go out, and put his hand back in his pocket, and stood beside Stark, not in front, while Yuta and Maki and Stark finished the other three. He pulled one more hound sideways, into Yuta's blade. He stopped one more in the air, for Stark. He did not kill a single one himself.

When it was over, he stood in the blue light among the scattered crystals, with his hands in his pockets, looking at the place where the last hound had been.

"Huh," he said quietly.

"What?" said Yuta.

"Nothing," said Gojo. He bent down and picked up one of the small crystals and turned it over in his fingers. "I've never done that before. Helped." He put the crystal in Fern's bag, which Yuta was carrying. "It's nice."

Yuta looked at his teacher for a long moment.

He did not say anything. But he was smiling again, all the way to the next room.



The chest was in the fourth room.

It was the kind of room Frieren liked. A clearing in the stone wood, round, with the stone trees standing in a ring and the crystals growing thick in their roots, and in the middle, on a low flat rock, a chest. Old wood, black with age. Iron bands. A heavy lock shaped like a closed eye.

Frieren stopped at the edge of the clearing.

Everyone else stopped too, because everyone else knew.

She held up one finger. Her lips moved. A small pale light went out from her fingertip across the clearing and touched the chest, and hung there for a moment, and went out.

"Mimic," said Frieren. "Ninety-nine percent."

Gojo had pushed his blindfold up onto his forehead. He was looking at the chest with his bare eyes.

"A hundred," he said.

Frieren looked at him.

"It's a mimic," said Gojo. "A hundred percent. I can see it. There's a mouth where the lid meets the box. The whole thing's breathing. Very slowly. There's—" he tilted his head "—something in its stomach. Metal. Small. It's alive and it's waiting and it's very, very hungry." He grinned. "One hundred percent."

"The spell says ninety-nine," said Frieren.

"My eyes say a hundred."

Frieren considered this.

"Then there's no point opening it," she said.

"Exactly," said Gojo. He paused. Something in his face changed. "So why do *you* always open them?"

The clearing was quiet.

Stark looked at the ceiling. Maki leaned on her spear. Milim, at the back, put both hands over her mouth, because she knew this story and loved it. Yuta did not know this story, and looked from his teacher to the elf.

Frieren looked at the chest.

"Because of the one in a hundred," she said.

Gojo waited.

"The spell is ninety-nine percent," said Frieren. "Not a hundred. So there is always one chance in a hundred that it's wrong. That it's a real chest. With something real in it. Something I didn't expect." She folded her hands inside the sleeves of the orange hoodie. "If I only opened the ones I was sure about, I'd never open anything that surprised me." She paused. "I've been alive a very long time. There aren't many things left that surprise me." She looked at the chest. "So I open them. All of them. For the one in a hundred."

Gojo did not say anything for a long moment.

He stood at the edge of the clearing with his blindfold pushed up and his bare sky-coloured eyes, looking at the chest. The chest that he could see straight through. The mouth. The breathing. The metal in its stomach. All of it, all at once, the way he had seen everything, all at once, every day of his life, since he was a baby.

"I've never been surprised by a chest," he said slowly.

Frieren looked at him.

"I've never been surprised by *anything*," said Gojo. He laughed, very quietly. It was not quite a laugh. "Not since I was born. I see it all coming. Every attack. Every trick. Every box. I always know what's inside." He looked at his own hand. "I didn't know that was a thing you could miss."

Nobody said anything.

Then Gojo walked across the clearing to the chest, and crouched in front of it, and looked at it with his bare eyes for a long moment, a hundred percent. And opened it anyway.

The mimic lunged.

It came up off the rock all at once, the lid flying back, the whole box unfolding into a great black mouth lined with iron teeth, and it closed on Gojo's head.

Or tried to.

It stopped. A hand's breadth from his face. Its jaws hung there, open, all the way round his head, trembling, closing slower and slower and slower, forever, and never arriving. Gojo crouched inside a mimic's mouth with his chin in his hand, perfectly comfortable, looking up at the iron teeth an inch from his eyes.

"Huh!" said Gojo, delighted. "So *this* is what it's like!"

Frieren had gone completely still at the edge of the clearing.

"*That,*" she said, "is cheating."

"Is it?"

"Mimics *bite* you," said Frieren. Her voice had risen, very slightly, which almost never happened. "That is the *rule*. You open the mimic and it *bites* you and someone pulls you out by the legs. That is the *agreement*."

"I didn't sign any agreement."

"*Everyone* signs it! By opening it!"

Maki had sat down on the moss. She was laughing so hard she could not hold her spear. Stark had his face in his hands. Milim was rolling on the floor at the back of the clearing, entirely failing at being a Guardian. Yuta was looking at his teacher, crouched comfortably inside the jaws of a mimic, with an expression of deep and helpless love.

Gojo reached up, inside the mimic's mouth, and very politely took the small metal thing out of its stomach. Then he stood up, and stepped out of its jaws, which went on trying to close on the empty air where his head had been. Stark took it apart with the axe.

Sparks. A crystal. And in Gojo's hand, a small round thing of tarnished silver. A compass, very old, with a cracked face, whose needle did not point north. It pointed at Frieren.

Gojo looked at it. Then he held it out to her.

"For you," he said. "Since I cheated."

Frieren looked at the compass. Then at him.

She took it.

"It's still cheating," she said.

"I know."

She turned the compass over in her fingers. The needle swung, and settled, and pointed at her again, wherever she held it. She put it in the pocket of the orange hoodie, carefully, beside the mimic ledger.

"Thank you," said Frieren, very quietly. And then, much less quietly: "*Cheating.*"



There was another chest in the sixth room.

Smaller. Plainer. In an alcove between two stone trunks, half buried in crystals, with no lock at all.

Frieren cast her spell on it. The pale light went out and touched it and hung there.

"Mimic," said Frieren. "Ninety-nine percent." She looked at Gojo. "Well?"

Gojo looked at the chest for a long moment.

Then he took his blindfold down from his forehead and pulled it over his eyes. Snug. Knotted once at the back of his head. And then, while everyone in the party watched, he did the thing that he had spent nearly a month teaching Mau to do in the yard. He did not look.

His eyes were behind the cloth, and they were awake, the way they were always awake. They could have seen through it. They always could. He did not ask them to.

He walked to the alcove blind, and crouched in front of the chest, and put his hands on the lid.

"I don't know what's in it," said Gojo. He sounded astonished. "I genuinely don't know."

"The spell says mimic," said Frieren, from the edge of the room. Her voice had something in it that was not quite a warning. "Ninety-nine percent. It's going to bite you."

"Maybe," said Gojo.

He dropped the Infinity.

Everyone in the party felt it go. It was like a sound they had stopped hearing, stopping. Maki stood up very fast. Yuta's cursed energy flared. Milim was on her feet at the back.

"*Satoru*—" said Yuta.

Gojo opened the chest.

It was not a mimic.

It was a chest. An ordinary wooden chest, in an alcove, in a stone wood, in a dungeon in another world. And inside it, on a bed of old dry moss, were three potions in small corked bottles, the deep red ones that Fern rationed like gold. And a little cloth bag, tied with string, which, when Gojo untied it with his blindfold still on, turned out to be full of something small and round and hard.

He put one in his mouth.

It was a sweet. A boiled sweet. Plain sugar, slightly cloudy, very old, tasting faintly of honey.

Gojo knelt in front of the chest with a sweet in his mouth and his blindfold on and three red potions in his lap, and did not say anything for a long time.

Then he started to laugh.

It was not his usual laugh. It was higher. Younger. It came up out of him all at once, helpless, like a child's, and it went on and on, and he did not seem able to stop it. He knelt in the alcove and laughed with his blindfold on until his shoulders shook, and nobody in the party had ever heard Satoru Gojo make that sound, not even Yuta, not even in another life.

"*I didn't know,*" he said, through it. "*Frieren. I didn't know. It was the one in a hundred. And I didn't know.*"

Frieren stood at the edge of the room.

She did not say anything for a moment. Then, slowly, she walked across to the alcove and crouched beside him, and looked into the chest. At the three red potions. At the bag of very old sweets.

"Ninety-nine percent," she said. "And it was the one."

"It was the *one*."

Frieren was quiet. Then she held out her hand, palm up.

Gojo put a sweet in it, still laughing.

She ate it. It was very old, and slightly stale, and tasted faintly of honey, and it was, she would say later, in the ledger, the best thing that had come out of a chest in the whole of the spring.



They came out an hour and twenty minutes after they went in, which made Fern purse her lips, and with forty-one crystals and three red potions, which made Fern's pen stop moving entirely.

Mau was at the red stake. He had been there the whole time, with Diablo behind him and Shoko beside him with her tea gone cold. He watched them come out of the blue gate one by one. Maki first. Stark. Frieren, with something in her hoodie pocket she kept touching. Milim, at the back, still giggling. Yuta. And Gojo, last, with his blindfold on and a cloth bag of sweets in one hand, walking straight across the forty paces of grass to the stake as if he could see every blade of it.

He stopped in front of Mau.

"I didn't look," said Gojo.

Mau looked at him.

"In there," said Gojo. "There was a chest. And I could have seen what was in it. I always can. I didn't look." He held up the bag of sweets. "On purpose. Like you do. Like I taught you." His voice was doing something strange. "And I didn't know what was in it, Mau. For the first time in my *life*."

Mau did not say anything. But he smiled. Slowly. All the way.

"Was it good?" he said.

"It was *sweets*," said Gojo. "It was sweets and *potions* and I didn't *know*." He held out the bag. "Have one."

Mau took one.

Beside him, Shoko was looking at Gojo over her cold tea.

"You're glowing again," she said.

"I was surprised," said Gojo. "By a box."

Shoko looked at him for a long moment. At the blindfold. At the bag of sweets. At whatever was in his face that she had not seen in all the years she had known him.

"Good," she said quietly. She held out her hand.

He put a sweet in it.



That night, in the east room, Frieren sat in the reading chair with the mimic ledger open on her knee and the old silver compass on the arm of the chair beside her, its needle pointing at her, steadily, whichever way she turned it.

She wrote, very carefully, under the old lines:

*Mimics found: 5. (One by Gojo.)*

*Mimics opened: 5.*

*Bitten: 4 (me). 0 (Gojo). CHEATING.*

And under that, after a long time:

*Real chests opened blind: 1 (Gojo). The one in a hundred.*

*He laughed like Himmel.*

She looked at the last line for a long time. Then she did not cross it out.
