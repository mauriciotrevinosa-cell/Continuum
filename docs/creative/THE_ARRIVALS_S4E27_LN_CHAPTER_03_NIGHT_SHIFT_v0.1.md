*When: The night of Day 171, into the hour before dawn on Day 172. Nazuna has kept the north field for eight nights; Anko the south. Frieren has a compass that points at her and will not say where she got it. The refuge families are down to three, with four children, in the cottages along the north road.*

Nazuna saw it at a quarter past two.

She was sitting on top of Anko's red stake in the north field, cross-legged, which should not have been possible on a stake the width of a man's wrist, and was possible for her. The gate hummed blue behind her, forty paces off. In front of her, the north field ran away in the moonlight to the line of the black hills, and the hills ran along the sky, the way they always did, low and dark, with the stars thick above them.

She had been watching the hills for eight nights.

She was good at it. Better than she had thought she would be. She had spent her whole life awake at night, but she had spent it in a city, in bars and on rooftops and in the bright noisy dark of streets that never slept, and she had never once in her life sat still and watched one thing for hours. She had thought it would be boring. It was not. It was the opposite of boring. The hills at night were full of small things. A fox. An owl. The wind moving in the long grass. Once, on the fourth night, a pale shape on the skyline that turned out to be a deer, standing very still, and she had nearly flown out to see if it was *the* deer, and then remembered that the deer had gone home months ago, with Nokotan, through a hole shaped like itself.

At a quarter past two on the ninth night, there was a new shape on the hills.

It was on the skyline. Due north. On the crest of the nearest ridge, where the old shepherd's track went over. A shape against the stars.

Tall. Upright. Still.

Nazuna looked at it.

She had very good eyes. She always had. She could see a moth on a wall across a street at midnight. She looked at the shape on the ridge for a long time, and it did not move, and she could not, for a while, make it into anything. It was too big to be a man. It was the shape of a man. It was standing on the ridge, perfectly still, the way a statue stands, with its arms hanging at its sides, facing south.

Facing the settlement.

Nazuna stood up on the stake.

Then she went up.



She did not go close. She was not stupid. She went up into the dark over the north field, high, higher than the gate, and drifted north on the cold air, slowly, the way she drifted over the city at home when she was looking for something, until she was perhaps a quarter of a mile from the ridge, and high above it, and could see.

It was a person.

That was her first thought, and she did not like it. It was the shape of a person, exactly. A man, roughly. Arms and legs and a head, a body. Naked, and sexless, like a doll. Pale, in the moonlight, the pale of something that had never been in the sun. It stood on the crest of the ridge with its feet apart and its arms hanging and its head very slightly tilted, and it was the height of a three-storey house.

And it was smiling.

Its face was a person's face, too big, wrong in a way she could not name. The proportions were off. The eyes were too far apart, and wide open, and did not blink. And its mouth was stretched in a smile, a wide flat contented smile, from one side of its face almost to the other, showing teeth. It stood on the ridge in the moonlight, perfectly still, eyes open, smiling at the settlement below it, the way a child smiles at a cake through a shop window.

It did not move. It did not breathe, as far as she could see. Its eyes did not follow her when she drifted closer. It just stood there, smiling, facing south, with the stars behind it.

Nazuna hung in the air above the ridge for a long moment.

She had seen a great many monsters in her life. She was one. She had seen vampires with teeth like knives and things in the city that wore people's faces and hunters with blades who wanted her dead. None of them had ever frightened her very much.

This frightened her.

"That's not a monster," she said aloud, very quietly, to nobody, to the cold air. "That's a person who forgot how."

Then she turned round and flew south faster than she had ever flown in her life.



Anko was at the south stake by the river path, on her rounds, with her notebook. She heard Nazuna coming before she saw her. The rush of air. The thud of boots landing too hard on the frozen mud. She turned round with her hand already inside her coat.

Nazuna's face was white. Whiter than usual. She did not look cheerful.

"North ridge," said Nazuna. "The shepherd's track. Come and look."

Anko looked at her for one second.

Then she took her hand out of her coat and closed the notebook and said, "Show me."

They did not go up the ridge. Nazuna would not let her. They went to the north end of the settlement, past the cottages on the north road where the refuge families slept, to the place where the street ran out into the fields and the road began. The place where Mikasa stood for an hour every afternoon, looking at the hills. And from there, in the moonlight, Anko could see it. Just. A shape on the far skyline. Too tall. Too still. Upright.

She looked at it for a long time with her tired eyes.

"It hasn't moved?" she said.

"Not once. Not in an hour." Nazuna was hugging herself, which Anko had not seen her do in ten years. "It's just standing there. Smiling. Its eyes are open."

"Smiling."

"Its whole face," said Nazuna. "Like it's happy to see us."

Anko took out her notebook. She did not write anything in it. She looked at the clean page for a moment, and then closed it again, and put it away.

"Fern," she said. "Now."



Fern did not ask any questions. She opened her door at a tap from Anko's knuckle, in her nightdress, with her hair down and her eyes already awake, and listened to six sentences, and went back into her room and came out again thirty seconds later dressed, with her hair tied back and the ledger under her arm.

"Who else," said Fern.

"Eren," said Anko.

Fern looked at her.

"It's the shape of a man," said Anko quietly. "Three storeys high. Naked. Smiling. Standing still in the dark." She held Fern's eyes. "Eren told us once. At the long table. In the first winter. What came over the walls in his world."

Fern did not say anything. She turned and went down the family corridor to the room with the two exits and the bunk beds, and knocked once, softly.

The door opened at once. Mikasa. Awake, the way she was always awake when anyone knocked, with her blades already in her hand. She looked at Fern, and at Anko behind her, and at Nazuna behind Anko with her white face, and something in her own face closed like a door.

She did not ask either. She turned round.

"Eren," she said.

He was on the top bunk. He sat up.



He stood at the north end of the settlement for a long time without saying anything.

The others stood a little way behind him. Mikasa, closest. Fern with the ledger. Anko. Nazuna. And Rimuru, who had come down the stair from his office in human shape with his coat over his nightclothes when Fern tapped on that door too. They stood in the cold at the place where the street ran out, and Eren stood in front of them, at the very edge, where the road began, with his arms at his sides, looking north.

The shape stood on the ridge in the moonlight. Too tall. Too still. Smiling, though none of them could see that from here, except Nazuna.

Eren's hands were shaking.

He did not seem to know it. He stood there very straight, with his jaw set, and his hands shook at his sides like leaves. Mikasa saw it. She did not say anything. She stepped up beside him, close, not touching, the way she stood beside him at the red stake. Half a step. Always half a step.

"Titan," said Eren.

His voice was perfectly flat.

Nobody said anything.

"It's a titan," said Eren. "A pure one. Seven metres. Maybe eight." He did not look away from the ridge. "They eat people. That's all they do. They don't need to. They don't digest it. They just eat people, and then they throw them up when they're full, and then they eat more." His voice did not change at all. "They're always smiling. Always. That's the worst part. They smile while they do it."

Fern had opened the ledger. She did not write anything.

"Why isn't it moving?" said Rimuru quietly.

"It's night," said Eren. "They need the sun. They slow down after dark. Most of them stop completely. They just stand there, wherever they were when the light went, until it comes back." He looked at the eastern sky, which was still black, with the stars thick on it. "When the sun comes up, it'll start walking. Toward people. They always know where people are. They can smell us. Feel us. Something." He paused. "It's facing us. It knows we're here."

The cottages on the north road were behind them. Three of them, with lamps out, with the refuge families asleep inside. Four children. The nearest cottage was perhaps forty paces from where Eren stood. The ridge was perhaps a mile.

"How long?" said Fern.

Eren looked at the sky again.

"Three hours," he said. "Maybe less."

Fern nodded once.

She turned to Rimuru. "The kitchen," she said. "Wake Diablo. Tea. The people we need." She looked at Anko. "Not the whole house. Not yet. Not the children." She looked back at Eren's back, at his shaking hands. "And Aira."

"Aira?" said Anko.

"The cottages," said Fern. "If it comes, it comes past the cottages first." She closed the ledger. "Aira keeps people behind her. It's what she does."



The kitchen was full by three.

Diablo had the tea on before anyone asked. Fern at the head of the table with the ledger. Rimuru. Eren and Mikasa, side by side. Anko. Nazuna on the counter, with her knees drawn up, not eating anything for once. Himmel, who had been woken by nobody and had come down anyway, dressed, with his sword, and his hair brushed, because Himmel always seemed to know. Stark, with his axe, because Fern had tapped on his door and he had come straight out without his boots and gone back for them. Aira, in a big coat over her nightclothes, with her arms folded and her face set, already looking at the north window.

And Milim, who had not been woken by anyone either, and had floated in through the door in her sleep at about ten past three, still tethered by the ankle to a length of red ribbon that trailed back down the corridor toward Ori's room. She bumped gently against the ceiling. Then she woke up, and looked round at all of them, and said:

"Is something happening? Can I come?"

Nobody laughed. That was how everyone knew how bad it was.

"Tell them," said Fern, to Eren. "Everything. How you kill one."

Eren told them.

He did it the way he had told them about the walls, once, in the first winter, at the long table, when nobody had asked. Flatly. In order. Not looking at anyone. How big they got. How they moved. How they healed, from almost anything, in seconds, steaming. How you could cut off an arm, a leg, a head, and it would grow back. How the only place that mattered was the back of the neck. The nape. A strip the width of a hand and the length of a forearm, just below the base of the skull. Cut that, deep, and it fell, and did not get up, and steamed away to nothing.

"In my world we had gear," said Eren. "Wires. Gas. You flew. You got up behind it and you cut." He looked at his hands on the table. They had stopped shaking. They were very still now, flat on the board, as if he were holding them there. "We don't have gear here."

"I can fly," said Milim.

Everyone looked at her.

"I can fly," said Milim again. Her face was very serious. "And Frieren can fly. And Nazuna can fly. And I can carry somebody. I carried Mau once, when he was asleep, and he didn't even wake up." She looked at Mikasa. "I could carry you. Up behind it. To the neck."

Mikasa looked at her for a long moment.

"Fast?" she said.

"So fast," said Milim. "So, so fast. Gently, though." A pause. "Mostly gently."

"Bring it down first," said Himmel quietly.

They looked at him.

"In my world, when something was too tall to reach," said Himmel, "we brought it down to where we could." He put one hand on his sword. "The legs. Behind the knee. The heel. If it can't stand, it falls. If it falls, its neck is on the ground." He looked at Stark. "We take the legs. Stark and I. While it's still slow, at first light. And Milim carries Mikasa, in case it doesn't fall far enough."

Stark looked at his axe. He swallowed. Then he nodded.

"It'll heal," said Eren. "The legs. In seconds. You'll get one chance before it stands up again."

"Then we take the one chance," said Himmel.

Eren looked at him across the table. At the hero with his brushed hair and his sword that was not the hero's sword, in a kitchen in another world, at three in the morning, offering to walk up to the ankles of a thing three storeys high that ate people.

"You don't know what they're like," said Eren quietly.

"No," said Himmel. "You do. That's why you're going to tell us exactly where to cut."

Rimuru had been quiet. He spoke now.

"I want to see it," he said. "Ciel wants to see it. Before it's gone. If it steams away when it dies, I'll have nothing to read." He looked at Eren. "I need a thread in it while it's alive. In its shadow. At dawn the shadows are long." He paused. "I'll stay back. I won't go near it. I just need its shadow to touch mine."

Fern wrote that down.

Then she looked at the last person in the kitchen who had not spoken. Eren.

"You're going," said Fern. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"You don't transform."

Eren went very still.

"We don't know what it does here," said Fern. "Your titan. We don't know if it's the same. We don't know what it costs." She did not look away from him. "You're going because you know them better than anyone in this world. You'll stand back with Anko and tell them where to cut. You don't transform. You don't go in." She paused. "Promise me."

Eren looked at her for a long time.

Then he looked at Mikasa. Who was looking at him. Half a step away, on the bench, close, not touching.

"I promise," said Eren.

Fern wrote it in the ledger. *E. does not transform. Promised.* Then she looked round the kitchen at all of them, at three in the morning, with the tea going cold and the window black.

"Mau stays in," said Fern.

Nobody argued.

"He's asleep," said Rimuru quietly. "He doesn't know yet."

"Good," said Fern. "Let him sleep. He'll know when it's over." She stood up. "First light. Positions in two hours."



Aira went out to the cottages alone, at four, with a lantern.

She did not wake the families. She sat down on the step of the nearest cottage, the one with the bean-girl in it, the Fastest Child, the one who had swept the step of Arrival House all winter. She put the lantern down beside her and turned it low. She folded her arms in her big coat and sat on the step, facing north, toward the road, toward the ridge she could not see in the dark.

She sat there for a long time.

At half past four, someone sat down on the step beside her.

Mikasa. With her blades. She did not say anything. She sat down on the cold step beside Aira, close, the way she sat beside people she trusted, and looked north too.

They had not talked much, the two of them. In all the time they had been in the house. They were not alike. Aira was loud and fierce and talked about aliens and had opinions about everything, and Mikasa did not talk at all if she could help it. But they had stood at the back of the same crowds. They had walked the same edges, on the same nights, with their eyes on the dark. They had both, at one time or another, in the plaza and on the road and at the bell, stood in front of a door with someone behind it.

"If it gets past us," said Mikasa quietly, "it comes here first."

"I know," said Aira.

"You'll be alone. With four children. And their parents."

"I know."

Mikasa looked at her.

Aira was looking at the road. Her face was set. Her jaw was set. She was afraid. Mikasa could see that. She was very afraid, and she was sitting on the step anyway, with her arms folded, facing north.

"Nobody gets past me," said Aira. "Not to these doors." She did not look round. "I don't care how tall it is."

Mikasa did not say anything for a long moment.

Then she reached into her coat and took out the old blades. Not the grey ones from the gate. The old ones. Her own. From her own world, with the nick in the left, that she had carried every day since she came, even after the new ones, even though steel was no good inside the gate.

She held them out to Aira. Hilts first.

"Steel works out here," said Mikasa. "On things from the woods." She paused. "I don't know if it works on them. But it's better than nothing."

Aira looked at the blades. Then at Mikasa.

She took them.

"I'll give them back," said Aira.

"I know," said Mikasa.

She stood up. She looked down at Aira on the step, with her lantern turned low and the two old blades across her knees, facing north, in front of the door of a cottage with four children asleep inside it.

"Behind you," said Mikasa. "Everyone. That's your job."

"Everyone," said Aira.

Mikasa went back up the road toward the north field, into the dark, to where the others were.



At ten to six, the sky in the east began to go grey.

They were in position. Rimuru at the north end of the field, back, with his shadow stretched out long and thin across the frost toward the ridge, waiting for the light. Anko beside him with her notebook and Eren beside her, very still, with his hands at his sides and his eyes on the shape on the ridge. Nazuna high above them, a dark speck against the paling sky. Himmel and Stark in the long grass at the foot of the ridge, crouched, two hundred paces from it, with the axe and the sword. Milim beside Mikasa, behind them, crackling faintly at the edges with something pink.

Frieren had come out too. Nobody had asked her. She stood at the red stake with her staff, beside the gate, with the silver compass in her hoodie pocket. She did not say anything. She was looking up the ridge, the way she looked at things she was preparing to kill.

The grey in the east went pink. Then gold, at the very edge.

The first light came over the hills.

It touched the crest of the ridge. It touched the top of the shape standing on it. Its head. Its wide flat face. Its open eyes.

The eyes moved.

Eren's breath went out of him all at once.

On the ridge, very slowly, in the first gold light of the hundred-and-seventy-second morning, the titan turned its head. Not toward the sun. Toward the settlement. Toward the people in the north field. Toward all of them.

And its smile got wider.
