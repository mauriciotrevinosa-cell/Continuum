*When: Day 172, from the morning after the titan into the night. Himmel has re-brushed his hair four times. Milim has told the fort about catching him eleven times, and the fort has made her a sash. Eren has been gone since dawn.*

Mau woke at eight and knew something had happened.

He did not know how he knew. He lay in the bed in the east room with the morning coming in gold through the new glass, and the house sounded wrong. Not loud. Too quiet, and then, in places, too loud. Voices in the yard, low and fast. A door. Someone laughing, high and shaky, the way people laugh after something. And under all of it, faintly, the smell of hot iron coming in through the window on the north wind, and something almost sweet.

Frieren was not in the bed.

She was in the reading chair, dressed, with her staff leaning against the arm of it and a cup of tea she had not drunk going cold on the shelf. She was watching him wake up. She had clearly been watching him for some time.

"Something happened," said Mau.

"Yes," said Frieren.

"Is everyone—"

"Everyone's fine," said Frieren. "Himmel's hair is not fine. Everyone else is fine."

He sat up.

She told him. All of it. Plainly, in order, the way she told him things when she had decided he should know them exactly. The shape on the ridge at a quarter past two. The kitchen at three. Eren's titans, that ate people and smiled. The cottages, and Aira on the step. Dawn. The heel and the knee. Milim, and Mikasa running up its back. Himmel in its fist. The nape. The steam. *It's empty.* And then Eren asking Ciel to look at him, and what Ciel found, and what she did not find.

Mau listened to all of it without moving.

When she had finished, he did not say anything for a long time. He sat on the edge of the bed with his hands on his knees and looked at the window. At the north light. At the smell of hot iron, going.

"You let me sleep," he said at last.

"Fern did," said Frieren. "Everyone agreed."

He nodded slowly.

Frieren watched his face. She knew what she was looking for. The thing that went across it when he found out something had come out of the dark and he had not been there in front of it. The thing that had gone across it at the long table nine days ago, at page four, when Fern shook her head.

It did not come.

He sat there on the edge of the bed, and thought about it, and she watched him think. And what went across his face instead was something slower. Something she had not seen there very often.

"Good," said Mau.

Frieren did not say anything.

"I'd have wanted to go," he said. "I'd have stood at the edge of the field and every part of me would have wanted to go in front. And I couldn't have, because of the rota, and because of Fern, and because of the walls." He looked at his hands. "So I'd have stood there wanting to. The whole time. And you'd all have had to watch me want to, as well as watching the titan." He paused. "That's one more thing to watch. You didn't need one more thing."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment.

Then she got up out of the reading chair and crossed the room and sat down on the bed beside him, close, with her shoulder against his. She did not say anything. She picked up his hand and held it in her lap, in both of hers, and looked at the window too.

"Mikasa didn't go after him," said Frieren, after a while. "Eren. When he walked away. She let him go."

"She's waited a long time," said Mau.

"Since they were nine," said Frieren. She was quiet for a moment. "I told her it gets harder at the very end. When you can see it's nearly over."

Mau looked at her.

"Does it?" he said.

Frieren did not answer for a long time. She held his hand in her lap and looked at the north light on the new glass.

"I'll tell you," she said, "when I get there."



Eren walked the lime line all day.

Everyone knew where he was. You could see him from anywhere on the north side of the settlement. A small dark figure walking, very slowly, along the white line of lime that Anko and the Searchers had laid in the winter to mark where the Goddess's protection ended. He walked it the way he always walked it, the long way round, from the red stake in the north field east past the timber yard and the barn and the dead earth of the south field, and round past the river and the low woods, and back. It was most of a day's walk, the whole line.

He walked it twice.

Nobody went after him. Fern had said not to, at breakfast, and nobody argued. Himmel went to the north end of the settlement at noon and stood there for a while watching him, a long way off, a small dark shape against the fields. Then Himmel went back inside without saying anything, and brushed his hair.

Mikasa stood at the north end for her hour, in the afternoon, the way she did every day.

She did not look at the line. She looked at the road, and the hills, and the bare black patch at the foot of the ridge where the frost had melted in the shape of two knees and a hand. She stood there for exactly an hour, with her new blades at her hip and one of her old ones, and the other old one gone. Then she went back inside and helped Momo with the potatoes.

When Momo asked if she was all right, Mikasa said, "Yes," and peeled a potato, and then peeled the same potato again, and Momo took it out of her hand very gently and gave her a new one and did not say anything else.



He came to the kilns at eleven.

Kaneki was there. Kaneki was always there, at that hour, on the night shift, raking the embers in the mouths of the three low humps of earth and turf at the edge of the timber yard, with his sleeves pushed up and the red light on his face. The small pot was on the flat stone at the edge of the fire. The two cups were upside down on the shelf in the bark shelter.

He did not look round when Eren came up. But he had heard him. He had heard him from the other side of the timber yard. He reached up, without turning, and took down one of the cups, and turned it over, and poured.

Black. Thick. Bitter. Steaming.

He held it out behind him.

Eren took it.

He sat down on the bench in the shelter. He did not drink. He held the cup in both hands and looked at the fire. His face was grey with tiredness. His boots were white to the ankle with lime dust. He had walked the whole line twice and he looked as if he had walked it a hundred times.

Kaneki raked the embers.

Neither of them said anything for a long time. That was how it was, between them. It had always been how it was. They had met on the night shift in the first winter, two people who did not sleep, and they had sat by fires together a great many nights since then without talking, and that had been enough. Kaneki was the only person in the house Eren had ever told about the walls. Not the story he told at the long table. The real one. And Eren was the only person in the house who knew the name of the woman Kaneki made two cups of coffee for, and only ever drank from one.

"Did you hear?" said Eren at last.

"I heard," said Kaneki.

Eren looked at the coffee.

"I've been counting," he said. "Since I found out. Every day. Thirteen years from the day I got it. I knew the date. I knew exactly how long." His voice was very flat. "Everything I did, I did knowing that. Every choice. I didn't—" He stopped. "There were things I didn't do. Because of the number. Things I didn't say. Because what's the point of saying something to someone if you're going to be dead in a few years and they're going to have to live with it?"

Kaneki did not say anything.

"And now there's no number," said Eren. "She looked. Ciel. And there's nothing. Nothing counting." He laughed. It was not a good sound. "I walked the line twice today. All the way round. Trying to work out what you do. When you've spent your whole life knowing exactly when it ends, and then someone tells you it doesn't." He looked at his hands round the cup. "I don't know how to have years. I've never had years."

Kaneki put the rake down.

He came over to the shelter and sat down on the bench beside Eren. Not close. Not far. He did not take down the second cup. He sat there with his elbows on his knees and looked at the fire with him.

"I thought I was going to die young too," said Kaneki.

Eren looked at him.

"Everyone did," said Kaneki. "Everyone who knew what I was. Ghouls don't get old, where I'm from. Not ones like me. Not ones who fight." He was looking at the embers. "I thought I had a few years. Maybe. If I was lucky. And then something would catch up with me, the way it caught up with everyone, and that would be that." He paused. "So I didn't say things either. To people. Because what was the point."

The kilns breathed.

"And then I didn't die," said Kaneki.

He said it very simply. As if it still surprised him.

"Everything that was supposed to kill me didn't," said Kaneki. "One after another. I kept not dying. And one day I looked up and I was older than I had ever thought I would be. And there was someone there." He paused. "She'd been there the whole time. Waiting. While I didn't say things."

Eren was quiet.

"Touka," he said.

Kaneki nodded.

He reached up, without looking, and touched the second cup on the shelf. The one he never poured. He did not take it down. He just touched it. Then he put his hand back on his knee.

"You know what I did," said Kaneki, "when I worked out I wasn't going to die?"

"What?"

"I married her," said Kaneki.

Eren stared at him.

"Not straight away," said Kaneki. "I'm not that brave. It took a while. I wasted a lot of days first. Being careful. Being sure. Saying *when this is over*." He almost smiled. "It's never over. That's the thing nobody tells you. There's always something else coming out of the hills. If you wait for it to be over, you wait forever." He looked at the fire. "So one day I stopped waiting for it to be over. And I said it. Badly. In a coffee shop. She hit me first." The almost-smile again. "And then she said yes."

Eren did not say anything for a long time.

He looked at the coffee in his hands. He drank some of it. It was terrible. It was the worst coffee he had ever had in his life, the way it always was.

"I keep saying it," said Eren, very quietly. "*When it's over.* To her. At the red stake. On the dating day, when she said it wasn't a date. I said *when this is over, I*—and I didn't finish it." He put the cup down on the bench. "I've been saying it for years. In my head. Since I was fifteen. *When it's over.*"

"It's not going to be over," said Kaneki.

"I know."

"Not in this world. Not in any of them." Kaneki looked at him sideways. "That's the good news, Eren. It's never going to be over. So you don't have to wait."

Eren put his face in his hands.

He did not cry. Or if he did, Kaneki did not look, and it did not matter. He sat on the bench in the bark shelter at the edge of the timber yard, with lime dust white on his boots and the red light of the kilns on him, with his face in his hands. And Kaneki sat beside him and looked at the fire and did not say anything at all, because there was nothing else to say. That was the whole of it.



After a long time, Eren lifted his head.

"How do I say it?" he said. His voice was rough. "I don't know how to say it. I've never said anything like that. I don't—" He stopped. "What do I say to her?"

Kaneki considered this seriously.

"I don't know," he said. "I said mine in a coffee shop and got hit." He picked up the rake again, and stood, and went back to the mouth of the middle kiln. "You don't have to say it right. You just have to stop saying *when it's over*." He raked the embers. "Say something else instead. Anything. Something with a tomorrow in it."

Eren sat on the bench and looked at the fire.

"Something with a tomorrow in it," he said slowly.

"You've never had one before," said Kaneki, without turning round. "Now you've got thousands. Use one."

Eren did not say anything else.

He sat there until the kilns had burned down to their night glow, and finished the terrible coffee, every drop, and rinsed the cup at the water barrel, and put it back on the shelf. Upside down. Next to the other one.

Then he stood looking at the two cups for a moment.

"You could bring her," he said. "Touka. You've got a coin."

Kaneki did not stop raking.

"I've got one coin," he said. "There's two of them. Her and our daughter." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm not bringing one without the other."

Eren looked at his back.

"Then we find another coin," said Eren.

Kaneki stopped raking.

He did not turn round. He stood at the mouth of the kiln with the rake in his hands and the red light on his face, very still, for a long moment.

"Go to bed, Eren," he said quietly.

Eren went.

But at the edge of the timber yard he stopped and looked back. Kaneki was still standing at the kiln, not raking. Just standing there. With one hand, Eren saw, resting on the shelf in the bark shelter. On the second cup. Not taking it down. Just resting there.

Eren walked back to the house through the dark, toward the room with two exits and the bunk beds, faster than he had walked all day. He did not go to bed. He sat on the bottom bunk, Mikasa's, in the dark, beside her sleeping shape, with his back against the wall, and did not wake her.

He sat there until the window began to go grey.

Then he touched her shoulder.
