*When: Dawn on Day 173, the morning after the titan. The kilns are banked; Kaneki is standing at the water barrel with two cups. Aira has slept, finally, on the cottage step with an old blade across her knees, and the bean-girl's mother put a blanket over both of them. In the third cottage on the north road, a family has been packing all night.*

He touched her shoulder, and she woke.

She always woke at once. She had always woken at once, since she was nine, since a night in a cabin in the woods that she did not think about. She opened her eyes in the grey dark of the room with two exits, on the bottom bunk, and he was sitting beside her with his back against the wall and lime dust on his boots, and his face was a face she had not seen on him before.

She did not say anything.

"Walk with me," said Eren.

She got up. She got her coat. She got her scarf, the red one, that she had worn every day of her life since she was nine, and wound it round her neck the way she always wound it, once and then again. She did not get her blades. She noticed herself not getting them, at the door, and stood for a moment with her hand out toward them where they hung on the peg, and then put her hand in her pocket instead, and went out.

He was waiting in the corridor.

They went out through the yard, past the ink tree, past the woodpile where Yuka sat to watch Gojo's lessons, and up through the sleeping settlement. North. Past the cottages on the north road, where Aira was asleep on a step under a blanket with a little girl curled against her. Past the place where the street ran out. Into the north field. To the red stake.

It was cold and very clear. The gate stood forty paces off, blue and humming in the grey. Beyond it, the black hills ran along the sky. And at the foot of the nearest ridge, where the shepherd's track came down, there was a patch of bare black earth in the frost, in the shape of two knees and a hand.

Eren stood at the stake with his hands in his pockets and looked at it.

Mikasa stood beside him. Half a step away. The way she had stood beside him at this stake, and at a hundred other places, in this world and the other one, for most of her life.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

The sky in the east went from grey to pearl. The stars went out one by one over the hills.

"I walked the line twice yesterday," said Eren.

"I know," said Mikasa. "I watched."

"All the way round. Both times." He was looking at the black patch at the foot of the ridge. "I was trying to work out what you do. When you've counted something your whole life, and then someone tells you there's nothing to count."

Mikasa did not say anything.

"I couldn't work it out," said Eren. "I walked all day and I couldn't." He paused. "Then I went to the kilns. And Kaneki told me something."

"What?"

"That it's never going to be over," said Eren.

Mikasa looked at him.

"That's what I keep saying," said Eren. "Isn't it. To you. Here. *When this is over.*" He took his hands out of his pockets. They were not clenched. They were just hands. "I've been saying it in my head since I was fifteen. When the walls are safe. When the titans are gone. When the war's over. When I've done what I have to do. When it's over, I'll—" He stopped. "And I never finish it. Because I knew. I knew there wasn't going to be a *when it's over*. Not for me. I had a number. And the number ran out before anything was ever over."

The north field was very quiet. Somewhere behind them, in the settlement, a door opened and closed. A cart creaked.

"I know," said Mikasa.

Eren turned his head and looked at her.

She was not looking at him. She was looking at the hills. Her face was perfectly still in the pearl-grey light, the way it was still when the most important things happened to her.

"I know about the number," said Mikasa quietly. "I know how it ends. The count." She paused. "I counted it myself. Afterward. On my fingers." Her hand moved in her pocket, very slightly, as if counting. "In another world. Alone. For a long time."

Eren did not say anything.

He had known that she knew. He had known it since the sea, in the first summer, when she looked at him across the sand with a face that knew too much and he had not asked. He knew there was a version of everything that she had lived and he had not. He knew she had come over the bridge from somewhere further down the road than he had. He had never asked how far.

He did not ask now.

"There was a road," said Mikasa. "In our world. That you were on. That everything was on." She did not look at him. "Ciel saw where it used to go. The root. Going down into nothing." Her voice did not change at all. "There's no road here, Eren."

He looked at her.

"Nothing to count down," said Mikasa. "Nothing to walk to the end of. No road." She turned her head, at last, and looked at him. Her dark eyes were very steady and very bright. "Whatever you were going to have to become on it, there isn't one. Not here. It can't happen here."

Eren stood very still.

He did not know everything she meant. He would not know, perhaps, for a long time, maybe ever. But he knew enough. He knew from her face. From the way her hand had stopped counting in her pocket. From the way she was looking at him, as if she were looking at someone she had been given back.

The sun came up over the black hills.

It came all at once, the way it did here, gold and sudden, and lit the frost and the north field and the patch of bare black earth at the foot of the ridge, and the blue gate, and the two of them standing at the red stake.

"Something with a tomorrow in it," said Eren.

Mikasa waited.

"That's what Kaneki said." He was not looking at the hills any more. He was looking at her. "Stop saying *when it's over*. Say something with a tomorrow in it." He swallowed. "I don't know how. I've never had one."

"Try," said Mikasa.

He tried.

"Walk with me tomorrow," said Eren.

Mikasa did not move.

"And the day after," said Eren. His voice was not steady now. "And the day after that. Here. Wherever. I don't care where." He took one breath. "I want to find out what I'm like at thirty. I don't know. I never thought about it. I want to find out if I get worse, or better, or just older. I want you to tell me when I'm being an idiot. When I'm forty. When I'm sixty." He laughed, once, short and shaking. "I don't even know what I look like at sixty. I never even *thought* about it, Mikasa, not once, I never let myself—"

He stopped.

"I love you," said Eren.

It came out very plainly. In the cold. At the red stake. In the first light. Not well. Not the way it would have come out in a story. It came out of him the way water comes out of a cracked jar, all at once, because the thing holding it in had finally given way.

"I've loved you since—I don't know since when," said Eren. "Since always. Since before I knew what it was. And I never said it. Because I wasn't allowed. Because I had a number, and you'd have had to live with it, after, and I wasn't going to do that to you. I wasn't going to make you count." His voice cracked. "And you counted anyway. Didn't you. You counted anyway."

Mikasa did not say anything for a long time.

She stood half a step away from him at the red stake with the sun on her face. Her face did not do anything. It did not do anything for so long that Eren began to think he had broken something.

Then her eyes filled.

She did not cry. Not the way other people cried. Her face stayed perfectly still, and her eyes filled, and two tears ran down her cheeks, one and then the other, and she did not wipe them away. She looked at him through them. Steadily. The way she had looked at him across a sea, a long time ago, knowing too much.

"Say it again tomorrow," said Mikasa.

Eren stared at her.

"Tomorrow," said Mikasa. Her voice was very low and not quite steady. "Say it again tomorrow. Here. At the stake."

"And the day after?" said Eren.

"And the day after." She did not look away. "Every day you've got."

He made a sound. It was not a word. He took the half step.

It was the first time. In all the years. In this world and the other one. In all the places they had stood side by side, at stakes and walls and fences, at the edge of things, half a step apart. He took it. And then there was no half step any more, and he was standing in front of her, close, and the scarf had come loose in the cold wind, one end trailing.

He picked it up.

He did not think about it. His hands did it. He picked up the loose end of the red scarf and wound it round her neck. Once. And then again. Slowly. Carefully. The way he had wound it round her once, a long time ago, in a cabin in the woods, when she was nine and shaking and had just lost everything. *It's warm, isn't it?*

He had not remembered that he remembered.

Mikasa stood very still while he did it. Her eyes were still full. When he had finished, and his hands were still on the ends of the scarf, at her collar, she put her own hands over his. And held them there.

"It's warm," said Mikasa. Very quietly. "Isn't it."

Eren looked at her.

Then he put his forehead down against hers, in the cold, at the red stake, in the gold light, and closed his eyes. And stayed there.

Neither of them said anything else for a long time.



They walked back across the north field together.

Not half a step apart. Not touching shoulders. His hand in hers, or hers in his. Neither of them could have said which. They walked back across the frost with their hands together between them, the way people walk who have walked together their whole lives and have only just noticed.

At the place where the street began, they stopped.

There was a cart in the north road.

It was outside the third cottage. A small cart, with a mule, loaded with what a family could carry. A rolled mattress. A box of pots. A chair with one leg mended. The father was tying the last of the ropes. The mother stood by the cottage door with her arms folded and her eyes on the ground. And a boy of about seven sat on top of the load, holding a bundle, looking north at the bare black patch at the foot of the ridge.

The third cottage. The family that had come over the bridge from somewhere else, the way all of them had, and stayed when the others went home at the end of the ten days, and been given the cottage and a stove and a place on the step of Arrival House. One of the last three.

They had seen the steam.

Eren and Mikasa stood at the end of the street and watched.

The father finished the rope. He saw them. He stopped, and looked at them, with their hands together, and something went across his face that was not anger, and not quite shame. Fear. Plain fear. The fear of a man who had seen something three storeys high stand on a ridge in the moonlight and smile at the house where his children slept.

"I'm sorry," said the father. To them. Across the road. "We're sorry. You've been—everyone's been—" He stopped. "We can't. The children. If it comes again. If something bigger—" He stopped again. "The river towns are safer. They say. Nobody there knows what we are." He looked at his son on the cart. "We'll keep it that way."

"I know," said Mikasa.

The man looked at her.

"I know," said Mikasa again, quietly. "Go. It's all right."

The father nodded. He could not seem to say anything else. He helped his wife up onto the cart. He took the mule's halter. And the cart began to move, slowly, down the north road, toward the place where the road turned south, toward the river and the ford and the river towns.

On the step of the next cottage, Aira was awake.

She was sitting up under the blanket, with the old blade across her knees and the bean-girl still asleep against her side. She was watching the cart go. Her face was set. She did not say anything. She did not try to stop them. She watched them go, the way Mikasa had watched Eren walk away yesterday, all the way, until they were round the turn.

As the cart passed her step, the boy on top of the load looked down at her. At the blade across her knees.

He lifted one hand. A small wave.

Aira lifted hers.

Then the cart was round the turn, and gone, and the north road was empty, and there were two refuge families left in the settlement, with three children.

Eren and Mikasa stood at the end of the street with their hands together.

"It's started," said Eren quietly. "Hasn't it. People leaving. Because of us."

"Because of what's coming," said Mikasa. "Not because of us." She looked at the empty road. "There's a difference."

Eren did not say anything.

But his hand tightened on hers. And hers on his.

They went in.



They came into the common room for breakfast at half past seven, with their hands together, and the common room went quiet from the door inward, the way it did.

Nobody said anything.

Nobody made a slate. Umaru, in the arch of the fort, opened her mouth, and looked at their hands, and looked at Mikasa's face, which was still very still, with the tracks of two dried tears on it that she had not wiped away. Umaru closed her mouth again. She sat down on the toll booth platform and put the Ministry slate face down on her knees, and did not write anything on it at all.

At the long table, Momo had already put out two bowls. Side by side. At the end of the bench. Close.

Mikasa looked at the two bowls.

She sat down at one of them. Eren sat down at the other. Their hands came apart, because you cannot eat porridge with one hand, and they ate their porridge side by side at the end of the long table. And under the table, every so often, Eren's knee touched hers. And stayed.

At the far end, Kaneki came in from the night shift with lime dust on his own boots. He saw them, and stopped in the doorway for a moment. Then he went past them to the cold-hearth end without saying anything. On the way, he put one hand very briefly on Eren's shoulder, and took it away again.

At the head of the table, Fern opened the ledger. She wrote something. Then she looked at it for a long time, and did not cross it out.

Mau was halfway down the bench, beside Frieren. He saw the two bowls. He saw their hands come apart over the porridge. He saw Mikasa's face, still, with the two dry tear-tracks on it.

He looked at Frieren.

Frieren was looking at Mikasa. Her serene face did not change. But under the table, she found his hand, and held it. Not hard. Just held it.

"She waited since she was nine," said Frieren, very quietly, so only he could hear.

"I know," said Mau.

"It got harder at the very end," said Frieren. "I could see it on her. Yesterday." She was quiet for a moment. "It was worth it."

Mau looked at her. At the elf beside him, who had lived a thousand years and would live a thousand more, and who had told him, in this room, at this table, that two years was a sneeze.

"Is it?" he said. "Worth it?"

Frieren did not answer at once.

She held his hand under the long table and looked at Mikasa and Eren, side by side at the end of the bench, with their knees touching. Eating porridge. Not saying anything. Not needing to.

"Ask me tomorrow," said Frieren.
