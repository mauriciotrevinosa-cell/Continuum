*When: Day 176, evening. Eren has said it at the red stake three mornings running, and Mikasa has said "again tomorrow" three times. Himmel's hair has recovered. Aira wears the old blade with the nick at her hip now, and the bean-girl follows her everywhere. The spring rain is coming; you can smell it.*

Rem poured.

She always poured. Every evening, at the long table, when the soup was done and the bowls were stacked and the long table had gone loud and warm, the way it went after a meal, Rem stood up. She went to the hearth and took the big teapot off its hook, the brown one with the chipped spout, and filled it, and came back. And she went round. The whole table. Every cup. From Seiko on her high seat at one end to the fort's cups on the toll booth platform at the other. Forty cups, more or less. She went round all of them, every evening, in her maid's dress and her apron, with her small careful smile, and filled every one.

Then she put the teapot back on its hook. And then she stood by the hearth, with her hands folded on her apron, while everyone drank. Until someone needed more. And then she went round again.

She did not sit down. She never sat down. Not until the long table had emptied, and the cups were on the rack, and the lamps were going out. Then, sometimes, she sat for a minute on the end of the bench, alone, and drank whatever was left in the bottom of the pot, cold, from her own cup. And then she washed it, and went to bed.

Mau had been watching her do it for more than a year.

He had not known he was watching. He had just seen it, the way you see the same thing every day without noticing that you see it. Rem and the teapot. Rem by the hearth. Rem, last, alone, with the cold dregs. It was part of the room, like the beam across the ceiling and the fort's curtain and Seiko's fan.

Tonight he noticed.

He did not know why tonight. Maybe it was the titan, and Eren, and the red stake. Maybe it was Mikasa's face at breakfast three days ago, with the two dry tear-tracks on it, and the two bowls side by side. Maybe it was Kaneki, and the two cups on the shelf by the kilns, and the one he never poured. Maybe it was just that Shoko had made him sit still for two weeks with a blindfold on and nothing to do but listen. And when you sit still long enough, you hear things that have been happening in the room the whole time.

He watched Rem go round the long table with the brown teapot. Seiko. Fern. Stark. The witches' end. The cold-hearth end, where Gojo held out his cup and said something that made Shoko roll her eyes. The fort. Ori. Frieren, beside him. Him.

She filled his cup. She smiled her small careful smile.

"Thank you," said Mau.

"You're welcome," said Rem, and moved on.

He watched her finish. Put the teapot back on its hook. Go and stand by the hearth with her hands folded on her apron. Watching the cups. Waiting.

At the far end of the table, Ram, beside Seika, was watching her too.

"Sit down, Rem," said Ram.

She said it without looking up from her own cup. Flatly. The way she said everything. She had said it every evening since she came, Mau realised. Every single evening. *Sit down, Rem.* And every evening Rem had said—

"In a minute, sister," said Rem.

And had not sat down.

Ram did not say anything else. Her face did not change. But her hand, round her cup, had gone white at the knuckles.

Mau put his cup down.

He got up.

Frieren looked up at him. He did not look at her. He went down the length of the long table, past the bench, past Fern, past the witches, to the hearth, to where Rem was standing with her hands folded on her apron, watching the cups. And he reached past her, and took the brown teapot off its hook.

Rem turned round very fast.

"That's mine," she said.

She said it before she could stop herself. It came out sharp. Sharper than anything Mau had ever heard her say. And her hand had come up, toward the teapot, toward his wrist, the way a hand comes up for something that is being taken.

Then she heard herself. And her hand stopped in the air. And her face went very still.

The long table had gone quiet.

Mau stood by the hearth with the teapot in one hand. He did not let go of it. He did not give it back.

"I know," he said. "I'm just borrowing it."

He went to the long table. To the end of the bench nearest the hearth, where there was a gap, always, because Rem never sat in it. He took a clean cup off the rack, and put it down in the gap. And he poured.

Badly. The brown teapot was heavier than it looked, and the spout was chipped, and he had never poured from it in his life. Tea went into the cup and over the side of the cup and onto the table. Momo, at the kitchen door, winced, very visibly, and did not move.

He put the teapot down. He pulled the bench out a little, at the gap.

Then he looked at Rem.

She was standing by the hearth. She had not moved. Her hands had come back down and folded themselves on her apron again, very tightly. She was looking at the cup on the table, in the gap, in a small brown puddle of spilt tea. Her face was doing something Mau had never seen it do.

It was afraid.

Not of him. Not of anything in the room. Of the cup. Of the gap. Of the bench pulled out.

"What do you want?" said Rem.

It came out very quietly. Very carefully. Each word a cup she might drop. The long table was completely silent.

"Nothing," said Mau.

"Everyone wants something." Rem did not move from the hearth. "When someone does something for you. They want something. That's how it works. You do things, and they owe you, or you owe them, and that's—" She stopped. "What do you want?"

Mau looked at her.

He thought about weeks, in another season. When he had not known what was real. When the Noise had been so loud he could not tell his own hands from someone else's, and could not remember the names of the people at the table, and had woken every morning not knowing where he was. And every morning, there had been someone at the side of the bed. With a cup. Small, in a maid's dress, with blue hair, with a careful smile. Every morning, for weeks. Pouring. Waiting. Never sitting down. Never asking for anything at all.

"I want you to sit down," said Mau. "Sometimes. That's all."

Rem stared at him.

At the far end of the long table, Ram put her cup down. Hard. It clicked on the board, and everyone looked at her.

"Sit," said Ram.

Rem looked at her sister.

"Sit, Rem," said Ram. Her voice was perfectly flat. Her face did not change at all. "The sheep is serving you. Badly. He has spilled tea on Momo's table and Momo is going to be very upset about it later." She picked her cup back up. "It is barely acceptable. Sit down and drink it before it gets any worse."

Nobody laughed.

Rem stood by the hearth for a long moment, with her hands folded on her apron, looking at her sister. Something passed between the two of them, down the length of the long table, that nobody else could read.

Then Rem crossed the room. Slowly. To the end of the bench. To the gap. To the cup in its puddle of spilt tea. And sat down.

She sat very straight. She put her hands round the cup. She did not drink at first. She just held it. Looking at it. As if it were something she had never seen before.

Mau picked up the brown teapot again.

He went round. The whole long table. Seiko. Fern. Stark. The witches. The cold-hearth end. The fort. Every cup. He spilled on Seiko, who looked at him over her fan. He spilled on Gojo, who said "*Hot,*" and then, "No, it's fine, it's an honour." He spilled on two separate members of the fort, and Umaru wrote something on her slate and then rubbed it out. He did not spill on Frieren, because Frieren held her cup very still and looked at him the whole time with an expression he could not read, and he was very careful.

When he had gone all the way round, he put the teapot back on its hook. And he did not stand by the hearth. He went back to his place on the bench, beside Frieren, and sat down.

At the end of the bench nearest the hearth, Rem was drinking her tea.

Slowly. Small sips. Both hands round the cup. Her face down. Nobody looked at her. Everyone very carefully did not look at her. The long table had started, slowly, to make noise again. Senku explaining something to Suika. Gojo complaining about his scalded hand to Shoko, who was not listening. The fort arguing about something on the toll booth platform.

When she had finished, Rem put the cup down. She looked at it for a moment, empty, in the dried brown ring of spilt tea.

Then she got up, and went to the hearth, and took the teapot off its hook. And went round again. Every cup. The whole table.

But this time, when she had finished, she did not stand by the hearth. She came back to the gap at the end of the bench. And sat down in it. And poured the last of the pot into her own cup, still hot. And drank it there.

Ram, at the far end, did not say anything. She did not look at her sister. But her hand round her cup was not white any more.



He was washing the cups when she found him.

It was late. The common room had emptied, and the lamps were going out, and he had stayed. He had told Frieren to go up. *One end*, Anko's rule four said, and cups were well under one end, and he had taken the whole rack of them to the kitchen and was standing at the big stone sink with his sleeves rolled up, washing them, one at a time. Badly, because he had never done this either.

He heard her stop in the kitchen doorway.

He did not turn round. He went on washing.

"Why," said Rem.

Not a question. Very quiet.

Mau put a cup on the drying board.

"You poured for me every day," he said. "For weeks. When I didn't know what was real." He picked up another cup. "In the bad season. When I didn't know anyone's names. When I woke up every morning not knowing where I was." He washed the cup. "There was always someone there. With tea. Every morning. Small. Blue hair. Smiling like she was being careful not to break anything." He put it on the board. "You never sat down once. All those weeks. I counted, afterward. When I could count again."

Rem did not say anything.

"I never said thank you," said Mau. "Not properly. I didn't know how, then. And then I was better, and you were just—there. By the hearth. With the teapot. Every night. And it was part of the room, and I stopped seeing it." He picked up another cup. "I saw it tonight."

"That was my job," said Rem.

He turned round, then.

She was standing in the kitchen doorway in her maid's dress, with her hands folded on her apron. Her face was very still. Very careful.

"Who gave it to you?" said Mau.

Rem opened her mouth.

She did not say anything. She stood in the doorway with her mouth a little open, and Mau watched her look for the answer, and not find it. Watched her look back, through a mansion in another world, and a village before that, and a sister with a broken horn. For who had given her the job. Who had told her that her place was by the hearth, with the teapot, never sitting down. Who had said she had to earn every cup.

"Nobody," said Rem, at last. Very quietly. "I gave it to myself."

"Then I'm taking it off you," said Mau. "Sometimes."

Rem looked at him.

"Not all the time," said Mau. "I'm terrible at it. You saw. Momo's going to kill me about the table." He almost smiled. "But sometimes. Some nights. I'll pour. And you'll sit." He turned back to the sink. "That's all I want. You asked."

He went on washing the cups.

Behind him, in the doorway, Rem did not move for a long time.

Then she came into the kitchen. She took a cloth off the hook by the stove. She came and stood beside him at the big stone sink, and picked up the first wet cup off the drying board, and began to dry it.

Neither of them said anything.

They did the whole rack. He washed, badly. She dried, perfectly. When they were done, she hung the cloth back on its hook, and stood for a moment looking at the clean cups in their rows.

"You spilled on Seiko," said Rem.

"I know."

"Nobody spills on Seiko."

"I know."

Rem's mouth moved. Very slightly. Not the careful smile. Something else, underneath it, that she did not quite let out.

"Good night, Mau," she said.

She went out.

He heard her go up the north corridor, toward the turn, toward the narrow room with REM on the door in pencil. He heard the door open and close.

And on the roof above the kitchen, very softly, so softly that at first he thought he had imagined it, he heard the first drops of the spring rain begin to fall.
