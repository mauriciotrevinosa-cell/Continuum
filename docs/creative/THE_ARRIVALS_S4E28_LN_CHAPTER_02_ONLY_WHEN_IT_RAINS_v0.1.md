*When: Day 177. It has rained since the small hours and will rain all day. The gate party did not go in. Kessoku are in the music room with the door open, playing the ninth song for Echidna, slowly. The fort has Yuka and the white dog and a game nobody will explain. Momo has said nothing about her table, which is worse than saying something.*

It rained all day.

Not hard. Steadily. The soft grey spring rain that comes after the last of the cold and does not stop, that runs off the bark shingles of the real house in a thousand small streams and fills the water barrels and turns the yard to mud and the dead earth of the south field, for the first time, dark. Everyone stayed in. The gate party did not go. The washing court was empty. Gojo stood in the yard for a full minute with his face turned up and his arms out and his blindfold off, letting the rain hit him, and then came in soaked and very happy and was sent upstairs by Shoko to change before he caught something.

Mau spent the morning in the common room, with the blindfold on for his two hours, listening to the house.

It was a good house to listen to in the rain. Kessoku in the music room, with the door open, playing the ninth song over and over, slowly, Seika stopping them every few bars to say *again*. Bocchi tried the same two bars once more, with Kita counting softly beside her, Nijika keeping the beat and Ryo waiting on the bass. The fort behind its curtain, where Yuka had been initiated into a game that involved the white dog, a cushion and a great deal of shrieking. Senku and Suika in the lab, arguing about a lens. Momo in the kitchen, scrubbing something on the long table, very hard, in a silence that everyone in the house had learned to fear.

Rem was in the kitchen too. He could hear her. She was helping Momo. He could hear her small quick steps, and the clink of cups, and once, very quietly, her voice saying something to Momo that made Momo stop scrubbing.

She did not come into the common room all day.



He found the cup outside the east room door at dusk.

He almost stepped on it. He came up the main stair after dinner, which Rem had not poured, because Rem had not come to dinner, and Ram had looked at the empty gap at the end of the bench all through the soup and not said anything. And there it was, on the floor, in front of the east room door. A teacup. Upside down. On a saucer.

Mau stopped and looked at it.

Frieren, behind him on the stair, stopped too.

"What is it?" said Mau.

Frieren looked at the cup for a long moment.

"It's an invitation," she said.

He looked at her.

"In her world," said Frieren, "in the big houses. The ones with maids. When a servant wanted to speak to someone, and couldn't knock, because it wasn't her place to knock." She looked at the upturned cup. "She'd leave a cup. Upside down. Outside the door." She paused. "I read about it once. In a book Echidna lent me."

Mau picked it up.

It was one of the plain white cups from the long table. Clean. Dry. Turned over on its saucer, very precisely, with the handle pointing up the corridor. North. Toward the turn.

"She wants me to come," said Mau.

"Yes."

He looked at Frieren.

"Go on," said Frieren. She took the cup and saucer out of his hands and went past him into the east room. "I'll be in the chair." She stopped in the doorway, with her back to him. "Take your time."



The narrow room was at the turn of the north corridor, where it bent round toward the west wing.

It had been the narrow room nobody marked, once. In the Muslin, in the autumn, when the house was one floor of posts and slotted boards, Mau had walked the corridor with Marin and her notebook and named every room. And when he came to this one, the narrow one at the turn, with its one window and its door that did not quite close, he had not named it. He had walked past it. Very carefully not looking at it. And it had stayed unmarked, with nothing written on the plan, for a whole season.

Now there was a slip of card in the little brass holder on the door. And on the card, in the ink Mau had first noticed after Ram came, in Rem's own hand:

*REM.*

And under it, in someone else's hand, still in pencil, added by the fort on the night the sisters made Rem one of them:

*(SISTER)*

Mau knocked.

The door opened at once.

Rem stood in it. Not in her maid's dress. In a plain grey dress he had never seen her wear, with her blue hair loose, without its ribbon, and no apron. She looked smaller without the apron. Younger. Her hands did not know where to go, without an apron to fold them on.

"You came," said Rem.

"You left a cup."

"I didn't know if you'd know what it meant."

"I didn't," said Mau. "Frieren did."

Rem almost smiled. "Of course she did." She stepped back. "Come in. Please."



The room was very small.

A bed against one wall, made with corners you could cut yourself on. A chair. A small table under the window, with a lamp on it, and a teapot. Not the big brown one. A small one, white, with blue flowers, that he had never seen before. Two cups. A window, with the rain running down it in long silver lines, and beyond it, the dark yard and the ink tree and the wet black shingles of the west wing.

That was all. There was nothing on the walls. Nothing on the floor. It was the room of someone who had owned nothing for a very long time and had not yet learned how to own things.

Rem went to the table.

She poured. Two cups. From the small white pot with the blue flowers, carefully, without spilling a drop. Then she put the pot down.

And sat.

She sat down on the chair by the table, first, before him. With her own cup in front of her. And looked up at him, standing in the middle of her room, with an expression that was almost defiant.

"You can have the bed," said Rem. "It's the only other place."

He sat on the edge of the bed. She pushed the second cup across the table toward him.

They drank. The rain ran down the window.

"I asked Fern for this room," said Rem, after a while, "only when it rains."

"I know."

"Did you?" She looked at him over her cup. "I asked her in the kitchen. On the night of the big rain, in the Muslin, when nobody worked. I said, could I use the narrow room. Only when it rains." She turned the cup in her hands. "And Fern looked at me for a long time. And then she wrote my name on the plan. In pencil. And she didn't write anything about rain."

"She wouldn't," said Mau.

"No." Rem looked at the window. "It's been raining for a year, I think. As far as Fern's concerned."

Mau did not say anything.

"I knew it was yours," said Rem.

He looked at her.

"The room," said Rem. "That you'd kept it. I worked it out. Later." She did not look away from the window. "Everybody else had a name on the plan. Every room, every door, from the first night. And this one had nothing. For a whole season. Not storage. Not a cupboard. Nothing. And you walked past it every day, and you never once looked at it." She paused. "People don't not-look at things that hard unless they mean something."

Mau was quiet for a long moment.

"I didn't ask you," he said.

"I know. Why?"

"Ori told me not to."

Rem turned her head and looked at him. Something went across her face very fast.

"Ori," she said.

"The night you went back to the inn," said Mau. "After the bad season. You were on the cart, and you said thank you for dinner. And I came back up the corridor and Ori was standing here. By this door." He looked at the floor. "And I said I wanted you to stay. And she said, then don't ask her." He paused. "She said if I asked, you'd say yes for me. You'd stay because I wanted it. She said, leave it empty, let her find it, let her say yes for herself."

Rem did not say anything for a long time.

She sat on the chair in her plain grey dress with her hair loose and her cup in both hands, and looked at him. Her face was very still. Not careful. Something else.

"She was right," said Rem at last, very quietly. "I would have."

Mau looked at her.

"If you'd asked," said Rem. "I'd have said yes. At once. For you. Because you'd been kind, and you wanted it, and that's what I do. That's what I've always done." Her hands tightened on the cup. "And I'd never have trusted it. Not for one day. I'd have lived in this room waiting for someone to tell me I'd been wrong to think it was mine. Waiting to be sent back."

The rain ran down the window.

"I don't trust people, Mau," said Rem.

She said it very simply. Without apology.

"I never have," she said. "In my world, I trusted my sister. And one boy. That was all. Everyone else, I watched." She looked down at her cup. "When I came to your house, I watched everyone. I watched Rimuru, because he was a monster. I watched Frieren, because she was the strongest. I watched Kaneki, because he smelled like something that ate people." She almost smiled. "We walked together for three days, looking for you, and neither of us called it trust."

"I know," said Mau. "He told me."

"I watched you most of all," said Rem.

She looked up at him.

"You smelled wrong," she said.

Mau went very still.

"I can smell things," said Rem. "Where I come from, what I am. I can smell things on people that other people can't. I could smell it on you, in the bad season. Something too big. In a jar too small for it. Something pressing against the inside of you, all the time, trying to get out." She was not looking away. "That's why I stayed by the bed every morning. Not because it was my job. Because I could smell it, and nobody else could, and I wanted to be there if the jar broke."

Mau did not say anything. He could not.

"And then," said Rem, "you went into the white place."

The room was very quiet. Even the rain seemed quieter.

"And you came out," said Rem. "And I smelled you. At the long table. The first night you came down." Her voice did not change. But her hands, round the cup, were white. "And you smelled like someone I knew."

"Who?" said Mau. His voice came out strange.

"The boy," said Rem. "The one I trusted. In my world." She was quiet for a moment. "He used to come back from nights he couldn't explain. Or mornings. He'd be in the middle of something, and then he'd stop, and his face would go—" She stopped. "As if he'd been somewhere else. Somewhere terrible. And died there. And come back. And he could never tell anyone. Not me. Not anyone. He'd just stand there in the kitchen with that face, and smile at me, and say *it's nothing, Rem*." She looked at her cup. "And I could smell it on him. Every time. Like ash. Like something burned a long time ago. And I loved him, and I never knew what it was. And he never told me."

Mau sat on the edge of the bed with his cup forgotten in his hands.

"When you came out of the white place," said Rem, "you smelled of it." She looked up at him. "Hundreds of times over. More than he ever did. So much I could hardly sit at the table." Her eyes were very bright. "And then, a week later, you told us. At the long table. Four hundred and thirty-seven. And the whole table went still." She paused. "And I didn't."

"You already knew," said Mau.

"I'd known for a week," said Rem. "I'd known since the first night. I could smell every one of them on you." She put her cup down on the table, very carefully. "And I didn't say anything. Because I thought, he'll tell us. He's not like the boy. He'll tell us." Her voice cracked, for the first time. "And you did. You told us. You stood up at the table and told all of us, every one, the number. Out loud." She looked at him. "He never did that. Not once. He carried it all by himself until the end."

Mau did not move.

Rem looked at him for a long moment across the little room, with the rain running down the window behind her.

"That's why I stayed," she said quietly. "Not the room. Not the tea. Not because you were kind." She paused. "Because you were the boy I knew, and you *told*."

Mau put his cup down on the floor.

He did not know what to say. He sat on the edge of the narrow bed with his hands on his knees, and looked at the small blue-haired girl in the plain grey dress, who had stood beside his bed every morning in the worst weeks of his life because she could smell something about to break, and had never said a word about it to anyone. Who had sat at the long table on the first night after the Trial and smelled four hundred and thirty-seven deaths on him and said nothing, and waited a week, trusting him to tell. Who did not trust anybody.

"I didn't know," said Mau. "That you could tell. That you knew. Any of it."

"I know," said Rem. "That's why I'm telling you now."



She showed him the door.

Not the outside of it. The inside. She got up, and went to it, and closed it, and pointed. On the inside of the door, low down, at the height of someone sitting on the floor with their back against the wall, there were marks. Small pencil marks. Tally marks, in rows of five. Rows and rows of them. Dozens. More than dozens.

"Every time it rained," said Rem. "In the first year. I made a mark. To check." She touched them with one finger. "That I was still allowed. That nobody had come to tell me it was only for when it rains, and it had stopped raining, and I should go."

Mau looked at the marks.

They went on for a long way. Row after row. And then they stopped. In the middle of a row. Three marks, and then nothing. The rest of the door clean.

"When did you stop?" he said.

Rem looked at the place where the marks stopped.

"The night before Ram's coin," she said. "When I came to your room after dark, and broke Fern's rule, and stood by your shelf and told you I was frightened it would go cold." She was quiet for a moment. "And you said, *that's not someone who's going to make a coin go cold*." Her finger rested on the last mark. "I came back here afterward. And I sat down on the floor to make a mark. Because it was raining that night too. And I had the pencil in my hand." She took her finger away. "And I didn't."

She turned round and looked at him.

"That's when I stopped checking," said Rem. "Pencil was enough. It was mine then too." She glanced toward the other side of the door. "I didn't write it in ink so you'd let me stay. I wanted my name there."



They sat for a long time after that, with the rain.

She poured again. Both cups. And sat down first again, and this time she did it without the almost-defiant look, as if she were practising, as if it were something she meant to get good at.

"You took care of me last night," said Rem, after a while. "With the teapot. In front of everyone."

"Badly."

"Badly," Rem agreed. "Momo is still not speaking about her table." She looked down into her cup. "But you did it. So now I'll say what I should have said a long time ago, instead of pouring." She took a breath. "I'm not your caretaker."

Mau waited.

"I was," said Rem. "Once. When you needed one. When the jar was nearly breaking and you didn't know anyone's name. I was your caretaker then, and I'm glad I was, and I'd do it again." She looked up. "But that was a season. It's over. You know my name now." She paused. "I don't know what I am now. To you. I've never had a word for it."

"Family," said Mau.

Rem looked at him.

"That's what I've been calling it," said Mau. "In my head. Since the room. Since before you wrote your name." He did not look away. "You took care of me when I didn't know what was real. That's what family does. And I kept a room for you without knowing if you'd come. That's what family does too." He picked up his cup. "I just never said it out loud. Because Ori told me not to ask."

"Family doesn't pour for each other every night," said Rem. Very quietly.

"Sometimes it does," said Mau. "Sometimes the other one pours."

Rem did not say anything.

She sat on the chair in her plain grey dress, with her hair loose, and her cup in both hands, and looked at him for a long time. And then something happened to her face that he had never seen happen to it. Not the careful smile. Not the small quick smile she had found again when Ram came back. Something underneath both of them, that she had been holding down for a very long time.

She laughed.

Not loudly. Rem did not do anything loudly. But it was a real laugh, low and surprised, and it went on for a moment, and she put her hand over her mouth, the way Yuka did, as if she had not meant to.

"Family," said Rem, through her hand. "With a sheep."

"With a sheep," said Mau.

She took her hand away. Her eyes were wet. She did not wipe them.

"Then I'll say one more thing," said Rem. "As family. And then you can go back to Frieren, and I'll go to sleep, and tomorrow I'll pour, and some days you'll pour, and we'll never talk about tonight again." She put her cup down. "The boy I knew. He died for us. Over and over. And came back. And carried it alone, and smiled, and said *it's nothing, Rem*." She looked at him. "Don't you *dare* be him."

Mau did not say anything.

"If something comes for you," said Rem. "If something calls you. If there's ever a night when you stand somewhere and your face goes like that, and you know you're going to have to do something terrible for us." Her voice did not rise. It did not need to. "You tell someone first. Before. Not after. Not with a note." She held his eyes. "You tell someone first."

The rain ran down the window.

"I promised that once," said Mau quietly. "To Ori. At the tidepools. *You first, before I go.*" He looked at his hands. "And I broke it. I left her a note."

"I know," said Rem. "She told me. In the fort."

"So I don't—"

"Then don't break it again," said Rem.

Mau looked up at her.

She was sitting very straight in the chair, in the lamplight, with the rain behind her, and her blue hair loose. Her face was not careful at all. It was perfectly, frighteningly clear. The face of someone who had loved one boy who never told her anything, and had decided, in a narrow room in another world, that she was never going to let it happen again.

"I won't," said Mau.

Rem looked at him for a long moment.

"Good," she said.

She picked up the small white pot with the blue flowers, and poured the last of it, half into his cup and half into hers. And sat back. And they drank it, together, in the narrow room at the turn of the north corridor, while the rain ran down the window and the ink tree stood black in the wet yard below.

"It's raining," said Rem, after a while. Looking at the window.

"You're allowed to be here when it doesn't," said Mau.

Rem smiled. A real one. Small, and not careful at all.

"I know," she said. "That's why I wrote it."



When he came back to the east room, Frieren was in the reading chair, with the grimoire open on her knee and the old silver compass on the arm of the chair. The upturned cup and saucer were on the shelf in the middle of the floor, beside the cars.

She looked up at him.

She did not ask. She looked at his face for a long time, the way she looked at things she was reading. Then she closed the grimoire.

"Family?" said Frieren.

Mau stared at her.

"Elves," said Frieren. "Ears." She stood up. "The walls in this house are clay and straw, and you have never once in your life talked quietly." She came across the room and stood in front of him. "She's right," she said. "About the boy. About telling first."

"I know."

"You'll tell me first," said Frieren. "Before. If it's ever—" She stopped. "If something ever calls you."

Mau looked down at her.

He did not know, then, that something would. That something already had, once, a long time ago, in a clearing, and he had said *it's not before, it's just before you*. That it would again. That one night, months from now, his whole body would tell him to go, and he would stand in the dark and remember a narrow room, and the rain, and a girl with her hair loose saying *don't you dare be him*.

He did not know any of that. He only knew that she was asking.

"First," said Mau. "Before. I promise."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment.

Then she put her cold foot on top of his, in the middle of the east room, in the evening, which was not the reset hour, which was not anything at all.

"One," said Frieren. "Still here."

"Still here," said Mau.

In her narrow room at the turn of the north corridor, Rem lay in bed and listened to the rain on the roof. She had not made a mark on the door. She had not even thought about it. She lay there in the dark with her hands folded on the blanket, the way she used to fold them on her apron, and then, after a while, unfolded them.

And slept.
