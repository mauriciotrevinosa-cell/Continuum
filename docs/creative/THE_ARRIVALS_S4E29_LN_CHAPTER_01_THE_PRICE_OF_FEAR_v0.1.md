*When: Day 180. The rain has stopped and the roads are mud to the ankle. Rem pours, and some nights sits; last night Mau poured and spilled on nobody. Eren said it at the red stake this morning for the seventh time. In the low woods, nothing has walked the line for a week.*

The southern merchant came up the bridge road on the hundred-and-eightieth day with two guards instead of one, and a cart that was half empty.

Mikasa saw him first, from the north end, and went down to meet him. Eren went with her. Not half a step behind, any more. Beside. The merchant saw that from a long way off, and when they reached him at the bottom of the road he looked at the two of them, and at the space between them that was not there any more, and something in his tired face eased very slightly.

"Well," he said. "That's something good, at least."

He had got thinner. He had been a stout, cheerful man when he first started coming up from the south, a year and a half ago, with cocoa and needles and salt and gossip, and he was still cheerful, mostly. But the stoutness had gone somewhere. His coat hung on him. The guard beside him was the same one as in the spring, the broad quiet man with the scar through one eyebrow. And behind them, walking at the back of the cart with a crossbow over her shoulder, there was a second guard, a woman with grey in her hair and the walk of someone who had been a soldier once and had not stopped walking like one.

"New guard," said Mikasa.

"New guard," said the merchant. "Two guards, for a cart this size." He laughed, without much in it. "Two guards for half a cart. That's what the road costs now."

He sold Momo salt and lamp oil and a bolt of cloth. That was all he had. He bought forty pots of hand cream from Maomao, and twenty jars of Daphne's honey, and stood in the kitchen doorway looking at the long table, where the house was eating, with his hat in his hands.

"I'd like to talk to you," he said. "All of you. If I may. After."



They gave him soup first. They always gave him soup first. He sat at the long table between Fern and Momo and ate two bowls, slowly, with both hands round them, and the house let him, and nobody asked anything until he was done. The guards sat at the end of the bench nearest the door. The scarred man ate his soup in silence, with his back to the wall, the way he always did. The grey-haired woman did not eat at first. She sat very straight, with her crossbow beside her, and looked round the long table at all of them. At the fort's curtain. At Rimuru, who was a slime on the table that evening, eating out of a bowl. At Gojo with his blindfold. At the seven witches at the far end. At Milim, upside down on the bench.

She did not say anything. But her hand did not leave her crossbow.

Momo put a bowl in front of her anyway.

After a long time, the woman picked up the spoon.

When the merchant had finished his second bowl, he put the spoon down and sat back and looked round the table, and the table went quiet without anyone asking it to.

"The roads are closed," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"Not shut. Not barred. Nobody's put a gate across them." He turned his hat round in his hands, on the table. "Closed. Nobody uses them. The river towns used to trade with the hill farms and the hill farms with the towns past the ridge and the towns past the ridge with the coast. Every road. Every week. Carts like mine. Hundreds." He shook his head. "Now nobody goes. The roads have things on them. Things that weren't there before the voice. And the people who used to drive the carts are sitting at home, frightened, eating what they've got and wondering what happens when it runs out."

"And you still come," said Fern.

"I still come." He smiled a little. "I'm stubborn. And I've got a mule who doesn't know any better." He looked at his hat. "But I can't keep coming for this. Half a cart of salt every two weeks, for one settlement. Two guards' wages. The mule." He paused. "I worked it out on the way up. I've got about four more runs in me before I can't pay the guards. And then I'll have to stop. And then you won't have salt."

The long table was very quiet.

"So I'd like to make you an offer," said the merchant.



He laid it out the way a merchant lays out cloth. Carefully. All of it, so you could see the whole bolt before you decided.

"The towns are short of things," he said. "Everything. Because nobody's carrying anything anymore. Salt, oil, iron, cloth. All of it. But some things they're shorter of than others." He held up one finger. "Medicine. There's nobody making it. The apothecaries who used to come up from the coast don't come. I sell your apothecary's hand cream in the first river town for four times what I pay her, and it's gone in a morning, and they call it *the good cream* and they don't ask where it's from."

Maomao, at her end of the bench, raised one eyebrow very slightly.

"Honey," said the merchant. A second finger. "The bees in the valley died two winters ago, nobody knows why. Your barn witch has hives." He nodded at Daphne, at the far end, who looked back at him with her mild hungry eyes. "Glass." A third finger. He looked at Senku, and then at Wakana. "There were two glassblowers in the river towns. One died in the winter. The other one walked south when the road was still open and didn't come back. There's not a whole lamp chimney left in the first town. People are burning oil in open dishes and setting their houses on fire." He put his hand down. "You make glass. I've seen your windows."

"We make glass," said Wakana slowly. "Badly. For us."

"Badly is better than none," said the merchant. "Believe me. Badly is a great deal better than none."

"And you want us to make it," said Fern. "For you to sell."

"I want you to make it," said the merchant, "and the cream, and the honey, whatever you can spare. And I carry it. Down the road, to the towns. Under my name." He spread his hands. "Not *monster town* cream. My cream. The merchant's. They'll buy from me. They've bought from me for twenty years. They won't buy from you." He said it plainly, without apology. "I'm sorry. But they won't. Not now. Not after the light."

Nobody argued with that.

"And the road?" said Mikasa.

The merchant looked at her.

"That's the other half," he said. "I can't carry that much with two guards. Not through the low woods. Not with what's in there." He turned his hat round once more. "So I'd hire more. Warriors. From the towns. There are plenty of people who need work, now. Soldiers the lords stopped paying. Farmers who lost their flocks to something nobody saw. Men and women who can hold a spear and are sitting at home doing nothing, getting frightened." He nodded at the grey-haired woman, who did not look up from her soup. "Like her. She held a gate for a lord for twelve years. He stopped paying her in the autumn. Now she holds mine." He looked back at the long table. "I hire them. They guard the road. They get a share."

"A share of what?" said Fern.

"Of the profit," said the merchant. "A third each. You. Me. Them." He paused. "And through the low woods—through those woods, specifically—" he looked at Mikasa again, and at Eren, and at Himmel at the far end "—I'd want some of yours. Walking with us. Because my warriors can hold a spear, but they've never cut the face off a thing made of rumours."

The long table was silent for a long time.



Eren spoke first.

"If they find out," he said. "In the towns. Where the cream comes from. The glass." He did not look at the merchant. He was looking at his own hands. "If they find out you're carrying for monster town. What do they do to you?"

The merchant was quiet for a moment.

"Burn my cart," he said. "Probably. And maybe me in it."

"Then why—"

"Because they won't find out," said the merchant.

"They'll find out," said Anko, from the end of the table, with her notebook. Not unkindly. "Eventually. Someone always does."

The merchant looked at her. Then he smiled. It was the old cheerful smile, a little thinner.

"Then I'll have made a great deal of money first," he said. "And I'll retire somewhere warm."

Somebody laughed. Gojo, probably. Shoko kicked him under the table.

Fern had the ledger open. She had been writing the whole time. Numbers. Columns. She looked up.

"Not a third each," said Fern.

The merchant raised his eyebrows.

"We make the goods," said Fern. "We walk the woods. That's two things. You carry and sell. That's one." She turned the ledger round so he could see the column. "Four parts to the settlement. Three to you. Three to the warriors. And the warriors' three come out of the profit before anyone else is paid, so if a run goes badly and there's no profit, they still get something."

The merchant looked at the column for a long time.

"You'd pay my guards first," he said slowly. "Before yourselves."

"They're the ones who'll die," said Fern, "if it goes wrong."

The merchant did not say anything for a moment.

At the end of the bench, the grey-haired woman had stopped eating. She was looking at Fern. At the small serious girl with the ledger, who had just said, in front of a whole table, that the people holding the spears should be paid before anyone else. She looked at Fern for a long time with an expression that was very hard to read.

Then she went back to her soup. But her hand had come off her crossbow.



Mau had not said anything.

He sat at the end of the bench beside Frieren with his hands on the table, and listened to all of it. The roads. The towns. *The good cream.* The lamp chimneys. Four parts and three and three. He did not speak. It was not his to speak. The house was deciding, the way the house decided things. Round the long table. Everyone.

But when the talking had gone quiet, and Fern had closed the ledger, and everyone was looking round at everyone else to see where it had landed, Rimuru, on the table in his slime shape, turned toward Mau. Just slightly. The way he did when it was something that the two of them, together, would have to put their names to. The two faces. The ones the outside world talked to.

Mau looked at the merchant.

"Why?" he said.

The merchant looked back at him.

"You could stop," said Mau. "You said it. Four more runs and you can't pay the guards. You could stop. Go home. Stay in a river town where nobody's going to burn your cart." He did not look away. "Why come up here with this? Why us?"

The merchant was quiet for a long time.

He turned his hat round in his hands. Once. Twice. He looked down the long table. At Momo with her ladle. At the fort behind its curtain, where twenty small faces were watching him through the gap. At Seiko on her high seat. At the witches. At Rimuru, a slime in a bowl. At the grey-haired woman, eating soup at a table full of things she had been told her whole life to be afraid of.

"I've eaten Momo's soup," said the merchant.

Momo, at the kitchen door, went pink.

"That's the first thing," said the merchant. "And you pay on time. And you never water anything." He almost smiled. "But that's not why." He put the hat down on the table. "I've been coming up this road for a year and a half. Every two weeks. I've got a route. Eleven towns, and a lot of farms, and the coast. And this is the only place on it, now, where nobody's frightened of the wrong things."

Mau did not say anything.

"Everywhere else," said the merchant, "they're frightened of you. Of a light they saw from a hill. Of a story. Of a name somebody gave you in a tavern." He looked round the table. "And up here, you're frightened of the right things. The things in the woods. The things on the ridge. You sit at a table and you work out what's real and what isn't, and then you go out and cut its face off." He shook his head. "I've been a merchant for twenty years. I know what things are worth. That's worth more than anything I've got on that cart."

The long table was very quiet.

"Not every bridge has to burn," said the merchant. "That's all. Somebody has to keep walking across one." He picked his hat back up. "It might as well be me and my mule."



They shook on it at the end of the long table. The merchant on one side. Mau and Rimuru on the other, because that was the rule, from the council: the outside world spoke to two faces, and never to one. Rimuru grew a hand for it, out of the slime, a small pale human hand, and the merchant looked at it for a moment and then shook it, very politely, without flinching.

Then he shook Mau's.

He held it a moment longer than he needed to. The way he had held Eren's at the ford, in the spring.

"I never told you my name," said the merchant.

Mau blinked.

"A year and a half," said the merchant. "Every two weeks. Nobody ever asked. And I never said." He looked a little embarrassed. "You were all *the merchant* to me too, at first. Then you weren't. And I never said."

"What is it?" said Mau.

"Hollis," said the merchant.

At the head of the table, Fern opened the ledger again. She turned to the page with the column of numbers, four and three and three. And at the top of it, above the numbers, where it had said *THE MERCHANT*, she crossed that out, very neatly, with one line.

And wrote: *Hollis.*

The grey-haired woman at the end of the bench put down her spoon. She looked at Fern writing. At the name going into the book.

"Brenna," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"My name," said the woman. She did not look at any of them. She looked at the ledger. "If you're writing them down." She paused. "I held a gate for twelve years. Nobody ever wrote mine down."

Fern looked at her for a moment. Then she bent over the ledger again, and wrote, under the column for the warriors' three parts, in small upright letters:

*Brenna. Gate, twelve years.*

At the end of the bench, the broad quiet man with the scar through his eyebrow, who had eaten soup at this table with his back to the wall every two weeks since the winter and never once said a word, cleared his throat.

"Dunmore," he said.

Fern wrote that down too.
