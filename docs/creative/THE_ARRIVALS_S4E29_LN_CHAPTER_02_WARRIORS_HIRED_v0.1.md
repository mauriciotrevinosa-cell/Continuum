*When: Day 187, a week after Hollis shook on it. Wakana and Senku have spent the week making lamp chimneys, badly, and have eleven whole ones out of forty tries. Maomao and Kusuri have made two hundred pots of cream and are not speaking to each other about the recipe. Daphne has given up thirty jars of honey with visible pain. Gojo has been on the gate list once more and was surprised by nothing.*

Hollis came back on the hundred-and-eighty-seventh day with six people the house had never seen, and they stopped at the line.

The line was a strip of white lime across the bridge road, a hand's width wide, where Anko and the Searchers had laid it in the winter. It marked where the Goddess's protection began. Everyone in the settlement knew it was there. Most of them stepped over it without thinking, a dozen times a day.

The six people stopped in front of it as if it were a wall.

Mikasa saw them from the north end and went down. Then Anko. Then, because the fort had seen Mikasa go and the fort saw everything, half the house, in a straggling crowd down the road, trying to look as if they were not coming to stare.

They stopped too, on their side of the line.

The two groups looked at each other across a strip of white lime on a muddy road, in the spring sun.

Hollis stood in the middle of the road beside his cart, between them, on the line, with one foot on each side of it. He looked very tired. He had clearly been talking for the whole of the walk up from the ford, and had clearly not convinced anybody of anything.

"They're good people," he said, to the house. Then he turned round. "They're good people," he said, to the six. "I promise. I've eaten their soup."

Nobody moved.

Brenna was there, the grey-haired woman, at the front of the six, with her crossbow. Dunmore, with his scar, behind her. And four more. A man of about fifty with a soldier's coat that had had its colours unpicked. Two brothers, young, with spears, who looked as if they had been farmers until very recently and might still be farmers in their hearts. And a girl of perhaps seventeen, small and thin, with a bow taller than she was, who stood at the very back and kept her eyes on the ground.

All six of them were looking across the line at the house. At the crowd on the other side.

Mau looked at the crowd on his side, and tried to see it the way they did.

Mikasa, with three blades. Eren. Anko, with her hand in her coat. Himmel with his blue hair, who had come down because he always came down. Milim, floating a hand's breadth above the mud, because she had forgotten she was not supposed to do that in front of strangers. Rimuru, in human shape, but with one arm that had not quite finished being an arm and was still faintly blue at the edges. Gojo, very tall, in a blindfold. Shoko, with an unlit cigarette between two fingers. Yuka, with a very large white dog. The fort, behind them, peering round Mikasa's legs, twenty small faces in hoods. Turbo Granny on Okarun's shoulder, glaring.

And him. Mau. Whatever they had heard about him.

He understood, then, why they had stopped.

"Oh," said Mau quietly.

Beside him, Frieren had seen it too. "Yes," she said.

On the far side of the line, the younger of the two farmer-brothers had gone white. He was staring past Mikasa, past Gojo, past all of them, at one person. At Mau.

"That's him," the boy said. Not loudly. To his brother. But the road was quiet, and everyone heard. "That's the one. With the shadow. The one who made the sun."

His brother put a hand on his arm.

Nobody on either side said anything.

Mau stood very still. He felt something lift its head, behind the new walls. Not the wheel. Not the shadow. Something older. The part of him that had stood in the plaza with every face turned toward him, and in the path to the washing court with every hand going to a blade. It wanted him to step back. Out of sight. Behind the others. So they would not have to look at him.

He did not step back.

He did not step forward either. He just stood there, on his side of the line, in the sun, with his hands in his pockets, and let them look.

It was Momo who moved.



She came down the road from the house with a pot.

The big one. The soup pot. Carried in both arms against her apron, still steaming, with a ladle sticking out of it and a stack of bowls balanced on the lid. She came down through the crowd on the house's side, and the crowd parted for her without anyone telling it to. She walked straight past Mau, and Mikasa, and Gojo, to the white line across the road.

And stepped over it.

Everyone on both sides went silent.

Momo put the pot down in the mud on the far side of the line. In front of the six. She took the bowls off the lid and set them out, one by one, on the lid itself, upside down, as a table. She filled one. She held it out.

To the boy who had said *that's him.*

The boy stared at her.

"It's leek," said Momo. "And potato. And a bit of the good bacon, because Seiko said I could." She did not lower the bowl. "You walked from the ford. You've had nothing since the ford. I can tell." She held it out a little further. "Eat it while it's hot. It's better hot."

The boy looked at the bowl. At Momo. At the line, behind her, that she had stepped over as if it were nothing.

"You're from in there," he said.

"I live in there," said Momo. "I'm from somewhere else." She shrugged. "So are you, now. You're from the ford." She pushed the bowl at him. "Eat."

He took it. He did not seem to know he had taken it. He stood in the mud on his side of the line holding a bowl of leek and potato soup with a bit of the good bacon in it, and looked down at it.

Then, because he was a farm boy who had walked from the ford and had had nothing since, and it smelled the way soup smells, he ate it.

His face changed.

Momo was already filling the next bowl. She held it out to his brother. Then to the man with the unpicked coat. Then to Dunmore, who took it without a word, the way he took it every two weeks, and nodded to her. Then to Brenna, who looked at the bowl, and at Momo, for a long moment, and then took it.

The thin girl with the bow at the back did not come forward.

Momo looked at her over the pot.

The girl was looking at the ground. Her hands were white on her bow. She was shaking, very slightly. Not from cold.

Momo picked up the last bowl, and filled it, and did not hold it out. She just put it down on the lid of the pot, at the edge nearest the girl, and stepped back. Back over the line. To her own side. And waited.

The girl looked at the bowl.

Nobody moved.

On the house's side of the line, very slowly, Ori came forward out of the crowd. She came past Mikasa's legs and the fort's hoods, and stood at the line, beside Momo. She did not cross it. She just stood there, a girl of about the same age as the girl with the bow, in a plain dress, with her hands at her sides.

"It's good soup," said Ori, across the line. "She makes it every day. She makes it for everyone." She paused. "Even the ones who are frightened. Especially those."

The girl with the bow looked up.

She looked at Ori for a long time. At a girl her own age, who did not look like a monster. Who looked, if anything, as if she might have been frightened once herself, a long time ago, and had got over it.

Then she came forward. Three steps. To the pot. And picked up the bowl.

She did not eat it there. She went back to her place at the back with it, and stood there, holding it in both hands, looking down at it. But after a while, when nobody was looking at her, she ate it. All of it.



It was the boy who crossed first.

The farmer. The younger brother. The one who had said *that's him*. He finished his second bowl, because Momo gave him a second bowl without asking. And then he stood in the mud on his side of the line, with the empty bowl in his hands, looking at the white strip at his feet for a long time.

Then he stepped over it.

Just one step. He stopped on the house's side, a pace in. He looked down at his own boots, as if checking that they had not caught fire.

"Nothing happened," he said.

"Nothing ever does," said Anko. "It's lime."

The boy looked up. He looked round. At the crowd. At the fort, staring at him from behind Mikasa's legs. At Milim, floating. At the slime-blue edges of Rimuru's arm. And then, last, slowly, at the one person he had been not-looking at since he arrived.

Mau.

Mau looked back at him. He did not say anything.

The boy's face did several things at once. Fear. Shame. Curiosity. Something else, underneath, that might have been the beginning of something.

"Is it true?" said the boy. His voice cracked. "The sun. The light. Over the field. Was it you?"

The road went very quiet.

Mau thought about it.

He could say no. He could say it was something else, an accident, a spell, a mistake. He could say it was not really him. Everyone on his side of the line would let him. Nobody would contradict him.

"Yes," said Mau.

The boy went white again.

"It was me," said Mau. "Something in me that I didn't ask for. It came out all at once, on a bad morning. It nearly killed me. It didn't hurt anyone else." He did not look away from the boy. "My family stood at a fence and watched it. A doctor came from another world to put me back together. And a man in a blindfold taught me how to keep it in." He nodded, very slightly, toward Gojo. "It's in now. It stays in. I'm careful every day."

The boy stared at him.

"They said you'd burn the valley," said the boy.

"I know," said Mau. "I heard."

"They said the grass died where it fell. And it's still dead."

"It is," said Mau. "Some of it. It's coming back. Slowly. It rained last week and the dead earth went dark for the first time." He paused. "I go and look at it most mornings. To see."

The boy did not say anything for a long time.

Then he looked down at the empty soup bowl in his hands. At the line behind him. At Momo, filling bowls on the other side of it.

"My sister's got sheep," he said. "Had. Something took four of them in the winter. Nobody saw what." He did not look up. "Everyone said it was the monster town. Something you let out." He swallowed. "I said it too."

Mau did not say anything.

"Was it?" said the boy.

"I don't know," said Mau honestly. "It wasn't us. But something's out there, taking sheep. And frightening people. And walking the woods saying what the towns say." He paused. "That's why you're here, I think. To help us walk through it."

The boy looked at him for a long moment.

"Cobb," he said, at last.

Mau blinked.

"My name," said the boy. "Cobb." He nodded back across the line at his brother, who was watching them both with his mouth open. "He's Tam."

"Mau," said Mau.

"I know," said Cobb. "Everyone knows." He looked down at the bowl again, and then held it out, awkwardly, toward Momo across the line. "Can I have another one? Is that allowed?"

"It's always allowed," said Momo, and filled it.



They loaded the cart in the afternoon.

The six came across the line one at a time, after Cobb. Tam next, because he was not going to let his little brother be braver than him. Then the man in the unpicked coat, who turned out to be called Aldric and to have been a sergeant, and who looked at Mikasa's blades with great professional interest and at Gojo's blindfold with great professional alarm. Then Dunmore, who had been coming in for months and had never thought of himself as having been outside. Then Brenna.

The girl with the bow came last. She came across the line very carefully, as if it might move, and went straight to Ori, and stood beside her. She did not say anything. She did not tell anyone her name. But she stood beside Ori for the rest of the afternoon, and Ori let her.

The goods came down from the house in crates and baskets, on backs and barrows. Two hundred pots of the good cream, packed in straw, that Maomao counted twice and Kusuri counted three times and that they had argued over all week. Thirty jars of honey, that Daphne handed over one at a time, saying goodbye to each of them under her breath. And eleven lamp chimneys. Eleven whole, clear, slightly lopsided glass chimneys, wrapped in cloth, out of forty tries. Wakana carried them down himself in a basket against his chest, as if they were eggs, and Senku walked beside him saying *ten billion percent* in a voice that meant he was terrified one would break.

Mau carried one end of a crate.

Anko had approved it. She had come down to the cart, looked at the crate of honey jars, looked at him, looked at her notebook, and said "That one. One end. Not the glass." And written it down. *Day 187. M. carried one end of honey. (A.U. approved.)*

So he carried one end of honey. Gojo carried the other, with one hand, because the crate weighed nothing to Gojo, and talked the whole way down the road about how he had once carried a whole building, which nobody believed.

At the cart, Cobb took the crate off them, and swung it up, and stowed it.

"Thanks," said Mau.

Cobb looked at him. Then at Gojo, who was very tall and blindfolded and grinning. Then back at Mau.

"You really are just a guy," said Cobb. Wonderingly. "Aren't you. You carry honey."

"One end," said Mau. "I'm only allowed one end."

Cobb stared at him. Then, for the first time since he had come up the road, he laughed. It burst out of him, surprised and young, and he put his hand over his mouth as if he had not meant to.

On the cart, Brenna was checking the ropes. She heard the laugh. She stopped, and looked down at the farm boy laughing with the one with the shadow, at the bottom of a muddy road, beside a crate of honey. She looked for a long moment.

Then she went back to the ropes. But she was smiling, very slightly, and she did not seem to know it.



That night the six slept in Arrival House.

It had been empty for a long time. The register girl made up six beds, and lit the stove, and wrote six names in the register for the first time since the spring. Five names. The girl with the bow still had not said hers. The register girl wrote *a friend of Ori's* instead, and underlined it.

Jinshi brought the key, in the morning, as he always did. He looked at the register for a long time. At the five names and *a friend of Ori's*.

"Guests," said Jinshi, to nobody, very quietly. "Six. Not a deer."

He sounded, the register girl said later, as if he might cry.

At the long table that night, Fern opened the ledger to the page with *Hollis* at the top and the column of numbers under it. Under the column for the warriors' three parts, she had written: *Brenna. Gate, twelve years.* And *Dunmore.* And now, under those, in her small upright hand, she added:

*Aldric. Sergeant.*

*Tam.*

*Cobb. (Asked for another bowl.)*

And then, after a moment:

*A friend of Ori's.*

She looked at it for a long time. Then she left a space after it, the width of a name.

For when she said it.
