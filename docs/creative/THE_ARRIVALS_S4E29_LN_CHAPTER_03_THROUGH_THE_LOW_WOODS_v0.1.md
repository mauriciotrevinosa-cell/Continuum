*When: Day 188, dawn to midday. The six slept in Arrival House; Jinshi has been looking at the register all morning. Mau is at the red stake with Diablo, on the rota, watching the cart go. The friend of Ori's has still not said her name.*

They left at first light, the way Mikasa wanted.

The cart went first, with Hollis at the mule's head and the goods roped down under a canvas: two hundred pots of the good cream, thirty jars of honey, eleven lamp chimneys wrapped in cloth like eggs. The six walked round it. Brenna at the front with her crossbow. Aldric at the back. Dunmore on the left. The brothers on the right, Tam and Cobb, with their spears held the way farmers hold pitchforks. And the girl with the bow, who walked beside the cart's tailboard and looked back, every few steps, at the settlement going small behind her.

And with them, from the house, four.

Mikasa. Eren. Himmel, with his hair brushed. And Aira, with the old blade with the nick in the left at her hip, in a big coat, walking beside Cobb. Fern had not put Aira on the list. Aira had put herself on it, in the kitchen, the night before, by standing in front of Fern until Fern wrote her name.

"The woods," Aira had said. "If something comes out of the woods at them, someone has to stand between it and the boy. The farm boy. Cobb." She had folded her arms. "He doesn't know how. I do."

Fern had looked at her for a long time. Then she wrote *Aira (between)*.

And Anko. Anko was not on the list either. Anko was going further than the list.

She walked at the back with Aldric, in a plain brown coat that was not her own, with her hair under a cap and her notebook in an inside pocket where nobody would see it. She had asked Hollis the night before, privately, at the end of the long table, if he needed a fourth guard for the town. Someone who would walk into the market with his cart and stand there and watch.

"Watch what?" Hollis had said.

"Whoever's talking," said Anko.

Hollis had looked at her for a long moment. Then he had said, "Wear something brown. Nobody looks at brown."

At the red stake, as the cart went past, Mau stood with his hands in his pockets and Diablo a pace behind him and watched them go. Cobb, passing, looked at him. Lifted one hand, uncertainly, the way the boy on the refuge cart had lifted his to Aira. Mau lifted his back.

Then the cart was over the rise, and down into the river valley, and gone.



The low woods were quiet.

They came to the edge of them in the middle of the morning, where the south road went in between the bare trunks, with the new green just thickening at the tips of the branches. There were no birds. There was no wind. The road went in straight, muddy, in bars of pale sun.

Hollis stopped the mule at the edge of the trees.

The six had stopped too. All of them. Without being told. They stood round the cart and looked at the woods, and none of them moved forward. Cobb's hands were white on his spear. Tam had put one hand on his brother's shoulder without seeming to know he had done it. The girl with the bow had come round from the tailboard and was standing very close to the mule.

"They say," said Aldric quietly, to nobody, "it walks in there. The thing. The one the towns made up." He paused. "The one with all the faces."

"It's not made up," said Eren.

Aldric looked at him.

"We've killed it twice," said Eren. "It keeps coming back. Bigger." He did not look away from the trees. "It's made of what your towns say. When they say it enough, it walks."

The six looked at him. Nobody said anything.

"So don't say anything," said Mikasa. She had drawn the grey blades. Both of them. She went past the mule, to the front, to the edge of the trees. "In there. Don't talk about us. Don't talk about the light. Don't talk about monster town." She did not look back. "Just walk."

They walked.



Halfway through, they heard it.

Not close. Far off. Deep in the trees, on the left, the way it had been the first time. A mutter. Many voices, overlapping, low, the way a market sounds from three streets away. Too far to make out words. It went on and on under the silence.

Cobb's breath went out of him in a sound.

Aira was beside him at once. She did not touch him. She just walked beside him, between him and the left-hand trees, close, with her hand on the hilt of the old blade.

"Keep walking," said Aira. Very low. "It's far."

"It's *there*," said Cobb.

"It's far," said Aira again. "And I'm here. And it's on the other side of me." She did not look at him. "Keep walking."

He kept walking.

The muttering did not come closer. That was the strange thing. It stayed where it was, deep in the trees, on the left. But it moved. When they moved, it moved. A hundred paces off, through the trunks, going the same way they went, at the same pace. Keeping level with them. Never coming nearer. Never falling behind.

Mikasa had seen it. She did not stop walking. She looked across at Himmel, on the other side of the road.

"It's following," she said, under her breath.

"Yes," said Himmel.

"Why isn't it coming out?"

Himmel walked for a few steps without answering. He had his hand on his sword. He was looking at the left-hand trees, at the place the mutter was coming from, with the face underneath the hero's face.

"It's learning," he said quietly.

Mikasa looked at him.

"Last time we cut the faces that looked," said Himmel. "The ones that meant it." He did not take his eyes off the trees. "If I were a thing made of what frightened people say, and three people walked into my woods and cut out every part of me that meant it, I'd—" He paused. "I'd stay back. And grow. And listen. And wait until I had more of them." He looked at the cart, at the six round it, at Cobb with his white hands on his spear. "And I'd be very interested in who was walking with them."

Mikasa did not say anything.

They came out of the trees at the river, at the ford, in the full sun, all of them, with the cart and the goods and the six and nobody touched. And behind them, in the woods, the mutter stopped. All at once. As if a door had closed.

Cobb sat down on the riverbank and put his head between his knees.

Aira sat down beside him. Not close. Close enough.

"See," said Aira. "It was far."

"You don't know that," said Cobb, through his knees.

"No," said Aira. "But I'd have been in the way if it wasn't." She looked at the river. "That's the job."

Cobb lifted his head and looked at her. At the big fierce girl in the big coat, with one old blade at her hip, who had walked a mile of haunted woods between him and the trees without once looking at him.

"What's your name?" he said. "I didn't ask. Yesterday."

"Aira."

"You're not afraid of anything, are you."

Aira laughed. Short and surprised.

"I'm afraid of everything," she said. "Ghosts. Things in the dark. Things with too many arms. All of it." She stood up and held her hand down to him. "You just stand in front of it anyway. And after a while being afraid gets boring, and you stop noticing."

Cobb looked at her hand. Then he took it, and she pulled him up.



The house stopped at the ford. That was the plan.

Mikasa and Himmel and Aira would wait on the near bank, at the edge of the trees, with the cart's empty crates and a fire. The six would take the cart across the ford and up the far bank and along the river road, an hour, to the first of the river towns. And Hollis would sell. And they would come back by the middle of the afternoon, and the house would walk them home through the woods again, before dark.

Eren went across with them.

He was not supposed to. He had told Fern he would stay at the ford. But at the water's edge, when the cart went in, he looked at Anko in her brown coat and her cap, going across at the back with Aldric. And then he looked at Mikasa.

"Someone should be with her," said Eren. "In the town. If something—" He stopped. "Anko can watch. She's better at it than anyone. But someone should be behind her while she watches."

Mikasa looked at him for a long moment.

"You'll be careful," she said. It was not a question.

"I'll be brown," said Eren. He took his cap out of his pocket and pulled it down over his hair. "Nobody looks at brown."

Mikasa did not smile. But something moved at the corner of her mouth.

"Say it again tomorrow," she said.

"I will," said Eren. "Tomorrow. At the stake." He waded into the ford after the cart. "I'll be back before dark."

She watched him all the way across. All the way up the far bank. All the way along the river road, until the cart and the six and the two brown coats at the back were small, and then gone round a bend in the river.

Himmel was watching her.

"You don't like it," he said.

"No," said Mikasa.

"You let him go anyway."

"Yes." She sat down on the bank, with her grey blades across her knees, facing the far shore. "He's not mine to keep in." She was quiet for a moment. "He never was. That's why it's worth it."

Himmel sat down beside her. He did not say anything. He looked at the river, and at the bend where the road went, and at the woman beside him, who had waited since she was nine and was waiting still, on a riverbank, with her blades on her knees.

"Frieren used to sit like that," he said, after a while. "When I went off somewhere. On the journey." He smiled a little. "I never noticed. Not until much later."

Mikasa looked at him.

"Eisen told me," said Himmel. "Years afterward. When she'd gone. He said, *she always sat facing the way you went. Every time.*" He looked at the river. "I'd thought she wasn't paying attention."



The first of the river towns was called Ashby, and it had been on the river for three hundred years, and it had a market every fifth day in the square in front of the reeve's hall, and it was frightened.

Anko saw that before they were through the gate.

It was in everything. In the way the gatekeeper looked at the cart, and at the six round it, and counted them twice. In the shutters, half of them closed in the middle of the day. In the way people in the street walked close to the walls, and did not stop to talk, and looked up at the hills over the river every so often without seeming to know they were doing it. In the market itself, which was half empty. Stalls with nothing on them. A baker with four loaves. A man selling turnips, and nothing else, and watching the crowd as if it might turn on him.

Hollis drew the cart into his usual place, at the corner by the well, and threw back the canvas.

They came at once.

Anko had seen markets before. In her own world, in a city full of night, she had stood in a great many crowds watching for the one face that did not belong. She had never seen a crowd do what this one did. They saw the cart, and the canvas coming off, and they came. Not running. Walking, fast, the way people walk when they are trying not to run in front of their neighbours. A woman with a baby. An old man on a stick. Two boys. A girl in an apron. A man in a good coat with a purse.

"Is that—" said the woman with the baby. "Hollis. Is that the good cream?"

"Two hundred pots," said Hollis.

The woman made a sound.

It went in an hour. Not the whole cart. The cream. Two hundred pots, at four times what he had paid Maomao for them, and people pushing, politely, at the edges, and Hollis counting coins into a bag at a speed Anko had not known a person could count. An old man bought six and wept, because his wife's hands had cracked to bleeding over the winter and there had been nothing, nothing at all, for months. A girl bought one with every coin she had and walked away holding it against her chest.

"Where does it come from?" the man in the good coat asked, counting out his money. "Nobody's making anything. Where'd you get it?"

"South," said Hollis, without missing a beat. "Old woman on the coast road. Makes it in her kitchen. Won't tell me the recipe."

"Lucky," said the man.

"Very," said Hollis, and took his money.

The honey went next. Slower. A jar at a time, to people who looked at it as if it were gold. A woman with a coughing child bought two and held one up to the light and said *I'd forgotten what it looked like*.

And then the lamp chimneys.

Hollis did not put them out. He kept them in their basket, wrapped. And when a woman came to the cart and asked, very quietly, if he had anything for lamps, he looked at her for a moment and then unwrapped one. Eleven whole, clear, slightly lopsided glass chimneys, out of forty tries. He held it up.

The woman put her hand over her mouth.

"Our house nearly burned," she said. "In the winter. My husband's been burning oil in a dish since the chimney cracked. He knocked it over in the night. The curtains." She did not take her eyes off the glass. "Where did you—there's nobody making glass. Nobody. Jory died and Wilm went south."

"South," said Hollis. "Old man on the coast road."

"The same one as the cream?"

"His brother," said Hollis.

The woman bought it. She held it the way Wakana had held the basket coming down the road from the house. Against her chest. Like an egg.

Anko stood at the back of the cart in her brown coat and her cap, with her hands in her pockets, and watched all of it.

Eren stood behind her. He had not said a word since the gate. He was watching too. The woman with the chimney, walking away down the half-empty street, holding it against her. The old man with his six pots. The girl with the one.

"They don't know," said Eren, under his breath. Very quietly. "Any of them. They don't know it's from us."

"No," said Anko.

"Wakana made that chimney. And Senku. Forty tries. That woman's house won't burn down now because of Wakana and Senku." His voice was doing something strange. "And she thinks it's an old man on the coast road."

"Yes," said Anko.

"That's—" He stopped.

Anko did not look round at him. She was watching the street. The whole street. The way she always watched.

"That's how it is," she said. "For now." She paused. "It's better than nothing. It's a great deal better than nothing."

Eren did not say anything.

But across the square, on the steps of the reeve's hall, someone had started to speak. And the crowd round Hollis's cart was turning, the way crowds turn, toward the sound.

Anko's hand went into her coat. Not for her weapon. For her notebook.

"Eren," she said quietly. "Stay behind me."
