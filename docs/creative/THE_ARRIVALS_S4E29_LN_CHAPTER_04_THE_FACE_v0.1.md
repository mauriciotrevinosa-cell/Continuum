## Chapter 12 — The Face

*When: Day 188, the afternoon, the same market. Mikasa, Himmel and Aira are at the ford with a fire and the empty crates. Back at the house, Wakana has been sitting on the woodpile since noon, looking south, and will not say why.*

He was standing on the steps of the reeve's hall.

Not at the top. Halfway up. Where a man stands when he wants to be seen above a crowd, but not so far above it that the crowd stops thinking of him as one of them. A man of perhaps forty-five, in a clerk's coat, good wool, dark, plain, with ink on the cuffs. Thinning hair, combed carefully. A pleasant, ordinary, tired face. The kind of face you would pass in a street and not remember.

He had a voice that carried.

"Neighbours," he said.

The square turned toward him. Not all at once. The way a field of barley turns in the wind, a little at a time, from one side to the other. The people round Hollis's cart turned. The baker with four loaves turned. The man with the turnips. The woman with the coughing child, with a jar of honey in her hand.

Anko did not turn. She was already facing that way. She had been facing that way since the first word, with her hands in her pockets and her cap pulled down, standing at the back of the cart where nobody looked at brown.

"You've heard the stories," said the man on the steps.

He did not shout. He spoke as if he were talking to each of them, one at a time, across a kitchen table. Reasonably. Sadly. A man telling his neighbours something he wished he did not have to tell them.

"The light," he said. "Over their field. In the spring. The shepherds saw it from the hills. A second sun, they said. Coming up out of the ground at dawn, in the monster town, over their south field." He paused. "And the grass died where it fell. You've heard that. It's still dead. My cousin's boy walked up as far as the ridge to look. Black. Like a burn."

The square was very quiet.

Behind Anko, Eren had gone completely still.

"And the sheep," said the man. "Hannet's sheep, on the east side. Three, in the winter. And the Corlew boy, who saw the thing standing at the end of his field three nights running, made of shadows." He shook his head, slowly. "And now the woods. The low woods, by the ford. Where the thing walks. The thing with all the faces, that talks. You've heard it. Some of you have heard it yourselves, going through." He looked out across the square. "It says what we're all thinking. Doesn't it. It says what we're too polite to say."

A murmur went across the crowd. Low. Not agreement, exactly. Something worse than agreement. Recognition.

Anko had her notebook out.

She held it inside her coat, against her chest, and wrote without looking down, the way she had written in a hundred dark streets in another city. Small. Fast. She did not write what he was saying. She had heard what he was saying before. She had heard it in the woods, from a hundred small grey mouths.

She wrote what he was doing.

*Reads.*

He was. Not openly. He had no paper in his hands. But his left hand was in his coat pocket, and every few sentences his eyes dropped, very slightly, very quickly, to the level of that pocket. And came back up. At the end of a thought. At the start of the next one. The way a man's eyes drop who has a page in his pocket folded so that the next lines show, and is reading them, and has practised not looking as if he is.

*Soft hands.*

His right hand, the one he gestured with, was a clerk's hand. Ink on the fingers. Soft palms. No calluses. A man who had held pens for thirty years and nothing heavier.

And the words were not a clerk's words. Anko listened to the rhythm of them. The pauses. The places where his voice dropped, and lifted, and stopped for the crowd to murmur. The words had been written by someone who knew exactly where a crowd would murmur. Someone who had stood in front of crowds before, many times, and made them do things. And then the words had been handed to a pleasant, tired clerk with soft hands to read.

*Doesn't write his own.*

"I'm not a lord," said the man on the steps. "I'm not a soldier. I'm the reeve's clerk. I keep the rolls. I count the taxes. I don't know anything about magic, or monsters, or what they've got up there in their hills." He spread his soft hands. "But I can count. And I've counted. Since they came over that bridge, from nowhere, from nothing. Since their Goddess spoke, and their Trial, and their coins. Since the voice told us the world was changing." He paused. "Every bad thing on our roads has come since then. Every one."

The murmur was louder.

"And I'll tell you something else I've counted," said the clerk.

He looked out over the square. And his eyes went, quite slowly, quite naturally, to the corner by the well. To Hollis's cart. To the empty straw where two hundred pots of the good cream had been an hour ago, and the basket where the lamp chimneys had been.

"Things are coming down the road again," he said. "After months of nothing. Cream. Honey. Glass, even. Glass, when there's nobody left in the valley who can blow it." He did not raise his voice. "And nobody can tell you where it comes from."

The square went very quiet.

Hollis, at the mule's head, did not move. His face stayed exactly as cheerful as it always was. His hand on the halter had gone white.

"I'm not saying anything," said the clerk. "I'm only saying be careful. Don't buy what you can't trace." He looked down at the crowd, sadly. "Because whoever carries for them, neighbours, carries their sickness too."

At the edge of the crowd, the woman with the lamp chimney had stopped walking.

She was holding it against her chest, wrapped in its cloth, the way she had held it when she bought it. Like an egg. She was looking at the clerk on the steps. Then, slowly, she turned her head and looked at the cart. At Hollis. At the empty basket.

She looked for a long time.

Then she turned round and walked away down the street, with the chimney still held against her chest. She did not give it back. She did not look back. She just walked away, very fast, holding it.

Eren breathed out.

"Behind me," said Anko, without turning. Very quietly. "Stay behind me."

"He's talking about *Hollis*—"

"I know."

"He's going to get him *killed*—"

"I know." Anko did not take her eyes off the steps. "And if you step out from behind me with that face, and somebody in this square looks at you and sees what you are, he'll get us all killed. Starting with Hollis." Her voice did not change. "Stay behind me. Watch with me. That's what we came for."

Eren stayed behind her.

His fists were clenched so hard at his sides that his arms shook. But he stayed.



There was a man at the back of the crowd.

Anko saw him because she was looking for him. Not him exactly. Anyone. The one face in any crowd that is not listening the way the others are listening. She had been looking for it since the clerk opened his mouth.

He was at the very back. By the corner of the reeve's hall, half in the shadow of the eaves. Tall. Gaunt. A man who had been broad once and was not any more. He wore a long coat, dark, of good heavy cloth, the kind of coat a city gave its guard. And on the breast of it, where a badge should have been, there was a patch of slightly darker cloth, the shape of a shield, where a badge had been cut off.

He was not murmuring. He was not looking at the clerk the way the others were looking at the clerk. He was listening the way you listen to a speech you have heard before, checking that it is being given correctly. And every so often, when the crowd murmured in the right place, he nodded. Very slightly. To himself.

Anko wrote.

*Back of crowd. NE corner, by the eaves. Tall, gaunt. City guard's coat. Badge cut off. Hasn't slept in a long time. Not listening. Checking.*

As she wrote the last word, the man in the coat turned his head.

He did not look at her. He looked at the cart. At Hollis. At the six round it. At Aldric with his unpicked coat, and Brenna with her crossbow, and the brothers, and the girl with the bow. His eyes went over them slowly, one at a time, the way a guard's eyes go over a crowd at a gate.

They reached the two brown coats at the back of the cart.

They stopped on Eren.

Only for a moment. Only the length of a breath. Eren had his cap down and his head lowered and his face turned toward the steps. There was nothing about him, from across a square, that said anything at all.

But the man in the coat looked at him for the length of a breath. As if something about the way he was standing, or the way his fists were clenched, or the way he was not listening, had caught on something.

Then he looked away.

And before the clerk had finished, before the last sad reasonable sentence and the last murmur of the crowd, the man in the coat with the badge cut off turned round and walked away down the alley behind the reeve's hall, without hurrying, and was gone.

Anko wrote: *Left before the end. Alley behind the hall. Didn't need to hear the rest.*

She underlined *didn't need to*.



"Master Pell," said someone, near them. An old woman, to her neighbour, as the crowd began to break up. "The reeve's clerk. Every market day, now. Since the spring." She clicked her tongue. "Used to be such a quiet man. Kept the rolls. Never said boo." She shook her head. "Now look at him. Like a preacher."

"He's right, though," said the neighbour. "Isn't he."

The old woman did not answer. She looked at the empty straw in Hollis's cart for a moment, where the cream had been. Then she went away.

Anko wrote: *PELL. Reeve's clerk. Every market day since the spring. Was quiet. Now isn't.*

And then, under it, after a moment:

*Somebody gave him a page.*



They left Ashby within the hour.

Hollis did not hurry. That was the thing Anko would remember about him afterward. He did not throw the canvas over the cart and run. He packed slowly, cheerfully, talking to the people who came up to ask if he would be back on the next market day. *Of course. Of course I will. Same corner. The old woman on the coast road's making more cream as we speak.* He sold the last two jars of honey. He laughed at a joke the baker made. He tipped his hat to the gatekeeper on the way out.

It was only on the river road, a mile out, round the first bend, out of sight of the town walls, that he stopped the mule, and stood very still in the middle of the road with both hands on the halter, and did not move for a long time.

"Well," said Hollis at last, to the mule. Very quietly. "That's that, then."

Brenna had come up beside him.

"You're going back," she said. "Next market day. Same corner."

"Of course I am," said Hollis.

"He'll say it again. Pell. He'll point at your cart again."

"He will." Hollis took a breath. "And people will buy the cream anyway. Because their hands are bleeding. And the glass. Because their houses are burning." He started the mule walking again. "Fear's strong. Cracked hands are stronger. You'd be surprised."

Brenna walked beside him for a while.

"You're a fool," she said.

"I know," said Hollis.

"I'll come," said Brenna. "Next market day."

Hollis looked at her.

She did not look back. She walked beside the mule with her crossbow over her shoulder and her grey hair in the wind, the way she had walked beside a lord's gate for twelve years, until he stopped paying her.

"Somebody has to stand by the cart," said Brenna. "When the clerk points at it."



They came through the low woods at four in the afternoon, in full light, with the house beside them again. Mikasa at the front. Himmel. Aira between Cobb and the left-hand trees.

The mutter was there.

Closer, this time. Not close enough to see. But closer. And Anko, at the back, in her brown coat, could make out words.

*...don't buy what you can't trace...*

*...whoever carries for them...*

*...carries their sickness too...*

She stopped walking.

Eren, beside her, stopped too. His face had gone white.

"That's—" he said. "That's what he said. Pell. In the square. Two hours ago."

"No," said Anko slowly.

Eren looked at her.

"He's said it before," said Anko. "Every market day. Since the spring. The old woman said." She was looking at the left-hand trees, where the mutter was coming from. "Every market day. To a square full of frightened people. *Don't buy what you can't trace.*" She paused. "And they go home and say it to each other. At their tables. In their beds. And the woods hear it." She took out her notebook. "It's not following us. It's following him."

They walked on. Out of the trees, at the edge of the fields, in the late gold light, all of them. The cart. The six. The house. And behind them, in the woods, the mutter stopped, the way it always stopped. All at once.



Fern counted the money at the long table that night.

She counted it twice. The warriors' three parts first, the way she had said. She put them out on the table in six small piles, in front of six people who sat at the end of the bench nearest the door and looked at the coins as if they had never seen coins before. Brenna. Dunmore. Aldric. Tam. Cobb. The girl with the bow.

Then Hollis's three. Then the settlement's four.

The settlement's four parts were more than Fern had expected. She looked at them for a long time. Then she wrote in the ledger, in her small upright hand: *Salt. Iron. Lamp oil. Medicine (Maomao's list). And forty pounds of sand for Senku*. And underlined the sand.

Wakana was at the far end of the table. She had been on the woodpile all afternoon, looking south. She had come in when the cart came up the road and had not said anything since.

"Hollis," she said now. Very quietly. "The chimneys."

Hollis looked at her down the length of the long table.

"All eleven," he said. "Gone before noon." He paused. "There was a woman. Whose house nearly burned in the winter. Her husband knocked a dish of oil over in the night, because the chimney had cracked and there was no one to make another." He looked at Wakana. "She held it like an egg. All the way down the street."

Wakana did not say anything.

She sat at the end of the long table, with Marin beside her, and her hands on the board in front of her, the hands that had made forty chimneys and broken twenty-nine. She looked at them.

Then she put her face down on her arms, on the table, and cried.

Marin put her hand on Wakana's back. Flat, between her shoulder blades. And left it there. And did not say anything. Across the table, Senku looked very hard at the ceiling and said *ten billion percent* to nobody, in a voice that did not quite work.



Anko told them the rest.

The clerk on the steps. Master Pell. The page in his pocket. The soft hands. The words that were not his. *Don't buy what you can't trace.* The man in the city guard's coat with the badge cut off, at the back of the crowd, checking that it was being said right, and leaving before the end.

And the woods, on the way back, saying Pell's words.

The long table was very quiet when she had finished.

"So someone's writing it," said Rimuru. Slowly. In his own voice. "The fear. Someone writes it down, and gives it to a clerk, and the clerk reads it out every market day, and people take it home and say it. And the thing in the woods—" He stopped.

"Grows," said Mikasa.

"*The devil is made of what people are afraid of,*" said Rimuru, for Ciel. "*I said that.*" A pause. "*I did not know someone was choosing what they are afraid of.*"

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Mau was at the end of the bench. He had listened to all of it. To *the one with the shadow made a sun*, from a clerk's mouth, in a square, to a hundred people. To *whoever carries for them carries their sickness*. To the woman walking away with the chimney held against her chest.

He did not say *it's about me*. He had said that once, at this table, and Mikasa had told him it was a story, and it was. It was a story somebody was writing.

He looked at Anko instead.

"The man in the coat," said Mau. "The one who left before the end."

Anko looked at him.

"You said he looked at Eren," said Mau.

"For a breath," said Anko.

"What did he see?"

Anko was quiet for a long moment. She looked down at her notebook, at the page, at the line she had underlined. *Didn't need to.*

"I don't know," she said honestly. "Something he recognised, maybe. Something about how Eren was standing." She closed the notebook. "He was a soldier once. City guard. Soldiers know what other soldiers look like when they're holding still." She paused. "I don't know what he saw. But I know he saw something. And then he left."

Fern had the ledger open. She was not writing.

"So they think we started it," said Fern slowly. "The towns. The light, the sheep, the woods. They think it's us." She looked down the table. "And we think someone in the towns is starting it. A clerk. A page. A man in a coat." She paused. "Both sides think someone started it. And both sides are answering."

Nobody said anything.

"And nobody knows who wrote the page," said Fern. She closed the ledger. "Not yet."



At the end of the bench nearest the door, the girl with the bow had not touched her pile of coins.

She sat with her hands in her lap, beside Ori, who had come and sat next to her without being asked, the way she had stood next to her at the line. She looked at the coins. At Fern. At the ledger.

"Wren," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

She went very red. She looked at the ledger, not at anyone. "My name," she said. "You left a space. In the book. I saw." She swallowed. "It's Wren."

Fern looked at her for a moment.

Then she opened the ledger again. To the page with *Hollis* at the top. To the column for the warriors' three parts, where it said *A friend of Ori's*, and then a space the width of a name.

And in the space, very small, very upright, she wrote:

*Wren.*

Beside her, Ori did not say anything. But under the table, very lightly, she bumped Wren's knee with hers.

And Wren, after a moment, bumped it back.
