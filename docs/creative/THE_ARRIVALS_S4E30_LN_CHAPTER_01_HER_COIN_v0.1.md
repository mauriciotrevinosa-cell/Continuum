*When: Days 194 and 195. Hollis has made a second run, and Pell pointed at his cart again, and the good cream sold out anyway. Wren eats with Ori at the end of the bench now. The dead earth in the south field has a green haze on it, if you look from the right place. Coco has been to Echidna's library every week since the ink, looking for another way, and has not found one.*

Coco went to Fern on the hundred-and-ninety-fourth morning, before breakfast, while the long table was still empty and the ledger was open and Fern was alone with it.

She stood by the end of the table with her hands behind her back and waited until Fern looked up.

"I want to use my coin," said Coco.

Fern put her pen down.

She did not say anything for a moment. She looked at the small girl in front of her, with her witch's hat under her arm and her hair tied back and ink on her fingers, the way there was always ink on her fingers now that the tree gave. She looked at her the way she looked at everything that mattered. All of it. At once.

"For your mother," said Fern.

"Yes."

"Sit down," said Fern.

Coco sat down. On the bench, across from Fern, with the ledger between them.

"I wrote something," said Coco. "In my notebook. After the tea at the north wing, when Echidna said about—about the wheel." She did not look away. "I wrote *not until Fern says*. And *not unless he wants to*. And *find another way first*." She swallowed. "I've been looking for another way. For five weeks. Every week, in the library. Agott helped. Tetia helped. Even Richeh helped, a bit, when she was awake." She looked down at the table. "There isn't one. Not one I can find. The lattice works on stone and glass and ice, and it won't work on a person, and nothing in any book in that library says how to fill the centre."

"So you want to ask Mau," said Fern.

"No."

Fern looked at her.

"I'm not asking him," said Coco. "I said I wouldn't. Not unless he wants to. And he'd want to, if I asked. He'd want to before I'd finished asking. That's why I can't." Her voice was very steady. "I'm not asking anyone to fix her."

"Then what are you asking?"

Coco was quiet for a moment.

"On the run," she said. "To Ashby. Hollis said there was a woman. Who bought one of Wakana's chimneys. And she didn't know where it came from. She didn't know anything about us. And she held it like an egg all the way down the street." She looked at her hands on the table. The ink on them. "And I thought about my mother."

Fern did not say anything.

"She's in a house by a stream," said Coco. "In my world. In the room where it happened. With her hand up. The way it was." Her voice did not change. "She doesn't know anything either. She doesn't know how long it's been. She doesn't know I'm here, or that I'm a witch now, or that I've got a teacher, and a library, and a lattice that works on ice." She stopped. "And there's nobody with her. Nobody at all. There hasn't been, since I left."

The common room was very quiet. Somewhere in the kitchen, Momo was lighting the stove.

"I don't need to fix her to bring her," said Coco. "I just need her not to be alone."

Fern looked at her for a long time.

"Bring her," she said. "Or fix her. Which is it?"

"Bring her," said Coco. "Fixing is later." She paused. "Maybe never. I don't know." She looked up. "But if she's here, at least I can sit with her. Every day. At least she'll be in a house where people know her name."

Fern did not say anything for a long moment.

Then she picked up her pen, and turned the ledger to the page of the List, where the coins were. *1. Nijika: her sister. HERE. 2. Rem: her sister. HERE. 3. Yuta: his teacher. HERE.* And further down, in other hands, other lines, with other dates. And near the bottom, a line that had been there since the winter, in Coco's own careful letters, with nothing after it:

*Coco: her mother. When I can.*

Fern looked at it. Then she wrote, beside it, in the margin, very small, the date.

"Yes," said Fern.

Coco let out a breath. It went on for a long time.

"One thing," said Fern.

Coco looked at her.

"If you ever want to fix her," said Fern, "you'll come back and sit on this bench and ask me again. Separately. It's a different question." She closed the ledger. "And I'll want to know what it costs, and who pays, before I say anything."

"I know," said Coco. "I wrote that too."



Qifrey gave her the coin at noon.

He did it in the Witch Hat room, with the door shut. He took it out of the inside pocket of his coat, on the left side, over his heart, where it had been since the night of the bridge, when every stayer felt a coin appear in their hand at sunset, and Coco gave hers to him to hold because she was afraid she would use it too soon. He held it out on his open palm. A small coin, like all of them, of no metal anyone had a name for, with nothing on either side. Warm.

"It's been warm the whole time," said Qifrey.

Coco looked at it.

"Every day," said Qifrey. "Since the bridge. I'd put my hand on it, sometimes, in the night, when I couldn't sleep. To check." He looked down at it. "It never once went cold."

Coco did not take it at once.

"You don't want me to," she said.

Qifrey was quiet.

"You said no," said Coco. "At the tea. To Echidna. When she said about the wheel. You said *no*, like that, in front of everyone. I'd never heard you say anything like that."

"That was about the wheel," said Qifrey. "Not about your mother." He did not lower his hand. "I don't want you to be hurt, Coco. That's all. I have never wanted anything else." He paused. "The crystal is dangerous. We know so little. Whoever touches it might—" He stopped. "And I'm afraid of what it will do to you. To see her. Every day. And not be able to—" He stopped again.

"I know," said Coco.

"But she's your mother," said Qifrey. "And it's your coin. And Fern said yes." He held it out a little further. "So I'm afraid. And here it is."

Coco took it.

It was warm in her hand. Warm the way a hand is warm. She closed her fingers round it.

"Will you be there?" she said. "Tonight. When I do it."

Qifrey looked at her. At the small girl with ink on her fingers, who had come to his atelier a long time ago with her whole world turned to crystal behind her.

"Every step," said Qifrey.



She did it at night, in the Witch Hat room, with everyone.

Agott, on the edge of her bed in the alcove, with her arms folded and her face severe, because Agott's face was always severe when she was frightened. Tetia, on her own bed, hugging her knees, bright-eyed. Richeh, awake, for once, sitting very still against the wall with her half-finished glyph forgotten in her lap. Qifrey, standing by the door, the way he always stood by doors. And Coco, cross-legged in the middle of the floor, on the boards, with a candle in front of her and the coin in her two cupped hands.

The others had offered. Ori. Umaru. The whole fort. Mau, at the long table, had looked at her across the soup and not said anything, and she had known he was offering too. She had said no to all of them. Gently. This was the Witch Hat room. This was hers.

She closed her eyes.

She thought about her mother.

Not the crystal. Before. The house by the stream. Her mother at the table in the evenings, sewing, with the lamp beside her, humming something without words. Her mother's hands, cracked from the washing, rubbing salve into Coco's chilblains in the winter. Her mother's voice, calling her in from the stream at dusk. Her mother laughing at something, once, in the doorway, with the sun behind her, so that her hair was full of light. Her mother saying *Coco* in a hundred different ways, for a hundred different reasons. Cross. Tired. Fond. Frightened.

The last one. Frightened. In the room, at the end. With the spell closing round her like water closing over a stone.

Coco held the coin and thought about all of it.

And the voice came.

It came from nowhere, the way it always came. Not loud. Not soft. A voice that was not a man's or a woman's, that seemed to come from inside the coin and inside Coco's head at the same time. Everyone in the room heard it. Agott's hands tightened on her arms. Tetia stopped breathing.

*You are sure?*

And then it said a name.

Not *Mother*. Not *Mama*. Her mother's name. Her own name, the one her mother had been called before she was anybody's mother, by her own mother, by the people in the village by the stream, by Coco's father, once, a long time ago. The name nobody had said aloud in years. Not even Coco. Coco had never said it in her life. She had never needed to.

The voice said it, very clearly, in the candlelight.

Coco's eyes filled.

"Yes," she said. "I'm sure."

The coin went warm in her hands. Then warmer. Then, all at once, so warm that she gasped and opened her fingers.

There was nothing there. The coin was gone. Her palm was pink where it had been, and the candle had not so much as flickered.

Nobody in the room said anything.

Then Tetia burst into tears, all at once, the way Tetia did everything, and came across the floor on her knees and threw her arms round Coco. And Agott, in her alcove, said "Oh, for—" and got up and came and sat on Coco's other side, stiffly, and put one hand on her shoulder and left it there. And Richeh, against the wall, did not move, but said, very quietly, "Well done, Coco," which was the most anyone had heard her say in a week.

Qifrey did not move from the door.

But when Coco looked up at him, over Tetia's shoulder, with her eyes full, he was not smiling. He had his hand flat on the left side of his coat. Over his heart. Over the empty pocket.

"Now we wait," he said quietly.



They waited all night.

Nobody in the Witch Hat room slept. Nobody in the house slept much. Word went round, the way word went round, in whispers and through walls, and by the small hours half the house was awake in the dark, listening. The fort sat up behind its curtain. Ori sat in her door. Mau lay in the east room with his eyes open, and Frieren lay beside him with hers open, and neither of them said anything, and at the reset hour she said *one* and he said *still here* and from the next room Ori said *two* and none of them went back to sleep.

It came at the eighth bell.



The register girl screamed.

That was how they knew. Not the bell. The scream. Short, and sharp, from the open door of Arrival House across the plaza, in the clear spring morning. And then Jinshi's voice, very calm, very loud, saying *don't touch it, don't anybody touch it*, which was the only time anyone in the settlement had ever heard Jinshi raise his voice.

Coco was out of the house before anyone could stop her.

She ran across the plaza with her hat in her hand and her hair coming loose and Qifrey behind her, running too, long-legged, faster than anyone had ever seen him run. And behind Qifrey, half the house, spilling out of the doors. And she ran up the step of Arrival House, and through the door, into the intake hall.

And stopped.

It stood in the middle of the intake hall, on the boards, in the light from the high windows.

A woman. Made of crystal.

Clear. All of her. Clear as water, as ice, as the eleven lamp chimneys. Clear all the way through, so that you could see the far wall of the intake hall through her, bent and shining, and the light from the high windows came down into her and broke into a hundred small rainbows on the floor round her feet. Every detail perfect. Every thread of her plain dress, every strand of her hair, every crease in her apron. Her face.

She was reaching.

One hand up. Reaching forward and down, toward something small, toward something at the height of a child's head. Her mouth was a little open. In the middle of a word. Her eyes were wide, and looking down, at the place where her hand reached, at the empty air.

At the place where a little girl had been standing, years ago, in a room by a stream.

"*Mama*," said Coco.

She went forward.

She did not think. Her hand was already out. Reaching up, toward the crystal hand reaching down. The way it had been, in that room. Her hand and her mother's, reaching for each other, an inch apart, a lifetime apart.

Qifrey caught her round the waist.

He caught her from behind, both arms, and lifted her off her feet, and pulled her back. Away. Off the boards in front of the crystal, back toward the door.

"*No*—" Coco twisted in his arms. "*No, let go, let GO, it's her, it's MAMA*—"

"I know," said Qifrey. "I know. I know." He did not let go. He held her against his chest with both arms and backed toward the door. "Coco. Whoever touches it. We don't know. It could take you too. It took her. It could take you." His voice was breaking. "I am not losing you to it. I am *not*."

"*LET GO*—"

She fought him. All of her. Kicking, hitting, twisting, screaming her mother's name. And Qifrey held on. He backed out of the intake hall with her in his arms, through the crowd at the door, which parted for him without a word. Down the step. Across the plaza. Toward the house. Toward the fort, where the curtain was already open, where Umaru was already standing on the toll booth platform holding it back, where there were cushions and walls and twenty small people who would close round her.

All the way across the plaza, she screamed. Not words, after a while. Just a sound.

And then, at the arch of the fort, she stopped fighting. All at once. And went limp in his arms. And turned her face into his coat and held on to him and cried, the way she had not cried, Agott said later, since the first night she came to the atelier.



The intake hall emptied.

Jinshi got them out. Gently, firmly, one at a time. *Don't touch it. Don't go near it. Out, please. Out.* The register girl. The half of the house that had followed Coco across the plaza. Anko, who wanted to stay and look, and was told no. Gojo, who had pushed his blindfold up and was looking at the crystal with his bare blue eyes with an expression nobody could read, and who went when Shoko took his arm.

Until there was nobody in the intake hall but the woman made of crystal, reaching down for something that was not there.

And Mau.

He had not left.

He was standing just inside the door, where he had stopped when he came in behind the crowd. He had not gone forward. He had not gone back. He was standing very still with his hands at his sides, looking at the crystal.

Jinshi looked at him.

"Mau," he said. "Please."

Mau did not move.

He was not looking at the crystal the way the others had looked at it. Not with horror. Not with pity. He was looking at it the way you look at something that is speaking to you, very quietly, in a language you are just beginning to understand.

Because something was.

Far back. Behind the new walls. Behind the filter and the blindfold hours and Gojo's lessons and Ciel's careful pinned places. Something had lifted its head.

Not the eyes. The eyes were quiet. He was not looking through anything. He was looking at a woman made of crystal, the way you look at a woman made of crystal.

Not Ear. Ear was asleep in his shadow, curled, quiet.

It was the wheel.

It had not turned. It was not turning. It was not reaching, or pressing, or wanting, the way it had wanted in the south field, the way it had pressed at the walls like an injury. It was just there. Awake. Looking at the crystal through him. Very still. Very interested.

The way a craftsman looks at a lock he has never seen before.

The way a doctor looks at a wound.

*Mau,* said Ciel, in his head. Carefully. *I feel it too.*

He did not answer her at once. He stood in the doorway of the intake hall in the morning light, with the hundred small rainbows on the floor round the crystal woman's feet, and listened to the thing in him that had lifted its head.

It was not asking to come out.

It was saying something else. Something he had never once heard it say, in two years. Something very quiet and very sure.

*I think I can help with that.*

"Ciel," said Mau.

His voice came out very level.

"Tell Rimuru and Yuta to come," he said. "Now. Here." He did not take his eyes off the crystal. "Something in me is saying it thinks it can help."
