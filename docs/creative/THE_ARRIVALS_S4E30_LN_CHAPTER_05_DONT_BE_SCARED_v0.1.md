*When: Day 195, a few minutes later, the same hall. Mau is on the floor with Coco holding him up. Fern has closed the ledger. Shoko is coming up the step with her bag. Inside the slime, something is being made.*

*It's done,* said Ciel.

She said it in Mau's head and, at the same moment, through Rimuru, out loud, from the middle of the great blue slime. Everyone in the intake hall heard her.

"*The skill,*" said Rimuru, for her. "*It's complete.*"

Coco lifted her head from Mau's shoulder.

"*I made it from both,*" said Rimuru, for Ciel. "*The binding as it is in her, whole. The binding as it moved in him, from the beginning, and the wheel undoing it, all eight turns. The order. The centre.*" A pause. "*It can go the other way now. Not for him. For anyone the binding has closed on.*" Another pause, very slightly different. "*I have named it. I will tell you the name later. It is not important now.*"

"Is it—" Coco's voice broke. "Can it—now? Can it—"

"*I've used it,*" said Rimuru, for Ciel.

The hall went completely silent.

"*While he was coming out,*" said Rimuru. "*It seemed wasteful to wait.*" A pause. "*She is not crystal any more. She is held, still, the way everything Rimuru holds is held. But she is herself.*" Another pause. "*I recommend bringing her out now. At once.*"

"Why at once?" said Fern, from the door. Sharply.

"*Because she is waking up,*" said Rimuru, for Ciel. "*And she is in the dark. In a place with no floor and no walls and no sound. And the last thing she remembers is—*" Ciel stopped. "*I would not want her to wake up alone in there. Not after that.*"

Coco was on her feet.

She did not remember getting up. She had been on the floor holding Mau, and then she was standing, and Mau was on the boards, and Frieren had come across the hall from the bench and was kneeling beside him, catching him as he tipped. And Coco was standing in front of the great blue slime with her hands at her sides and her hair all the way loose and her face wet, looking up at it.

"Bring her out," said Coco. "Please. Please, Rimuru. Bring her out."

The slime leaned forward. Very gently. The way a wave leans before it breaks on a beach.

And opened.



She came out on her knees.

The way Mau had. Onto the bare boards of the intake hall, in the light from the high windows. A woman. Not glass. Not clear. A woman, in a plain dress and an apron, with her hair coming loose from its knot, and colour in her face, and her chest moving. Breathing. On her knees on the boards, with one hand up. Reaching. Forward and down. Toward the height of a child's head.

Her mouth was open.

And she finished the word.

"—*scared*," she said.

It came out of her all at once. Into the bright still hall. In a voice that was hoarse from years of not being used, and urgent, and frightened. The end of a sentence that had started in a room by a stream, years ago, and had never been allowed to finish.

"*Don't be scared*," said Coco's mother.

To the empty air at the height of a child's head.

Nobody in the hall moved.

Coco's mother knelt on the boards with her hand reaching, and blinked. Once. Twice. She was looking at the place where her hand reached. At the empty air. And her face changed. Slowly. From the urgent frightened face of a woman in the middle of the worst moment of her life, to something else. Confusion. The face of someone who has turned round in a familiar room and found it is not the room they were in.

Her hand came down, a little.

She looked up.

Not at the height of a child's head. Higher. At the girl standing in front of her. A girl, not a little one. Taller. Thinner. With her hair loose and wet on her face, and a witch's hat crushed in one hand, and ink on her fingers. Standing very still, a step away. Shaking.

Coco's mother looked at her.

For a long time. Her face did not move. She looked at the girl's face, and at her hair, and at the ink on her fingers, and at the hat, and back at her face. The way you look at someone you know, when you have been told they are dead, and they are standing in front of you, and you do not dare believe it.

"Coco?" she said.

Coco's face crumpled.

"*Mama*," she said.

She did not run. She could not, any more. She went down on her knees on the boards, in front of her mother, at the height her mother's hand was still half-reaching for. Not the height of a little girl's head. Higher. Her own height now. And her mother's reaching hand came the rest of the way. It came forward the last inch, the inch it had been trying to cross for years. And touched her face.

Warm.

Coco's mother made a sound that was not a word. And then both her arms were round her daughter, and Coco's were round her, and they knelt on the bare boards of the intake hall in the light from the high windows, holding each other, and neither of them said anything at all. Coco cried. Her mother cried. Neither of them seemed to know it.

In the doorway, Qifrey had put his hand over his eyes.

Fern was standing beside him with the ledger closed against her chest. She was looking at the floor. Not at the two of them. At the floor. Very hard. As if the floor were very important. Her face was quite still and her eyes were very bright.

Behind them, in the plaza, someone had started crying, loudly, and someone else had told them to be quiet, and then started crying too.



It went on for a long time.

When Coco's mother lifted her head, at last, from her daughter's hair, she did not let go of her. She held Coco at arm's length, by the shoulders, and looked at her. All of her. Her face, her hair, the hat on the floor beside them, her hands. Her mother looked at her hands for a long time. At the ink on them.

"You're so tall," said Coco's mother.

Her voice was hoarse and shaking.

"You're so *tall*. How are you so—how long has it—" She stopped. She looked round. For the first time. At the hall. At the bare boards and the high windows and the light. At the great blue slime in the middle of the floor, which was, as she watched, becoming a slight, tired, ordinary-looking young man in a coat, sitting down very suddenly on the boards with his head in his hands. At the tall white-haired man in the doorway with a black cloth pushed up on his forehead and blue eyes. At the small serious girl with a book. At the young man lying on the floor by the wall with his eyes closed. At the woman kneeling beside him with long white hair and long pointed ears.

Her face went white.

"Coco," she said. Very quietly. Her hands tightened on Coco's shoulders. "Where are we?"

"It's all right," said Coco.

"Where *are* we? That was—that man was—" She was looking at Rimuru. "And that woman's ears—and that man's *eyes*—Coco, the spell, the room, I was standing in the—"

"Mama," said Coco. "It's all right. It's all right. I promise."

"How can it be all right, I don't know where—"

"Mama," said Coco. "Hold on."

Her mother stopped.

Coco was looking at her. Very straight. With her wet face and her loose hair and her shaking hands still on her mother's arms. And something in her face was not the face of a little girl any more. Something steady.

"Hold on," said Coco again. "Just a little. Please." Her voice wobbled, and steadied. "I'll tell you everything. All of it. Where we are, and who they are, and how long it's been, and what happened." She swallowed. "I promise. Every part. But not here. Not on the floor of the intake hall."

"Coco—"

"I want everything to be ready," said Coco.

Her mother looked at her.

"I've been waiting," said Coco. "For years. For you. And I never—I never had anywhere. I was in Qifrey's atelier, and then here, and I never had a room to put you in. I never had a place to bring you. I never—" She stopped. Her face was crumpling again. She held it. "I want to get your room ready. And a bed. With a quilt. And make food. Proper food. And put flowers in the window. And then bring you to it. And sit you down. And then tell you everything." She was crying again. "Please. Just let me get it ready. I've wanted to get it ready for so long."

Her mother did not say anything for a long moment.

She knelt on the boards in a strange bright hall, in a place she did not know, surrounded by people who were not quite people, with her daughter in front of her, years older than she had been a moment ago. And she looked at her daughter's face.

Then her hand came up, and touched Coco's cheek again. Wiped it, with her thumb. The way she had wiped it a thousand times, in a house by a stream.

"All right," said Coco's mother. Very softly. "All right, my love. I'll hold on."

She looked past Coco, at the doorway. At the tall man with his hand over his eyes.

"Is he yours?" she said. "Your teacher?"

Coco nodded.

Qifrey took his hand away from his face. He came forward. Into the hall. He knelt down on the boards beside them, a little way off, the way Mau had knelt in the fort.

"I'm Qifrey," he said. His voice was not steady. "I've been looking after her. I'm sorry. For everything." He paused. "She's been very brave."

Coco's mother looked at him for a long time.

"I can see that," she said.



By the wall, Mau had not opened his eyes.

He was lying on the boards with his head in Frieren's lap. He could not have lifted it if he had tried. He was not asleep. He could hear everything. Coco's mother saying *don't be scared*. Coco saying *Mama*. The crying in the plaza. *Hold on*. *I want everything to be ready*. He heard all of it, from very far away, the way you hear a house through a wall when you are lying in bed with a fever.

Frieren's hand was on his forehead.

Cold. Her hands were always cold.

"Five minutes," said Frieren, very quietly. Not to anyone. To him.

He did not open his eyes. "I said."

"You said."

"Did I come back?"

"You came back," said Frieren. Her thumb moved, very slightly, over his eyebrow. "You told me first. And then you came back." She was quiet for a moment. "Both."

Footsteps. Fast. A bag set down on the boards beside him, hard.

"Out of the way," said Shoko.

Frieren did not move out of the way. Shoko knelt down on Mau's other side anyway, and put two fingers on the inside of his wrist, and her other hand flat on his chest, and looked at nothing, counting.

"Hm," said Shoko.

Mau opened one eye.

"Boring?" he said.

Shoko looked down at him. At his face, grey with exhaustion. At his right hand, lying open on the boards, pink and warm and his. She picked it up and turned it over and looked at the fingers, one at a time, very carefully, the way she looked at things she had opened. She pressed the fingertips. She looked at the nails.

"Not boring," said Shoko.

She put his hand down.

"Next week," she said. "Next week, boring. You'll lie in bed for three days and drink what I give you and you'll be so boring I'll write a paper about it." She stood up. "Somebody carry him."

Gojo was already there. He had come across the hall from the doorway without anyone seeing him come, and he bent down, and picked Mau up off the boards, out of Frieren's lap, the way you pick up a sleeping child. Easily. Carefully. With one arm under his knees and one under his shoulders.

"Hey, kid," said Gojo.

Mau looked up at him. At the blue eyes, uncovered, very close.

"That was—" said Gojo. He stopped. He did not seem to know what it was. His face was doing something it did not usually do. "That was something."

"Was it boring?" said Mau.

Gojo laughed. Short. Cracked. Exactly the way he had laughed in the south field, once, kneeling in the snow with Mau's head in his hand.

"No," said Gojo. "No, kid. It wasn't boring."

He carried him out of the intake hall. Past Coco and her mother on the floor, still holding each other. Past Qifrey, kneeling. Past Rimuru, sitting on the boards with his head in his hands, being very quietly held by Yuta, who had let his cursed energy down at last and was kneeling with one arm round him. Past Fern, in the doorway, with the ledger. Out, into the plaza, into the sun.

The plaza went quiet when they came out.

The whole crowd. The house, and the fort, and the six from Arrival House, and the witches, and Hollis, who had come up for the morning and stayed. All of them. They went quiet and watched Gojo carry Mau across the plaza toward the house, with Frieren walking beside them and Shoko behind.

Nobody cheered. Nobody said anything.

But as they passed the fort's curtain, Umaru, standing on the toll booth platform with the Ministry slate, held it up. She did not say anything either. She just held it up, so he could see it, if he opened his eyes.

He opened his eyes.
