*When: Day 195, from the afternoon into the night. Mau is in bed by Shoko's order. Rimuru is a slime in the loft and has not moved since Yuta carried him up. Coco's mother is sitting in Arrival House with a cup of tea she has not drunk, holding on.*

The empty room was in the west wing, at the end, past Ram's door and Seika's.

It was the newest room in the house. Rimuru's rule: one empty room, always. The last empty room had gone to Shoko, on the day she came, while Mau was still in the clinic. And the week after, by the rule, Wakana had chalked this one out on the floor at the end of the corridor, and the beams had gone up in the spring rain, and it had been finished, and swept, and left. A bed frame with no mattress. A window with no curtain. A shelf with nothing on it. And on the door, a card in a little brass holder, in Fern's small upright hand, the same words she had pencilled on the first plan of the west wing on moving day: *For whoever comes*.

Wakana had already chalked the next one, two doors down. So this one could be filled.

Coco stood in the doorway of it at two in the afternoon with her hat in her hand and looked at it.

"This one," she said.

Fern, beside her, with the ledger, nodded once. She took out her pencil. On the card in the little brass holder on the door, under *For whoever comes*, in her small upright hand, she wrote:

*Came.*

Then she gave the pencil to Coco.

Coco looked at the card for a long moment. Then she wrote her mother's name under it. The name the voice had said, in the candlelight. The name Coco had never said aloud in her life.

She did not say it now either. She wrote it.



Everyone came.

Not because anyone asked them. Nobody asked anybody. Word went round the house the way word always went round, through walls and down corridors and across the yard. *The little witch's mother. The empty room. She wants it ready by dusk.* And by three o'clock the corridor of the west wing was full.

Wakana and Marin came first, with a mattress between them, and then with a quilt. Not any quilt. The one Wakana had been making in the evenings since the winter, in squares, out of every scrap of cloth the house had ever thrown away, without telling anyone what it was for. It had the pale blue from Marin's dating-day dress in it, and the red left over from Diablo's sash, and a square of bright orange that Marin swore was left over from something and would not say what. Wakana had not known what it was for, he said, when he started. He had just known somebody would need it.

"Now I know," said Wakana, and spread it on the bed, and smoothed it, and stepped back. Marin put her hand on the small of Wakana's back and left it there.

Senku came with a lamp.

Not an ordinary lamp. A lamp with a glass chimney. Clear and whole and very slightly lopsided, a little fatter on one side than the other. He set it on the shelf by the bed, very carefully, with both hands.

"Twelve," he said.

Coco looked at him.

"Eleven went to Ashby," said Senku. "Forty tries. Twenty-nine broken." He did not look at her. He was adjusting the lamp's wick with great concentration. "I made one more. In the night. After the run. When Wakana was asleep." He pushed the wick down a fraction. "Forty-one tries. It's the worst one. It's fatter on that side." He stepped back. "Ten billion percent the worst one."

Wakana, at the door, was staring at him.

"You made a forty-first," he said. "Without me."

"You were asleep," said Senku. "You'd broken twenty-nine. You deserved to be asleep." He did not look at him either. "It seemed like someone might need a lamp."

Momo came with soup. Of course she did. A covered pot of leek and potato, with the good bacon, and a loaf of pear bread still warm, and two bowls, and two spoons. She set them on the little table under the window and did not say anything and went away again, very fast, before anyone could see her face.

Kusuri came with a pot of hand cream.

She held it out to Coco in the doorway. The good cream. A small pot, the size of a fist.

"For her hands," said Kusuri.

Coco looked at it. And then, slowly, her face changed.

"Her hands," she said. "They were always cracked. From the washing. In the winter. They used to bleed." She took the pot. "I used to—when I was little, she'd rub salve into my chilblains, and I'd rub it into her hands after, and she'd say—" She stopped.

"Now she won't have to," said Kusuri, and smiled, and went away.

Maomao came behind her, with nothing in her hands, and stood in the doorway of the room and looked at it with her clinical eyes. At the bed, the quilt, the lamp, the soup.

"I'll look at her in the morning," said Maomao. "Properly. Not tonight. Tonight she should just sit somewhere warm and eat soup and look at you." She paused. "Years in a binding. I don't know what that does to a body. I'd like to find out gently."

"Thank you," said Coco.

"Don't thank me yet," said Maomao. "I haven't done anything. I might find something terrible." She almost smiled. "I probably won't."

Daphne came from the barn with a jar of honey, and handed it over, and said goodbye to it under her breath, and then, after a moment, came back with a second jar, and handed that over too, and went away without saying goodbye to that one at all.

Seiko sent crackers. She did not come herself. She sent them with Okarun, in a paper twist, with a note in her brushwork that nobody could read except Momo, who read it and went pink and would not say what it said.

Himmel came with a flower. A single one, white, from the foot of the vermilion columns at the gate, where something small and white grew in the spring. He put it in a cup of water on the windowsill, and stepped back, and looked at it, and moved it a fraction to the left, so the light would come through it better.

"Every room should have a flower," said Himmel. "When someone's come a long way."

The fort came last. All of it. Up the west wing stair in a procession, in hoods, with Umaru at the front holding the Ministry slate over her head with both hands. They did not come into the room. They stopped in the corridor outside, in a heap, and Umaru held the slate up so Coco could see it through the door.

*WELCOME (MOTHER OF COCO).*

And under it, in a different colour, smaller, in a dozen different hands, each one adding a word:

*(SHE IS VERY BRAVE.)*

*(SHE DREW THE LATTICE 81 TIMES.)*

*(SHE GAVE MILIM HER LAST BISCUIT ONCE.)*

*(SHE IS OUR FRIEND.)*

*(THE FORT VOUCHES FOR HER.)*

Coco looked at the slate for a long time.

Then she went and stood in the corridor in front of the whole fort, and did not say anything, and they did not say anything either. Until Tetia burst into tears, the way Tetia did, and the whole fort fell on her in a heap, Coco included, and Umaru held the slate up over the heap so it would not get trodden on.

Agott, at the back of the fort, did not join the heap. She stood against the wall with her arms folded and her face severe.

When it was over, and the fort had untangled itself, Agott came forward. She held something out to Coco. A sheet of paper. Folded.

"Satella brought it," said Agott. "From the north wing. A minute ago. From Echidna."

Coco unfolded it.

It was not a letter. It was a page. A single page, very old, the colour of weak tea, in a language made of shapes. Torn out, very neatly, along the spine, from a pale book. The third page. The one with the lattice on it, drawn small. The outer lattice, and the smaller one nested inside it, with the empty centre.

And under it, in the margin, in fresh ink, in a fine pale hand that was not the woman's in the other world, two words.

*Finish it.*

Coco looked at the page for a long time.

Then she folded it again, very carefully, and put it in her private notebook, between the pages, beside her own two lattices and her own empty centre. And the word she had written there, on a snowy night, and never crossed out. *Mau?*

She did not cross it out now either. She drew a small tick beside it. The same as the one under *not unless he wants to*.



Two people did not come.

Mau was in bed in the east room, by Shoko's order, and could not have got up if he had tried. And Rimuru was a slime in the loft above the closet, where Yuta had carried him in both arms at one o'clock and put him down on his pillow, and he had not moved since. Not to eat. Not to change shape. Not for anything.

Nobody expected them to.

At dusk, Coco went back across the plaza to Arrival House. Her mother was where she had left her, on a bench that Jinshi had brought back into the intake hall for her, with a cup of tea she had not drunk and a blanket round her shoulders, looking at the high windows. Qifrey was sitting on the bench beside her. Not close. Close enough. He had been sitting there all afternoon. They had not talked much, he said later. She had asked him a few things. He had answered. Mostly they had sat.

"It's ready," said Coco.

Her mother looked up.

"Your room," said Coco. "It's ready. Everything." Her voice was shaking. "Will you come?"

Her mother put the cup down.

She stood up. Slowly. Still unsteady, on legs that had not walked in years. Qifrey stood up too, and offered his arm, and she looked at it, and then took it. And Coco took her other hand. And the three of them went out of the intake hall, across the plaza in the dusk, past the fort and the long table and the yard and the ink tree, and up the west wing stair, and along the corridor, past Ram's door and Seika's, to the room at the end.

The card on the door said *For whoever comes*. And under it, *Came*. And under that, a name.

Her mother stopped in front of the card.

She read it. Her own name. In her daughter's hand.

She did not say anything. She put her fingers on it. Then she went in.

The lamp was lit. Senku's forty-first try, fatter on one side, throwing a warm uneven light over everything. The quilt on the bed, all its squares, blue and red and orange and a hundred other colours. The soup on the table under the window, still warm under its cover. The pear bread. The two bowls. The honey. The crackers in their paper twist. Kusuri's pot of cream on the shelf. And on the windowsill, in a cup of water, a single white flower, placed so the last of the light came through it.

Coco's mother stood in the middle of the room and looked at all of it.

For a long time.

"Who did this?" she said. Very quietly.

"Everyone," said Coco. "Everyone in the house. I didn't ask them. They just came." She swallowed. "Wakana made the quilt. Senku made the lamp. Momo made the soup. The fort made a sign. Himmel brought the flower." She stopped. "Everyone except two. And they would have, but they're asleep. Because of you. Because of what they did for you." She looked at her mother. "I'll tell you about them. I'll tell you about all of them."

Her mother sat down on the edge of the bed. On the quilt. She put one hand flat on it, on a square of orange. She looked up at her daughter.

"Tell me," she said. "Now. Everything."

Coco looked at Qifrey, in the doorway.

He nodded, once. And stepped back. And closed the door. Gently. And stood outside it, in the corridor, the way he stood outside doors.

Inside, Coco sat down on the bed beside her mother, and began.



In the east room, Mau woke at nine.

He did not know where he was, for a moment. Then he did. The bed. The reading chair. The shelf in the middle of the floor. The cars on the sill, with Ori's stone between them. The lamp, turned low. His whole body very heavy, very far away, as if it had been filled with sand while he slept.

He turned his head.

The other side of the bed was empty.

"Frieren?" he said. It came out very small.

From the door between the rooms, open a hand's width, Ori's voice. "In the bath."

He let out a breath.

"The small one," said Ori. "The queen's one. She's been in there an hour." A pause. "She sat on the bench in the intake hall all morning. She needed to sit somewhere warm and not be brave."

Mau did not say anything.

"Go back to sleep," said Ori. "She'll come."

He went back to sleep.



Above him, in the loft over the closet, something moved.

It had not moved since one o'clock. It had lain on its pillow in the dark, very flat, very still, a pale blue puddle of exhausted slime, the way it had lain after Foundation, the way it had lain after the walls. It had held a woman made of crystal and a man turning to crystal in its stomach, and felt every turn of a wheel inside it, and held all of it still, and then made itself back into a person and sat down on the floor. And then it had stopped being able to do anything at all.

It lifted one edge of itself, very slightly, off the pillow.

It looked down through the hatch in the loft floor. At the bed below. At Mau, asleep, alone, on his back, with his mouth a little open, and the other side of the bed empty.

It considered this for a long moment.

Then the slime gathered itself together, very quietly, into a ball the size of a cat. And rolled to the edge of the hatch. And dropped.

It landed on Mau's stomach.

"*Oof*," said Mau, waking all at once.

The slime settled. It spread itself out, comfortably, across his stomach, like a cat claiming a warm spot. It was surprisingly heavy for something that looked like a puddle.

"Rimuru," said Mau. His voice was strangled. "What are you—"

"You used me," said the slime. In Rimuru's voice. Muffled. Very satisfied. "All day. You used me as a *room*. You put a crystal woman in my stomach and then you got in there with her and *turned to glass*. Inside me. I felt *all of it*." It wriggled, slightly, getting comfortable. "So now I'm going to use you back."

"As *what*?"

"A mattress," said the slime. "You're warm. And I'm too tired to climb back up." It went still. "Goodnight."

Mau lay on his back with a slime on his stomach and stared at the ceiling.

He could not move it. He could not move anything. He did not, he found, very much want to.

"Goodnight," he said.



The door opened at half past nine.

Frieren came in from the bath. She was in the orange hoodie, which she always put on after the bath, too big, with the sleeves over her hands, and her hair down and damp, and her face pink from the heat. She shut the door behind her, quietly, because she thought he was asleep.

Then she turned round. And saw the bed.

Mau, on his back, awake, looking at her. And on his stomach, spread out comfortably like a cat in the sun, a pale blue slime. Fast asleep.

Frieren stopped.

She stood very still in the middle of the east room, by the shelf, with her damp hair and the sleeves of the hoodie over her hands, and looked at the two of them. At Mau's face. At the slime. At Mau's face again.

Something happened in her face that he had not seen in a long time.

"Mau," said Frieren.

"Yes."

"Now who," said Frieren, very slowly, very seriously, "is cheating on who?"

There was a silence.

Then Mau laughed.

He could not help it. It came up out of him all at once, from very deep, from somewhere under all the sand. He laughed, and it hurt, everything hurt, his whole body was full of sand and every laugh shook it. And on his stomach the slime woke up and said "*Wha*—" and then, understanding, started to shake too, laughing, in a wobbling bubbling way that made Mau laugh harder.

And Frieren, standing by the shelf in her hoodie, put one sleeve over her mouth. And her shoulders went. And she laughed. Not quietly. Not into anything. Out loud, in the middle of the east room, the way she almost never did.

It was the first time.

Neither of them said so. Neither of them would, for a long time. But it was the first time either of them had said one word about that night. The night in the other bed, in the other room, with the hole and the wet sheets and Rimuru, a slime, in her lap, while she counted his breaths. The night the whole house had signed a page in the ledger never to speak of. Unless he did. Or she did.

She had. And it was a joke.

And they were laughing.

When it was over, when they could breathe again, Frieren crossed the room and got into the bed. On his other side. The side that was hers. She did not say anything else. She lay down with her damp head on his shoulder and her cold foot against his leg, and put one arm across him, over the slime, which wriggled once, contentedly, and went still.

"Goodnight," said the slime.

"Goodnight," said Frieren.

"Goodnight," said Mau.

The three of them slept.

In the next room, Ori, who had heard all of it through the door that was open a hand's width, lay in the dark and smiled at the ceiling. Above the second bed, at the end of a red ribbon, Milim floated in her sleep and murmured something about biscuits.

And in the west wing, at the end of the corridor, past Ram's door and Seika's, a door was closed, and a tall man in a long coat stood outside it, and from inside it, very quietly, for a long time, two voices were talking.

One of them was telling everything.

The other one was listening.
