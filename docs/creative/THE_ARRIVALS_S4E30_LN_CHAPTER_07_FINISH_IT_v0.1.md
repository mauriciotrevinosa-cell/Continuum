*When: Day 196, the morning after. Mau is in bed by Shoko's order, on the first of his three boring days. Rimuru woke on Mau's stomach at dawn, said "Ah," and went to find Coco in the west wing, still half a slime, before he rolled back up into the loft. Qifrey stood outside the door at the end of the west wing until two in the morning, and then sat down against it, and fell asleep there.*

Maomao looked at her in the morning, properly, the way she had said she would.

She did it in the room at the end of the west wing, with the door shut and Coco sitting on the end of the bed and the lamp with the forty-first chimney turned up. She looked at everything. Her eyes, and her tongue, and the beat of her heart under two fingers, and the colour of her nails. She asked her to stand, and walk to the window, and walk back. She asked her to close her eyes and touch her nose. She asked her what she remembered, and listened to all of it, and wrote nothing down.

Then she sat back on the chair and looked at her for a long time.

"Nothing," said Maomao.

Coco's mother looked at her.

"There's nothing wrong with you," said Maomao. She sounded, very faintly, disappointed. "You're thin. Your legs are weak. You'll be tired for a week, and you'll want to sleep in the afternoons, and you should. You should eat everything Momo gives you." She paused. "Apart from that, you're exactly as you were. As if you'd stepped out of the room for a moment and come back."

"It's been years," said Coco's mother. Very quietly. "Coco says. Years."

"For her," said Maomao. "Not for you." She stood up. "The binding held you. All of you. Outside time. Like a hand closed round something." She looked down at Coco's mother's hands, on the quilt. "Your hands are better than they've ever been, I'd guess."

Coco's mother looked down at them.

They were soft. Last night, before she slept, Coco had sat on the edge of the bed and rubbed Kusuri's cream into them, slowly, finger by finger, the way she had rubbed salve into them when she was very small. And the cracks across the knuckles, the old ones, from the washing, from the cold, that had been there every winter of her life, were gone. Healed. The skin was soft and whole.

She looked at them for a long time.

"I don't remember them ever being like this," she said.

"Magic," said Maomao, with great distaste, and went out.



It went wrong at noon.

Not badly. Not loudly. Coco would say afterward that she had known it was coming, all morning, the way you know rain is coming. Her mother had been very quiet since Maomao left. She had sat on the bed with her soft hands in her lap, looking at them. And when Coco brought her soup at noon, she put the bowl down on the table without eating it and said:

"Coco. Sit down."

Coco sat down.

Her mother did not look at her for a moment. She looked at the window, at the white flower in its cup, at the yard beyond and the ink tree with its dark leaves.

"Last night," she said. "You told me everything."

"Yes."

"Where we are. How long it's been. Your teacher. The atelier. The witches. The lattice." She paused. "And you told me—" She stopped. "You told me it was you. Who drew it. The spell. In the room."

Coco did not move.

"I knew," said her mother.

Coco looked at her.

"I knew," said her mother, very quietly. "I saw your face. At the end. When it was closing round me. You were standing in the middle of the floor with a pen in your hand, looking at what you'd drawn. And your face—" She stopped. "I knew it was you. I knew you didn't mean it. You were a little girl. You thought it would be beautiful." Her voice did not change. "That's why I said *don't be scared*. Because I could see how frightened you were going to be. Afterward. Of what you'd done."

Coco's eyes filled.

"I'm not angry with you," said her mother. "I've never been angry with you. Not for one second. I want you to know that before I say anything else." She turned and looked at her daughter. "But I'm frightened, Coco."

"Of what?"

"Of it," said her mother. "Magic." She looked down at her soft hands. "It did that to me. It turned me to stone. To glass. It took years from you. It took me from you. And you—" She stopped. "You went *toward* it. You went to a witch's house. You learned to draw it. You're a witch now. You have ink on your fingers. You told me about a lattice you drew eighty-one times in water, and a library full of books about bindings, and a wheel in a young man's shadow that turns things to glass and back." Her voice was shaking. "Everything that happened to us happened because of magic. And you walked straight into it, and you're still in it, and I—"

"Mama—"

"I want to take you away from it," said her mother.

The room went very quiet.

"I don't know how," said her mother. "I don't know where. You said we can't go home. You said there's no going back over the bridge." She was not crying. She was past that. "But there must be somewhere. A town. A village. Somewhere without witches and slimes and wheels. Somewhere you can just be a girl again. Not a witch." She looked at Coco. "I lost years. I'm not losing you to it as well."

Coco did not say anything.

She sat on the edge of the bed with her mother's untouched soup going cold on the table and her mother's soft hands in her mother's lap. And she did not argue. She did not cry. She did not say *no*. She sat very still, the way Fern sat when something mattered, and thought about it.

Then she got up.

"Will you come somewhere with me?" she said. "Before you decide. Just one place. And then you can decide anything you want." She held out her hand. "Please."

Her mother looked at the hand. Small. With ink on the fingers.

She took it.



Coco took her to the Witch Hat room.

Not far. Down the west wing corridor, round the turn, past the narrow room with REM on the door in pencil. Coco's mother walked slowly, on her weak legs, holding Coco's hand. She looked at everything as they passed. The doors. The cards on the doors, with names. The clay walls. The beams.

Agott was in the Witch Hat room, at her table in the alcove. Tetia. Richeh, asleep. And Qifrey, who had woken up against the door at the end of the west wing at dawn with a crick in his neck and not said a word to anyone about it, sitting on the edge of Coco's bed.

They all looked up.

Coco did not say anything to them. She led her mother to the table under the window, the one where she had drawn the outer lattice on a river stone, and pulled out the chair, and sat her down. Then she went to the shelf at the end of the row, and took down the small glass bottle. The one that had been empty for so long, except for a stain at the bottom. The one that was full now, dark and real, because the ink tree in the yard gave a bottle a week.

She put it on the table. She put a pen beside it.

Then she took out her private notebook, and opened it, and took out the page.

The page from the pale book. The one Satella had brought from the north wing. The colour of weak tea, in a language made of shapes. The outer lattice, drawn small. The inner lattice, nested inside it. The empty centre. And in the margin, in fresh ink, in a fine pale hand: *Finish it.*

She laid it on the table in front of her mother.

"This is the spell," said Coco. "The one I drew. When I was little." Her voice was very steady. "Not the same drawing. The same shape. A woman in another world drew it too, a long time ago, on someone she loved. And it closed round them, like it closed round you." She put one finger on the outer lattice. "She spent her whole life working out how to undo it. And she worked out all of it. Every line. Except the middle." Her finger moved to the empty centre. "To undo it in a person, you have to know what shape it took inside them. And nobody can know that from outside. She never found out. She tried it anyway, with the middle empty, on the person she loved." She paused. "That's where the book ends."

Her mother was looking at the page. Her face was very still.

"I found the outside," said Coco. "Here. By myself. I drew it eighty-one times in water on the atelier floor until it was right. And then I drew it in ink, here, on a river stone. And it worked on stone. And glass. And a bird made of ice." She took her finger away. "But not on a person. Because I didn't have the middle."

"And yesterday," said Coco's mother, slowly. "The young man. The wheel."

"He went inside," said Coco. "Inside Rimuru. With you. And he put his hand in yours. The one that was reaching." Her voice wobbled, and held. "And he let it take him. On purpose. So the wheel could feel it go in, and learn the shape, from inside a person. And it did. Eight turns. And Ciel—that's the one who lives in Rimuru, who thinks—Ciel saw the whole shape. Both of you. And made it into something that could undo it." She paused. "That's how you came out."

Her mother did not say anything.

"Ciel told me," said Coco. "This morning. Before you woke up. Rimuru came and found me in the corridor at dawn, still half a slime, and Ciel told me through him." She took a breath. "She told me what the middle was."

She picked up the pen.

She uncapped the ink.

And her mother watched her daughter's hand, with ink on the fingers, the same small hand that had drawn something beautiful in a room by a stream and closed it round her, dip the pen and lift it and hold it over the empty centre of the oldest lattice in Echidna's library.

"It went in through your hand," said Coco. Very quietly. Not looking up. "The reaching one. First. Before anything." She drew a line. Fine. Dark. Into the empty centre, from one edge. "And then up your arm." Another line. "And then it didn't go into your head, or your chest, or anywhere else. It went straight into your heart. Along the arm. Along the reach. As if the reach were a road." A third line, curving. "And from your heart, everywhere."

She drew.

Her mother watched. Agott, at her table in the alcove, had stood up. Tetia had stopped breathing. Richeh had woken, and was sitting up very straight against the wall with her eyes wide. Qifrey, on the edge of the bed, had put his hand over his mouth.

The lines went into the empty centre. Fine and dark and certain. Not the way Coco had drawn the outer lattice, eighty-one times, carefully, checking each angle. Faster. Surer. As if her hand already knew the shape and only had to remember it. Lines folding inward, toward a point. Toward a place in the middle. Toward the shape of something reaching. Forward and down. Toward the height of—

She stopped.

She lifted the pen.

The centre was not empty any more.

It was a shape. Small. Very simple, at the heart of all those lines. You would not have known what it was, if you did not know. It was the shape of a hand, reaching. Forward and down. Toward something small.

Toward the height of a child's head.

"Ciel said," said Coco. Her voice had gone very small. "The shape it took in you. The centre. It's the shape of your reaching." She put the pen down. "Toward me."

The Witch Hat room was completely silent.

"The spell went in along it," said Coco. "Because that's where you were. That's what you were doing, the whole time it closed. Reaching for me. To tell me not to be scared." She looked up at her mother at last. Her eyes were full. "So the middle of the undoing is that. You reaching. For me. That's what was missing. That's what the woman in the book never had." She swallowed. "She was reaching for someone too. She just didn't know that was the answer."

Her mother did not say anything.

She sat at the table under the window with the page in front of her. The old page, the colour of weak tea. The outer lattice, and the inner one, and the centre, finished, in her daughter's ink, still wet, in the shape of a hand reaching down.

She put out one finger. Her soft finger, with no cracks across the knuckle. And very lightly, not quite touching the wet ink, she traced the shape of the reaching hand in the centre.

"It was your hand," said Coco. Barely audible. "That did it. To you. Everyone says it wasn't my fault. Qifrey says. Agott says. Mau says. But it was my hand."

Her mother looked up from the page.

"And now," said Coco, "it's my hand that finished it."

Her mother looked at her for a long time.

At her daughter. Standing at a table in a witch's room, in a house full of monsters and witches and wheels, in another world, years older than she had been a moment ago. With ink on her fingers. With a pen in her hand that had just drawn the end of a spell that a woman in another world had spent a whole life failing to finish. With her face wet, and very steady, and very tired, and not frightened at all.

"Magic did that to me," said her mother. Very slowly.

"Yes."

"And magic undid it."

"Yes."

"Your hand did it."

"Yes."

"And your hand finished it."

"Yes," said Coco.

Her mother looked down at the page again. At the reaching hand in the centre.

"Then it isn't the magic," she said. "Is it."

Coco did not say anything.

"It's the hand," said her mother. "It's what the hand's reaching for." She closed her eyes. "It was always the hand."



Qifrey spoke then. For the first time. From the edge of the bed.

"She wrote three things," he said quietly.

Coco's mother opened her eyes and looked at him.

"In her notebook," said Qifrey. "Five weeks ago. After a tea where someone held out the answer across a table, like a thing you could take, and didn't care what it cost." He did not look at Coco. He looked at her mother. "She wrote: *Not until Fern says.* Fern is the girl with the ledger. The one who counts us. And: *Not unless he wants to.* The young man. Mau. And: *Find another way first.*" He paused. "She could have asked him that night. He'd have said yes before she finished asking. She knew that. That's why she didn't." He looked down at his hands. "She looked for another way for five weeks. And when there wasn't one, she still didn't ask. She brought you here so you wouldn't be alone. That's all. Just so you wouldn't be alone. And he came to her."

Coco's mother looked at him.

"I've taught a great many people magic," said Qifrey. "In my world. Some of them very gifted. Some of them very dangerous." He looked up at her. "I have never taught anyone who was more careful with it than your daughter. Not one."

Coco's mother was quiet for a long time.

She looked at Qifrey. At Agott, standing in her alcove with her arms folded and her face severe and her eyes very bright. At Tetia, crying, the way Tetia did. At Richeh, wide awake, which nobody had seen at this hour in weeks. At the room. The beds. The table. The small glass bottle of ink.

At her daughter.

"I wanted to take you away from it," she said.

"I know," said Coco.

"Somewhere with no witches. No slimes. No wheels." Her mother almost laughed. It came out wet. "I don't think there is anywhere like that. Here."

"No," said Coco. "I don't think there is."

"And if there were," said her mother. "I don't think you'd come."

Coco did not say anything.

Her mother held out her hand. Her soft hand, with no cracks. Across the table. Over the page with the finished lattice, still wet.

Coco took it.

"Then I'll stay," said her mother. "Here. With your witches. And your teacher. And the girl who counts. And the young man with the wheel." She held her daughter's inky hand in her soft one. "And you'll show me. All of it. The lattice. The ink tree. The library. Everything." She paused. "I want to see what my daughter's hands can do."

Coco's face crumpled.

She came round the table and put her arms round her mother, in the chair, and held on. And her mother held her. And in the alcove, Agott said *oh, for—* in a cracked voice and turned round to face the wall. And Tetia sobbed. And Richeh, very quietly, said *well done, Coco*, for the second time in two days.

Qifrey did not say anything. He sat on the edge of the bed and looked at the two of them, and at the page on the table between them, with the reaching hand at its centre. And he put his hand flat on the left side of his coat. Over his heart. Over the empty pocket where the coin had been.

It was not warm any more. There was nothing in it.

He left his hand there anyway.



That night, in the room at the end of the west wing, Coco's mother sat on the edge of the bed with a small pot of cream in her lap.

Coco sat beside her. Her hands were black to the knuckle with ink. She had spent the afternoon drawing the finished lattice, over and over, in the Witch Hat room, while her mother watched. On paper. On a river stone. In the ledger of every witch who asked, which was all of them. On the back of the fort's slate, for Umaru, who had demanded it for the Ministry archive. Forty times. Fifty. Her hands were black with it.

Her mother opened the pot.

She took Coco's hand. The right one. The inky one. And began, slowly, finger by finger, to rub the cream into it. The way Coco had rubbed it into hers, the night before. The way she had rubbed salve into Coco's chilblains, when she was very small, in a house by a stream, every winter.

The ink did not come off. It never did. It was in the creases now, in the nails, in the whorls of the fingertips, the way it was in Qifrey's and Agott's and every witch's in the atelier. It would never quite come off again.

Her mother did not try to make it come off. She just rubbed the cream in. Round the ink. Over it. Into the small cracked places on Coco's knuckles, from the cold and the water and the work.

"Your hands are cracked," said her mother softly.

"They always are," said Coco. "From the ink. And the washing up."

"Mine were always cracked," said her mother. "Every winter."

"I know."

Her mother was quiet for a while, rubbing the cream in.

"Now mine aren't," she said. "And yours are." She almost smiled. "I suppose that's how it goes."

Coco leaned against her. Her head on her mother's shoulder. Her inky hand in her mother's soft one.

"I'll do yours," said Coco sleepily. "Tomorrow. If they crack again."

"They won't," said her mother. "Not for a while." She went on rubbing, gently, finger by finger. "Let me do yours. For a while. I've got years to catch up on."

Outside the door, in the corridor, a tall man in a long coat stood for a moment, listening. Then he went away, quietly, down the west wing stair, to his own room, and lay down, and slept.

He did not sit against the door that night. There was no need.
