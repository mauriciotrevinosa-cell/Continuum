*When: Days 204 and 205. Mau's three boring days are long over and Shoko has allowed him two ends, one at a time. Coco's mother walks to the ink tree every morning and sits under it while Coco draws. Hollis has made a third run; Brenna stood by the cart while the clerk pointed, and the good cream sold out by noon. Summer is coming in; the dead earth in the south field is green at the edges.*

Senku announced it at breakfast, the way he announced everything. As a finding.

"I've decided," he said. "About the coin."

The long table went quiet from his end outward.

Senku did not look up from his porridge. He had a sheet of paper beside his bowl, covered in his small fast handwriting, in columns, with arrows. He had clearly been up most of the night with it.

"I've done the analysis," said Senku. "Every option. Weighted. Costs, benefits, risks to the settlement, risks to the person, separation from others in the source world, net contribution to current projects." He tapped the paper. "There's only one answer. It's ten billion percent the most logical choice. It's not even close."

"Who?" said Fern.

Senku ate a spoonful of porridge.

"Byakuya," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"He's dead," said Senku, to his porridge. "In my world. Thousands of years before I woke up. So bringing him separates him from nobody. No family left behind. No village losing its elder. He's already gone from all of it." He tapped the paper again. "He was an astronaut. A real one. An engineer. He knows more about building things from nothing than anyone alive, because he did it. He built a whole village from nothing, with a handful of people and a stone age. He'd be useful to every project in this house. The glass. The kiln. The route. Hollis's axle, which is going to break by midsummer." He ate another spoonful. "The Goddess said *some* of the dead can come. Gojo came. So it's possible. It's logical. It's the most efficient use of a single coin in this entire settlement."

He put his spoon down.

"That's all," said Senku. "That's the analysis."

The long table looked at him.

For a long moment, nobody said anything at all. Then, from the arch of the fort, Umaru said, very quietly:

"Nobody believes you."

"It's not a matter of belief," said Senku. "It's a matter of data."

"Nobody believes you," said Gojo, cheerfully, from the cold-hearth end.

"Nobody believes you," said Yuka.

"Nobody believes you," said Ram, at the far end, flatly. "It is barely acceptable that you would try."

Senku looked up from his porridge for the first time. He looked down the long table. At forty faces, all looking at him, with forty versions of the same expression.

"It's *logical*," he said.

Beside him, on the bench, Suika put her hand on his arm.

She did not say anything. She had her glasses on, the good ones he had ground for her in the winter. She just put her small hand on his arm, on his sleeve, and left it there.

Senku looked at her hand.

He did not say anything else. He picked up his spoon and went back to his porridge. But his ears had gone red, all the way to the tips, and they stayed red for the rest of breakfast.

Fern opened the ledger. She turned to the page of the List. She wrote, in her small upright hand, on a fresh line:

*Senku: his father. (Logical.)*

And then, after a moment, she crossed out *(Logical)*, very neatly, with one line.



He did it that night, in the lab.

Not in his room. In the lab, with the kiln banked and glowing and the shelves of jars and lenses and crucibles round him, and the long bench where he had ground Suika's glasses, and the NOT YET box in the corner with the dust of seven broken bulbs and a dead crystal in it. It was the only room in the house that had ever felt like his. It was the only room where he could do anything that mattered.

Suika was there. He had not asked her. She had come in at nine with a cup of tea for him and sat down on the stool in the corner by the NOT YET box, where she always sat when he was working late, and not said anything, and he had not told her to go.

He sat on the bench with the coin in his hand.

He had held it every day since the bridge. In his pocket. Turning it over. Weighing it. It was warm, the way they were all warm. He had run every test he could think of on it, in the first month, and it had told him nothing, which he had found deeply offensive. He had written it down. *Coin. Material: unknown. Mechanism: unknown. Warm.*

He looked at it now for a long time.

"Suika," he said.

"Mm."

"You told me. In the winter. When I was grinding your lens." He did not look round at her. "That he made your village."

"Suika told you," said Suika.

"And that the Goddess said *some*."

"Suika told you that too."

Senku was quiet.

"I said it was ten billion percent irrelevant," he said.

"Suika remembers," said Suika.

He turned the coin over in his fingers. Once. Twice.

"It wasn't irrelevant," said Senku. Very quietly. To the coin. "It was the least irrelevant thing anyone's ever said to me." He paused. "I've been thinking about it every day since. Every single day. For five months." He laughed, very short, not at all like himself. "I did the analysis because I didn't know how else to do it. If I wrote it down in columns, I could pretend it was a calculation." He looked at the paper on the bench beside him, with the arrows. "It's not. It was never a calculation."

Suika did not say anything. She sat on her stool in the corner with her cup of tea in both hands, and waited, the way she had waited all afternoon in the winter while he ground her lens.

"He left me a message," said Senku. "In my world. He knew I'd wake up one day. He was certain. So he made a story. A long one. And he taught it to the children, and they taught it to their children, for thousands of years. The hundred tales. And the last one, the hundredth, was just him. Talking to me. Across all that time." He looked at the coin. "Telling me he believed in me. That I'd do it. That I'd bring everyone back." His voice did not change. "And I listened to the whole thing. In a little hut, with Ruri telling it, the day after her fever broke. And I didn't say anything. And then I said it was irrelevant. And went back to work."

He closed his hand round the coin.

"I never got to answer," said Senku. "He talked to me across three thousand seven hundred years. And I never got to say anything back."

Suika put her tea down on the floor.

"Say it now," she said.

Senku closed his eyes.

The voice came. From nowhere. From inside the coin and inside his head. Suika heard it too. She sat up very straight on her stool.

*You are sure?*

*Byakuya Ishigami.*

Senku did not open his eyes.

"Ten billion percent," he said.

The coin went warm. Then warmer. Then so warm that he gasped, and opened his fingers.

There was nothing there.

His palm was pink where it had been. The kiln glowed. The jars on the shelves did not so much as rattle. And Senku Ishigami sat on the bench in his lab with his empty hand open in front of him, looking at it, and did not move for a very long time.

Suika got off her stool.

She came across the lab and sat down on the bench beside him. Close. She did not touch him. She picked up the sheet of paper with the columns and the arrows, and looked at it, holding it very close to her glasses, the way she still held things close even with the good lenses.

"Senku," she said. "Your sums are wrong here."

He looked at her.

"There," said Suika, pointing. "Benefits. You forgot one."

"What?" said Senku. His voice did not quite work.

Suika took the pencil from behind his ear. And at the bottom of the column marked *BENEFITS*, under *engineering* and *astronautics* and *village-building* and *Hollis's axle*, in her careful round letters, she wrote one more line.

*Senku gets to say something back.*

She put the pencil behind his ear again.

"Now it's logical," said Suika.



He came at seven in the morning.

Arrival House. The intake hall. Where Coco's mother had stood in crystal a week and a half before, and Jinshi had cleared the benches. They were back now. The register girl was at her desk. Jinshi was at the door with the key. And half the house was in the plaza, not looking as if it were waiting, because Senku had told everyone at the long table that it was ten billion percent unnecessary for anyone to wait, and so everyone had come.

Senku was not in the plaza.

He was standing just inside the door of the intake hall. Alone. With his hands in the pockets of his coat and his hair sticking up in its usual impossible way and his face doing nothing at all. He had been standing there since six.

Suika was on the step outside. Watching him through the door.

At seven, the air in the middle of the intake hall moved.

Not a light. Not a sound. Just the air, the way air moves over a road in summer. And then there was a man standing on the boards in the middle of the hall, where there had been nobody.

A man of about forty. Tall. Broad in the shoulders. With a big open friendly face and a jaw like a shovel and a shock of hair that stuck up from his head in exactly the same impossible way as Senku's, as if both of them had been struck by the same lightning. He was wearing a jumpsuit. White, with patches on the shoulders, and a name on the chest, and a flag.

He looked round.

At the high windows. At the clay walls. At the register girl, who had stood up behind her desk with her pen in her hand. At Jinshi in the doorway with the key.

"Huh!" said the man, delighted. His voice was very loud. "That's new!"

He turned round slowly, taking in all of it. The beams. The floor. The light.

"I was dead," he said, conversationally, to the register girl. "I'm pretty sure I was dead. I remember being very old, and very tired, and lying down in a hut with everyone round me, and thinking, well, Byakuya, that's that." He looked down at his own hands. Big, square, a working man's hands, with no age spots on them. "These aren't old." He flexed them. "Huh!"

The register girl did not seem able to say anything.

"Is this heaven?" said Byakuya. "It's very nicely built. I like the joinery." He looked up at the beams. "Who did the joinery? That's good work. That's someone who knows what they're—"

He stopped.

He had turned round. And seen the young man standing just inside the door.

He did not say anything for a moment. He looked at him. At the hair. At the face. At the hands in the coat pockets. At the eyes, which were red, though their owner did not seem to know it.

The young man in the door did not move either.

"Senku?" said Byakuya.

It came out very small. Not loud at all. Not like anything else he had said.

Senku did not answer.

He stood in the doorway of the intake hall with his hands in his pockets. His face did not do anything. It did not do anything for so long that Byakuya took a step toward him, and then stopped, as if afraid that if he came any closer the young man in the door would turn out to be something else. A story. A dream. A thing a very old man thinks he sees at the very end.

"You woke up," said Byakuya.

Senku took his hands out of his pockets.

"I woke up," he said.

"You—" Byakuya's voice cracked, all at once, in the middle of the word. "You did it? Everything? Everyone?"

"Most of it," said Senku. His voice was perfectly level. Perfectly flat. "Still working on the rest."

Byakuya made a sound that was not a word.

He crossed the intake hall. Not running. He could not seem to run. He walked across it in four long strides, the way a man walks toward something he has been walking toward for three thousand seven hundred years. And Senku did not move. He stood in the doorway with his hands at his sides and his face doing nothing at all.

Until Byakuya got there. And put both his big square hands on Senku's shoulders. And looked at him, very close.

"I left you a message," said Byakuya. Barely audible. "Did it—did you ever—"

"I got it," said Senku.

"All of it? The whole—"

"Every word," said Senku. "In a hut. Ruri told it." His voice was still level. "I said it was ten billion percent irrelevant."

Byakuya stared at him.

Then he laughed. It came out of him wet and enormous and broken, the way it comes out of a man who has been holding his breath for longer than a lifetime. And he pulled his son in, both arms, all the way, and held on.

Senku stood very still in his father's arms.

For a long moment, he did not do anything. His arms stayed at his sides. His face stayed flat.

Then, very slowly, as if remembering how, his arms came up. And held on back.

"It wasn't irrelevant," said Senku. Into his father's jumpsuit. Very quietly. So quietly that only Byakuya could hear it. "It was never irrelevant. I just didn't know how to answer."

"You're answering," said Byakuya. Into his son's impossible hair. "Right now. That's an answer."



On the step outside, Suika had taken off her glasses.

She was holding them in both hands, in her lap, and the intake hall was a blur of light and shapes through the door, and she could not see Senku's face, or his father's, or anything at all clearly. She did not need to. She could see two shapes in the doorway, one big and one not, holding on to each other. That was enough.

Beside her, Gojo had sat down on the step. He did not say anything. He was looking through the door with his blindfold pushed up, at the two shapes, with an expression that was very hard to read.

"Dead guy," he said, after a while, very quietly. "Came back. Saw his kid." He paused. "That's what it looks like. From outside." He looked down at his own hands. "Huh."

Shoko, on his other side, put her hand flat on the step beside his. Not touching. Close.

On the step below them, Suika put her glasses back on. The intake hall came back into focus. Sharp. All at once.

Senku was looking at her.

Over his father's shoulder, through the door, with his face wet and his hair sticking up and his arms round his father. Looking straight at her. And as she watched, his mouth moved. He did not say anything out loud. She was too far away to have heard it if he had.

But she knew what it said. She could read it, with the good lenses on.

*Ten billion percent.*

Suika smiled.
