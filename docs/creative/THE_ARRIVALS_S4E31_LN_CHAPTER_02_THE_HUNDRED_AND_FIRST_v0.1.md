*When: Days 206 to 208. Byakuya has a room in the west wing, two doors past Coco's mother, and has already rebuilt the latch on it because he did not like the old one. The first week of summer. The south field is green almost to the middle.*

Byakuya Ishigami was loud.

That was the first thing everyone in the house learned about him, and they learned it in the first hour. He was loud the way a bell is loud, without meaning to be, simply because it is a bell. He laughed at everything. He laughed at Rimuru being a slime, delightedly, and asked if he could poke him, and did, and laughed harder. He laughed at Gojo's blindfold and asked if it was a fashion thing, and Gojo said yes, and Byakuya said *I respect that*. He laughed at the fort, and at the toll booth, and at the ONE DAY PASS that Umaru issued him at the arch, and then he looked at it very seriously and put it in the breast pocket of his jumpsuit and buttoned the pocket.

He did not laugh at Frieren. He stood in front of her at the long table on the first evening, with his big hands at his sides, and looked at her ears, and her white hair, and her green eyes, for a long moment.

"Elf," he said. Wonderingly. "An actual elf."

"Yes," said Frieren.

"How old?"

"Over a thousand."

Byakuya looked at her for a long time.

"I was in space for six months," he said. "Once. It was the longest I'd ever been away from anything." He paused. "Then I was on the ground for the rest of my life, waiting for my kid to wake up. That felt longer." He shook his head. "A thousand years." He looked at her with an expression that was not pity, and not awe. Something more like respect, from one long waiter to another. "That's a lot of waiting."

Frieren looked back at him.

"It's less," she said, "when there's something at the end of it."

Byakuya looked at her. Then down the bench, at the young man sitting beside her, who was carefully eating his soup and not looking at anyone. Then back at Frieren.

"Yeah," said Byakuya softly. "Yeah, it is."



He did not need anything. That was the second thing everyone learned.

He did not need to be shown round. He did not need to be told how things worked. He walked round the whole settlement on the first morning, by himself, at six, while the house was still asleep, and came back to breakfast knowing where everything was and what it was for and what was wrong with most of it.

"Your mule cart's axle is going to break," he said to Hollis, at breakfast, before anyone had introduced them. Hollis had come up the night before with the empty cart, for the next load. "The left one. There's a crack in the hub. Running round about a third of it. You can see it if you get underneath." He took a slice of pear bread. "By midsummer, I'd say. On the river road. With a full load."

Hollis stared at him.

"Senku said that," said Hollis slowly. "Last week."

"Did he?" Byakuya looked down the table at his son, delighted. "He's right. Course he's right." He bit the pear bread. "I'll fix it after breakfast. I fixed every axle in my village for forty years. Wood and iron and nothing else." He chewed. "This bread is incredible. Who made this bread?"

He fixed the axle after breakfast. He fixed the latch on his door, which he did not like. He fixed the hinge on the fort's curtain rod, which had been sagging since the winter and which Umaru had filed four complaints about to the Ministry, all of them to herself. He looked at the water barrels and said something about the runoff from the bark shingles that made Rimuru go very quiet and then say *oh*. He did not make a fuss about any of it. He just did it, and moved on, and laughed at something, and did the next thing.

Nothing revolved around him. He did not seem to want anything to. He was simply there. The way Seiko was there on her high seat. The way the ink tree was there in the yard.

And by the end of the second day, it was hard to remember the house without him.



He found Senku and Wakana in the lab on the second afternoon, making glass.

They were trying for chimneys again. Hollis wanted twenty for the next run. Senku and Wakana had made thirty tries since the morning, and nineteen of them were on the floor of the lab in pieces, swept into a corner. Wakana was red-faced and furious with concentration. Senku was saying *ten billion percent* to each new failure in a voice that was getting less and less convincing.

Byakuya stood in the doorway and watched them for a while without saying anything.

Then he said, "They're cracking when they cool."

Senku did not look round. "I know."

"Too fast. You take them out of the heat and put them on the bench and they cool from the outside in, and the outside shrinks before the inside's ready, and—" Byakuya made a small cracking noise with his tongue.

"I *know*," said Senku. "It's called annealing. You cool them slowly. In an oven that holds the heat and lets it go over hours. I know about annealing." He picked up a pair of tongs. "I knew about annealing when I was six. You told me about it when I was six."

"I know you know," said Byakuya. He came into the lab. "You haven't got an oven that holds heat."

Senku stopped.

"You've got a kiln," said Byakuya. "A good one. Hot. But it doesn't hold. It goes up and it comes down. You need a second chamber. Next to it. Fed off the same heat, but thick-walled. Brick and clay. So it holds, and lets go, over a night." He looked at the nineteen pieces of broken glass in the corner. "You'd lose one in ten, not two in three."

Senku did not say anything for a long moment.

"I know," he said at last. Very quietly. "I'd worked that out. I was going to build it." He put the tongs down. "Next month. When there was time."

"There's time now," said Byakuya. "I'm here." He rolled up the sleeves of his jumpsuit. "Show me where you get your clay."

Senku looked at his father.

At the big man in the white jumpsuit with patches on the shoulders, with his sleeves rolled up and his impossible hair, standing in the doorway of the only room in the house that had ever felt like Senku's own. Offering. Not taking over. Offering.

"The riverbank," said Senku. "By the weir."

"Good," said Byakuya. "Let's go."

Wakana, at the bench, was looking from one of them to the other with his mouth slightly open.

"You told him about annealing," he said to Byakuya, "when he was *six*?"

"Five and a half," said Byakuya. "He'd broken a glass. He wanted to know why it broke the way it did." He laughed. "He asked me forty questions. I answered thirty-nine. He was furious about the fortieth."

"What was the fortieth?" said Wakana.

Byakuya looked at his son.

"I don't remember," he said. "He does."

Senku was already walking out of the lab, toward the riverbank, with his ears red.

"*Why is glass a liquid if it doesn't move*," he said over his shoulder. "And it's not a liquid. That's a myth. You said you'd look it up. You never looked it up."

"I was in space!"

"You were in space for *six months*. I was five and a *half*."



They built the second chamber in three days.

Byakuya and Senku and Wakana, with Rimuru advising and Momo bringing soup out to the riverbank at noon and the fort, in hoods, carrying clay in buckets because the fort carried things now that the fort had seen honey carried. Byakuya laughed the whole time. Senku did not laugh at all. But on the evening of the third day, when the chamber was finished and the first batch of chimneys went into it to cool overnight, Senku stood beside his father in front of it for a long time, not saying anything, with their shoulders almost touching.

In the morning, eighteen of the twenty were whole.

Wakana took them out one at a time and held each one up to the light and did not say anything at all. He lined them up on the bench. Eighteen. Clear and whole and only very slightly lopsided.

Then he sat down on the floor of the lab and cried.

Byakuya looked at him. Then at Senku.

"Is he all right?" he said.

"He broke twenty-nine," said Senku. "The first time. Out of forty." He was looking at the eighteen chimneys on the bench. "He held every one like an egg."

Byakuya looked at Wakana on the floor for a moment. Then he sat down on the floor of the lab beside him, a big man in a white jumpsuit, and did not say anything, and patted his shoulder, once, awkwardly, the way fathers do.

Wakana cried harder. But he was laughing too.



Suika found him on the third evening, under the ink tree.

He was sitting on the bench there, alone, with his big hands on his knees, looking up at the dark leaves. Everyone else was at dinner. He had eaten, very fast, and talked to everyone, and laughed, and then he had got up and gone out to the yard by himself.

Suika stood in the back doorway for a long time, watching him.

Then she came out and stood in front of the bench. She had her glasses on. She was holding something under one arm. Round. Green and dark green, in stripes.

Byakuya looked at her.

He had seen her, of course. Every day. The small girl in glasses who sat in the corner of the lab, and went everywhere with Senku, and talked about herself in the third person. He had said hello to her. She had said hello back. That was all.

"You're the one from the village," said Byakuya.

Suika nodded.

"Senku told me," said Byakuya. "This morning. While we were mixing clay." He looked at her, very carefully. "He said you're from my village. From Ishigami village. He said—" He stopped. "He said everybody's great-great-great-grandfather was in the stories."

"Suika's too," said Suika.

Byakuya did not say anything.

"The hundred tales," said Suika. "Everybody in the village knew them. The old ones told the little ones. Every winter. By the fire." She held the watermelon a little tighter. "Suika didn't know they were real. Suika thought they were just stories. About a sky-god and his friends who came down from the stars and made the village out of nothing. And then Senku came. And Ruri told the last one. And it was a man, talking to his son." She looked at him through her glasses. "It was you."

Byakuya's face had gone very still.

"Three thousand seven hundred years," he said. Very quietly. "I made those up for the kids. Lillian's kids. Yakov's. To keep them busy in the winter. So they'd remember how to do things. Fire. Salt. Soap." He looked down at his big hands. "So they'd remember Senku was coming." He shook his head. "I didn't think they'd last. Not that long. Not three thousand—"

"They lasted," said Suika.

Byakuya looked at her.

"Will you tell me one?" he said. "How they tell it now. In the village. After all that time." His voice was not loud at all. "I'd like to hear what they turned into."

Suika thought about it.

Then she sat down on the bench beside him, with the watermelon in her lap, and told him the first tale. The way her grandmother had told it to her, by the fire, every winter. In her own voice, in the third person, because that was how Suika told everything. About the sky-god who fell to earth, and made fire with two sticks, and taught the first children how.

It was not the way he had told it. It had changed. Over three thousand seven hundred years, a lot had changed. The sky-god had grown a beard. The two sticks had become magic sticks. The children had become twelve brothers. There was a talking bird in it now that Byakuya had definitely never put in it.

He listened to the whole thing without interrupting once.

When she had finished, he sat on the bench under the ink tree with his hands over his face, and his shoulders shaking.

Suika looked at him, alarmed.

"Is Suika's version wrong?"

"No," said Byakuya. Through his hands. He was laughing. And crying. Both. "No. No, it's perfect. It's *perfect*." He took his hands away from his face. "The *bird*. Where did the *bird* come from?"

"The bird was always there," said Suika, offended.

"It was *not* always there," said Byakuya. "I promise you. There was no bird." He wiped his eyes. "Somebody added the bird. Some grandmother, a thousand years ago, who thought it needed a bird." He laughed again. "She was right. It's better with the bird."

Suika considered this. Then she smiled.

They sat there for a while.

"Suika," said Byakuya, after a long time.

"Mm."

"There were a hundred," said Byakuya. "Tales. I made a hundred. The last one was the message. For Senku." He looked up at the dark leaves of the ink tree. "But I never made a hundred-and-first."

Suika looked at him.

"Would you like one?" said Byakuya. "A new one? Nobody's ever heard it." He paused. "You could be the first. You could take it back to the village one day, if there's ever a way. Or tell it to the fort. Or just keep it."

Suika held the watermelon very tightly.

"Yes," she said. "Suika would like that."

Byakuya leaned back on the bench. He looked up at the ink tree, and through it, at the first stars coming out over the yard. Strange stars. Not his. Not one of them his.

"Once upon a time," said Byakuya, "there was a boy with very silly hair."

Suika giggled.

"And he went to sleep," said Byakuya. "For a very long time. Longer than anybody had ever slept. And while he was asleep, the whole world turned to stone." He paused. "And his father, who was very old by then, and very tired, made up a hundred stories for some children by a fire. So they'd remember how to make soap. And so they'd remember the boy was coming." He smiled at the stars. "And then the father died. And he never knew if the boy woke up."

Suika had gone very still.

"But the boy woke up," said Byakuya. "Of course he did. He was the cleverest boy in the world, and very stubborn, and he had silly hair. He woke up, and he brought everyone back, one at a time, with science and soap and stubbornness." His voice went soft. "And then, one day, in another world, he held a coin in his hand and thought about his father. And said ten billion percent."

He was quiet for a moment.

"And the father came," said Byakuya. "And the first thing he saw was the boy. Grown. Standing in a doorway. With his hands in his pockets. Pretending he hadn't been crying." He laughed, softly. "And the boy said it had never been irrelevant. And the father said, *you're answering*. And then they built an oven together, and cooled some glass, and a boy who'd broken twenty-nine of them sat on the floor and cried."

Suika was crying too. Quietly. Behind her glasses.

"And a girl from the village," said Byakuya, "who'd known the hundred stories her whole life, sat under a tree with the father, and told him the first one. With a bird in it." He looked at her. "And he liked the bird."

He stopped.

"Is that the end?" said Suika, after a while.

"No," said Byakuya. He looked back up at the stars. "That's the thing about the hundred-and-first. It doesn't end. Not yet. We're still in it." He paused. "You'll have to tell me how it ends. When you know."

Suika sat beside him on the bench under the ink tree with the watermelon in her lap and her glasses wet, and did not say anything for a long time.

"Suika will remember it," she said at last. "All of it. Suika is very good at remembering stories."

"I know," said Byakuya. "It runs in the family."



Senku found them there an hour later, when he came out to look for his father.

They were both still on the bench. Byakuya was pointing up at the sky, at a cluster of strange stars over the west wing, and saying something about it. Suika was holding the watermelon and listening very seriously.

Senku stopped in the back doorway.

"I don't know a single one," Byakuya was saying. "Not one. I spent my whole life looking at stars. On the ground, in the village, every night for forty years. I knew every one. I could navigate by them. I named a few." He shook his head at the sky. "And I don't know a single one of these."

"Senku can build a telescope," said Suika. "With lenses. Senku makes good lenses."

"Does he?" Byakuya turned round on the bench, and saw his son in the doorway. "Do you?"

Senku came out into the yard. He stood in front of the bench, with his hands in his pockets, and looked up at the strange stars too.

"Ten billion percent," he said. "First thing."

"Second thing," said Byakuya. "First thing, you're going to make twenty chimneys for that nice man with the axle, so his cart doesn't go down to the river towns empty." He grinned. "Then a telescope."

Senku looked at him.

Then, very slightly, so slightly that only Suika saw it, he smiled.

"Then we name them," said Senku. "All of them. Every star in this sky."

Byakuya looked up at his son. At the hair. At the hands in the pockets. At the face, which was trying very hard not to do anything, and failing.

"Yeah," said Byakuya. "Then we name them."
