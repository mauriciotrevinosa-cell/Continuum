## Chapter 22 — Okarun's Coin

*When: Days 210 and 211. Hollis took eighteen chimneys down the river road on Day 209, and the clerk pointed, and they sold before the clerk had finished his sentence. Byakuya and Senku have started grinding a telescope lens. It is hot, properly hot, for the first time; the fort has moved its cushions into the shade of the ink tree and calls it the Summer Annexe.*

Okarun had been thinking about it for a month.

He did not tell anyone. He did not tell Momo. He did not tell Turbo Granny, who lived on his shoulder and on his windowsill and in his pocket and knew everything he thought before he thought it anyway, and who had therefore known for a month and said nothing, which was the most suspicious thing she had ever done.

It had started on the dating day. On the bell tower. With the stars, and Momo beside him on the platform, and Vamola guarding the ladder, and Turbo Granny supervising from his shoulder. Momo had been looking at the stars. And she had said, out of nowhere, very quietly, the way she said things she had been thinking about for a long time:

"Jiji would have liked this."

Okarun had not said anything.

"He loved the stars," said Momo. "When we were little. Before he moved away. He'd lie on the roof of his house and point at them and make up names. All wrong." She smiled at the sky. "He's still there. In our world. With his family. With—" She stopped. "With the thing in him."

"The Evil Eye," said Okarun.

"It comes out when he's cold," said Momo. "You know. We both know. You throw hot water on him and it goes back down." She hugged her knees. "Who's going to throw hot water on him now? With us gone?"

Okarun had not answered that. He had not known how.

He had thought about it every day since.



He went to Fern on the hundred-and-tenth morning. No. The two-hundred-and-tenth. He had lost count of the days, which Fern never did, and which she pointed out, at once, gently, when he said the wrong number.

He sat down on the bench across from her with the coin in his fist.

"I want to use mine," said Okarun.

Fern looked at him.

"For Jiji," said Okarun. He was looking at the table. "Jin Enjoji. Momo's—" He stopped. "Momo's oldest friend."

Fern did not say anything for a moment.

"Momo has a coin," she said.

"I know."

"She's had it since the bridge. She hasn't used it."

"I know," said Okarun. "That's why." He turned the coin over in his fist. "She should keep hers. For someone else. Someone she needs. Later. When she knows." He swallowed. "And Jiji's alone. With the—with the thing in him. And nobody to throw hot water on him when it gets cold. And she thinks about it. On the bell tower. When she looks at stars." He did not look up. "So I'll use mine. For her. So she doesn't have to use hers."

Fern looked at him for a long moment.

Then she opened the ledger to the page of the List, and wrote, in her small upright hand, on a fresh line:

*Okarun: Jiji. (For Momo.)*

She looked at it.

Then she crossed out *(For Momo)*, very neatly, with one line.

Okarun stared at it.

"Why did you do that?" he said.

"Because the coin doesn't carry *for* anyone," said Fern. She closed the ledger. "It only carries if you love the person. And they love you back. Both." She looked at him. "You know that. Everyone knows that. Nijika. Rem. Maki. It goes cold otherwise." She paused. "So it can't be for Momo. It can only be for you."

Okarun opened his mouth.

He closed it.

"He's my *rival*," he said.

"I know," said Fern.

"He's liked her since they were *six*. He still—he used to call me—he tried to—" Okarun's voice was rising. "We *fought*. Properly. He's my *rival*, Fern."

"Then it'll go cold," said Fern calmly, "and you'll still have your coin."

Okarun did not say anything for a long time.

"And if it doesn't go cold?" he said at last. Very quietly.

Fern looked at him over the closed ledger. Her violet eyes were very steady.

"Then you'll know something," she said.



"It's going to work," said Turbo Granny.

It was night. Okarun was sitting on the floor of his room under the window, with his back against the wall and the coin in his hand. Turbo Granny was on the windowsill above him, which was her separate address, in her lucky-cat body, with her paws folded, looking down at him with deep satisfaction.

"You don't know that," said Okarun.

"I know it," said Turbo Granny. "I've lived in your body. I've been in your *pocket*. I know exactly what you think about that boy." She cackled. "It's disgusting. It's very sweet. It's going to work."

"He's my *rival*."

"He's your rival," Turbo Granny agreed. "And you fought him. And then you fought *with* him, against the thing in him, and you threw hot water on him, and he called you his best friend, and you said *shut up*." She settled herself more comfortably on the sill. "And you've thought about him every day for a month. Every day. I counted. You've been very boring to live in."

Okarun put his face in his hands.

"If it works," he said, through them, "it means—"

"I know what it means," said Turbo Granny.

"It means I—"

"*I know what it means,*" said Turbo Granny. "Hold the stupid coin."

He held the coin.

He closed his eyes, and he thought about Jiji. Not about Momo. Not about the bell tower. Just Jiji. The big stupid grin. The way he called Okarun *Okarun* too, because Momo did, like it was a joke they were all in on. The fight. The hot water and the steam and the Evil Eye going back down, and Jiji sitting on the wet floor afterward with his head in his hands, saying *thank you*, saying *I'm sorry*, saying *you're my best friend, you know that, right*. And Okarun saying *shut up*. And meaning *yes*.

The voice came.

*You are sure?*

*Jin Enjoji.*

Okarun did not open his eyes.

"Yeah," he said. Very quietly. "Yeah. I'm sure."

The coin went warm.

Then warmer.

Then so warm that he gasped, and opened his hand.

There was nothing there. His palm was pink. The coin was gone.

It had worked.

Okarun sat on the floor of his room and stared at his empty pink palm for a very long time.

On the windowsill above him, Turbo Granny made a sound. It started as a cackle. It got louder. It went on for a long time. It was the sound of a very old yokai who had lived in a tunnel and a lucky cat and a high school boy, and had seen a great many things, and had never in her long life been as delighted as she was at that moment.

"*It means you love him,*" she said, through it.

Okarun lay down on the floor, face down, and put the pillow over his head.

"*You LOVE him,*" said Turbo Granny. "*Your RIVAL. You love your RIVAL. The Goddess KNOWS. It's OFFICIAL. It's in FERN'S LEDGER*—"

"*Shut UP*," said Okarun, into the floor.



Jiji came at the eighth bell.

Arrival House. The intake hall. The register girl at her desk, Jinshi at the door with the key, and half the house in the plaza not looking as if it were waiting. Okarun was not in the plaza. He was standing in the doorway of the intake hall, just inside, with his hands in his pockets and his face bright red, the way Senku had stood there a week before. Momo was beside him.

He had told her at breakfast. He had not wanted to. He had sat down next to her at the long table with his porridge, and not eaten it, and Momo had looked at him, and looked at Turbo Granny on his shoulder, who was making a small continuous sound like a kettle, and said, "What did you do."

And he had told her.

She had not said anything for a long time. She had looked at him with her mouth a little open. And then, very slowly, she had put her spoon down, and taken his face in both her hands, and looked at him, very close.

"You used *your* coin," said Momo. "For *Jiji*."

"You should keep yours," said Okarun. "For someone else. Later."

"For *Jiji*," said Momo again.

"It worked," said Okarun miserably. "It went. It means—"

"I know what it means," said Momo.

And then she had kissed him. At the long table. In front of everyone. Which made the fort explode behind its curtain, and Seiko lift her fan very slowly in front of her face, and Turbo Granny scream with joy.

Now Momo stood beside him in the doorway of the intake hall, holding his hand, very tight.

At the eighth bell, the air in the middle of the hall moved.

And there was a boy standing on the boards.

Tall. Taller than Okarun by a head. Broad. With messy dark hair and a big open face and a wide, easy, slightly stupid grin, which was the first thing he did, before he had even looked round. He grinned. As if he had been about to say something funny, and had been interrupted, and was still going to say it.

Then he looked round.

The grin went away, slowly. He looked at the high windows. At the clay walls. At the register girl. At Jinshi.

"Uh," said Jiji. "Where am I?"

Then he saw the doorway.

He saw Momo.

His face did something. All of it at once. His mouth opened. His eyes went very wide. He took one step forward, and stopped, as if the floor had gone soft.

"*Momo*?" he said.

Momo let go of Okarun's hand.

She crossed the intake hall at a run and threw herself at him, and he caught her, the way he must have caught her a hundred times when they were small, and lifted her off her feet, and held on. And she was laughing and crying and hitting him on the shoulder, and he was saying *Momo, Momo, how are you here, where is here, Momo*, over and over, into her hair.

Okarun stood in the doorway.

He did not go in. He stood there with his hands in his pockets and his face red and watched them. And something in his chest that he had been holding very tightly for a month let go, a little.

Then Jiji looked up. Over Momo's shoulder. At the doorway.

And saw him.

"*Okarun?*" said Jiji.

Okarun did not say anything.

Jiji put Momo down. Very slowly. He looked at Okarun in the doorway. At the red face. At the hands in the pockets. He looked at Momo beside him. At the plaza beyond the door, full of strange people not looking as if they were waiting. At the intake hall.

"Wait," said Jiji. "Wait, wait, wait." He held up one hand. "How did I get here? Somebody—Momo, did you—"

"No," said Momo.

"Then who—"

From the step outside, Fern, with the ledger under her arm, said clearly:

"Okarun's coin."

There was a silence in the intake hall.

Jiji looked at Fern. Then at Okarun.

Okarun looked at the ceiling.

"Okarun's coin," said Jiji slowly.

"There are coins," said Momo quickly. "Everyone has one. You can use it to bring someone. From home. Someone you—" She stopped. She went pink.

"Someone you what?" said Jiji.

Momo did not answer.

"It only works," said Fern, from the step, in exactly the same clear calm voice, "if you love the person. And they love you back."

The silence in the intake hall was absolute.

Jiji turned his head, very slowly, and looked at Okarun.

A grin was spreading across his face. Slowly. From one side to the other. The biggest grin Okarun had ever seen on anyone, including Gojo. A grin of total, overwhelming, unrepentant delight.

"Your coin," said Jiji. "You used *your* coin. On *me*."

"Shut up," said Okarun.

"It only works if you *love* the person—"

"*Shut UP*—"

"Okarun," said Jiji, crossing the intake hall with his arms out. "*Okarun*. My *best friend*. My *rival*. You *love* me—"

"I *will* hit you—"

Jiji grabbed him. Both arms. A bear hug, enormous, lifting him clean off the floor of the intake hall, while Okarun struggled and kicked and said *put me DOWN* and *I hate you* and *this is the worst day of my life*. And Momo was laughing so hard she had to hold on to the register girl's desk. And on Okarun's shoulder, squashed between the two of them, Turbo Granny was shrieking with joy.

"*The coin WORKED*," Jiji was shouting. "*It WORKED, Okarun, the Goddess KNOWS*—"

"*I KNOW SHE KNOWS*—"

Out in the plaza, the fort, which had been watching through the door, made the kettle sound. *EEEEEEEEEE.*



They kept him warm all day.

Nobody else understood why. Okarun and Momo did not explain. They just did it. They put a blanket round Jiji's shoulders the moment he came out of Arrival House, in the summer sun, and he looked at it, puzzled, and said *it's hot, though*, and they said *keep it on*. Momo brought him hot tea at breakfast. Hot soup at lunch. Hot tea again in the afternoon. Okarun walked beside him everywhere, between him and the river, between him and the water barrels, between him and the well. When Jiji went to wash his hands at the trough in the yard, Okarun intercepted him and brought him a basin of water that Momo had heated on the stove.

"It's *summer*," said Jiji, sweating under the blanket. "Why is everyone—"

"Just keep it on," said Momo.

"I'm going to *melt*."

"Better than the other thing," said Okarun.

Jiji looked at him. Then, slowly, his face changed. The grin went away.

"The cold," he said quietly. "You're worried about the cold." He looked down at his own hands. "The thing. In me. You're worried it'll—"

"We don't know what it does here," said Momo. "Nobody does. We don't know if it's different. We don't know if it's worse." She pulled the blanket tighter round his shoulders. "So we're keeping you warm. Until we know."

Jiji did not say anything for a moment.

He looked at Momo, fussing with the blanket. At Okarun, standing beside him between him and the well, with his jaw set, sweating in the sun too, because he had been walking beside Jiji all day in his coat.

"You'd throw hot water on me," said Jiji. "If it came out."

"Obviously," said Okarun.

"Even here."

"*Especially* here," said Okarun. "I didn't use my coin to bring you here so a thing in your eye could eat the house."

Jiji looked at him for a long moment.

Then he put his big arm round Okarun's shoulders, under the blanket, so that both of them were under it. In the summer sun. Sweating.

"Best friend," said Jiji.

"Shut up," said Okarun.

But he did not move out from under the blanket.
