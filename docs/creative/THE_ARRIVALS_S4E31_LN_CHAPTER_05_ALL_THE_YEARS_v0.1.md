*When: Day 212, the morning after the soup, then that afternoon. Everyone who has come by coin so far is here. Market Day is still two days away.*

Frieren had decided to be helpful.

That was how she described it when Mau asked why she had been looking at him all through breakfast.

"You missed a bit," she said.

He looked down at his bread.

"Here."

She touched the corner of his mouth with her thumb. There was a crumb on it. A very small crumb. It did not require the time she took removing it.

Across the table, Gojo became interested in his cup. Shoko became interested in the remaining half of her bread. They had been sitting close enough for their elbows to meet since breakfast began, and had been very careful about each other's elbows.

"Thank you," said Mau.

"You're welcome."

Frieren kept looking at him.

"What?"

"You look nice when you don't know what to say."

Gojo put down his cup before anything unfortunate happened to it.

Mau looked at him, then back at Frieren.

"Could you say that a little quieter?"

"You said I should tell you things."

"I did."

"I'm telling you."

Her face was perfectly serious. Her ears were faintly pink, which made it worse. She had used that same face while saying something about his eyes in front of the fort, and had been very indignant afterward that people had heard her. He was beginning to suspect she remembered.

"Why are you doing this to me?"

"It's your fault."

"How?"

"You keep doing it to me."

"Saying you look nice?"

"Making me forget where people are."

Shoko reached for her cup. Gojo reached for the same cup. Both withdrew their hands. Neither looked at the other.

Mau, whose face had gone warm enough without an audience, tried to recover some dignity.

"You should be more respectful," he said. "I'm older than you."

Frieren's eyes narrowed.

"You used that argument in a dungeon. It wasn't useful then either."

"You're right. Older than Milim, too."

"Mau."

He grinned. She caught his hand under the table, firmly, which was much nicer than the warning in her voice.

Gojo looked up.

"Wait. How are you older than her?"

"He's taller, Satoru. Duh," said Shoko, still looking at her bread. She had caught the comparison and missed the word that mattered.

"I said *older*."

"Oh."

"Because of the Trial," said Mau.

He said it without thinking, still looking at Frieren.

"What about the Trial?" Gojo asked.

"The four hundred and thirty-seven times. All the time in them. And then the coins—what the Goddess said they were for."

Frieren's hand stopped moving against his.

Shoko set down her bread.

Mau looked across the table. It took him a moment to understand the two faces looking back.

"You know the number," he said to Gojo.

"I know the number. You've told me a little about one of the things in there." Gojo's voice was quiet now. "You didn't tell me about the coins."

"Four hundred and thirty-seven *what*?" said Shoko.

Mau opened his mouth. Closed it.

He had been talking as if they had sat on the floor of the common room that night. As if they had waited at the white wall, and heard him afterward, and known ever since. People became part of the house, and he forgot there were things the house knew before they came.

"I thought—" He stopped. "Sorry. I thought you knew."

Shoko glanced at Gojo. This time he met her eyes.

"He asked the house not to pass it on," said Frieren. "What happened inside."

"You don't have to tell us because you started a sentence," said Shoko.

Mau looked down at his plate. At the bread, at the little place where he had torn it badly. Frieren loosened her grip, and left her hand open under his.

He thought for a while.

"I know," he said. "But I think I want to."

Gojo waited.

"Not just you two. Seika. Ram. Yuka. The others who came after." Mau looked toward the kitchen, where someone was laughing about the soup. "They're living here with us. I keep expecting them to understand things I've asked nobody to explain."

"Today?" said Frieren.

"This afternoon. If they want to hear it."

"How much?"

He looked at her then.

"Enough that they know what I mean. I don't want to go through every room again."

"All right."

She lifted their hands above the edge of the table, and kissed his knuckles, once. He went red again, instantly.

"And that," he said.

"Your fault," said Frieren.

Nobody laughed about the number. But Shoko smiled at the way he looked at the elf, and Gojo took his own cup this time.



They met after the afternoon work had stopped.

Fern asked people quietly, one at a time. Mau wanted to explain something about the Trial. They could come. He was choosing to tell it. Nobody needed to give an answer at the door.

Seika came with Nijika. Ram sat beside Rem, who put the teapot down and sat too. Yuka took the place between her grandparents and kept her hands away from the cord at her neck. Gojo and Shoko sat opposite Mau again. Byakuya came from the workroom with Senku, wiping his hands on a cloth. Coco brought her mother, then sat beside her with their sleeves touching. Jiji followed Okarun and Momo in, without his blanket.

Around them, the people who had been there before made room. Bocchi shifted nearer Kita so Seika and Nijika could both sit. Milim settled beside Rimuru. Ori brought her stool to Mau's end of the bench and did not ask what he was going to say.

Frieren sat on his other side.

He asked Ciel to watch the guardrails and tell him if he needed to stop. She answered him privately. She did not go looking through the memories to help him tell it.

Fern put her closed ledger on the table.

"Do you want this written down?"

"No."

She left it closed.

Mau looked at the people who had arrived by coin. All of them here, in this room, alive in ways their own worlds had not always allowed them to be. None of them had asked him to go into the white for them.

"Some of you know there was a Trial," he said. "Seika knows the coins came out of it. Gojo knows the number. You've heard different pieces. I'm going to tell you what those pieces mean."

He put his hands flat on his knees.

"Four hundred and thirty-seven is how many times I died."

The room stayed still.

"Every time," said Mau. "Then it started again. Ciel could get a number through at the beginning. Just one. She didn't know what had happened. Outside, they couldn't see anything."

He looked toward Senku.

"Five hours and forty-one minutes," said Senku quietly. "For us."

Byakuya turned toward his son. Senku kept looking at Mau.

"It wasn't that long for me," said Mau. "It was much longer. Some ended quickly. Some had winters. Years. I got old in some of them. In some, the elf route was open, and there was time I don't know how to count."

Frieren's thumb moved once against his hand.

"That's what I meant at breakfast. Ciel tried to work out the time from what I remembered. The small estimate was about a hundred years more than Frieren. Rimuru told me the longer one made me older than Milim. They couldn't give me an exact total."

"Time you lived through," said Shoko.

"Yes. It doesn't tell us what I am. Or how long I get here."

Shoko nodded. She did not turn it into a question about his body.

Mau breathed in, slowly.

"I kept trying to find a way where the people in this house stayed alive. Standing in front. Running. Staying. Leaving. Asking. It used places I knew. Faces I knew. Sometimes things I didn't know at all. I couldn't look at you out here and check which was real. I didn't know how to get out."

He stopped, and looked at the common-room window. The leaves of the ink tree were moving. He could hear a plate being rinsed in the kitchen.

Frieren waited with him. Nobody tried to finish the sentence.

"When I came out, there was a way to go back to our worlds," he said at last. "I thought you'd all be safer if you took it. I went away because I couldn't see how you could stay near me and be safe. The house came after me. We talked. We stayed."

Rem's hand lay beside Ram's on the table. Ram turned hers over and took it.

"You don't remember all of it?" said Byakuya.

"No. I remember enough to know it happened. Some comes back. Sometimes I wish it didn't."

Byakuya nodded once. He did not ask him to try harder.

"I wasn't doing it to get the coins," said Mau. "I didn't know there would be coins. After the return window closed, the Goddess gave one petition to each person who stayed. She said it had been purchased by the one who stood in the Trial. Four hundred and thirty-seven times. That's when I found out."

Yuka looked at Gojo, then at Shoko. At the people at the table who could not have been at it without a small warm thing in someone else's palm.

"So that's why we're here," she said.

"It's why the coins exist," said Mau. "Yuta wanted his teacher. Seiko gave him hers. Maki wanted you. Nijika wanted her sister. You all had people who wanted you here. That part belongs to them."

Gojo looked down at the table. Shoko's hand was beside his. He put his fingers against hers without hiding it.

"I didn't ask them to keep it quiet because I wanted something later," said Mau. "I didn't want you to look at me and see a bill. Or think coming here was something you had done to me. You didn't know me. Some of you weren't even names I knew."

"Nijika stopped when I asked," said Seika.

She was looking at her sister. Nijika nodded.

"Because I asked her to," said Mau. "She didn't lie to you."

"I know." Seika turned back to him. "And I don't owe you my life."

"Good."

"Neither does she," said Seika, with her hand on Nijika's sleeve. "Neither does anyone in here."

Mau's shoulders came down a little.

"That's what I wanted," he said.

"I'll still tell you when you're being an idiot."

"You were doing that anyway."

"Good. Saves us changing the arrangement."

Nijika's face moved. She pressed her lips together, then gave up and leaned against her sister.

Coco's mother had been looking at Mau with her hands folded in her lap. Now she reached across the small gap to his cup and moved it nearer him. He had forgotten it was there.

"Thank you for telling us," she said.

He took a drink. The tea was still warm.

"Is it still happening?" asked Jiji.

It came out much quieter than his usual voice.

"The Trial? No."

"But you remember."

"Yes."

Jiji looked at Okarun for a moment. Okarun stayed beside him, his elbows on the table.

"Okay," said Jiji. "I wanted to know which one."

"Some things in me learned from it," said Mau. "That didn't stop when I came out. The guardrails are still there. We still ask. We still stop when someone says stop. Telling you this doesn't make any of it safe to try."

Rimuru nodded. He did not promise more than that.

"And it doesn't mean you have to tell us every part," said Shoko.

"I can't tell you every part."

"You also get to keep some."

Mau looked at her.

"This much can be told," he said, after a moment. "What I've said here. If somebody asks why I sometimes need a minute, you don't have to pretend you don't understand. But the things I haven't told you are still mine."

Gojo nodded first. Then Seika. Then the others, one at a time.

"If someone new comes later," said Fern, "we ask you before explaining more."

"Yes."

There was no new rule in the ledger. She did not need to turn his permission into a public record of what had hurt him.

For a little while they sat without asking anything.

Yuka put her hands in her lap. Ram kept holding Rem's. Byakuya leaned back on the bench, his shoulder against Senku's, and looked at the open window. Milim watched Mau with her chin on her knees; she had heard the first telling and had never liked what the years meant.

Then Shoko picked up the teapot.

"I'm staying," she said, and filled her cup. "You don't need to earn it again."

Mau looked at her. At Gojo beside her, alive. At Seika with Nijika leaning on her. At Coco's mother's hands, able to hold a cup. At Jiji, who had no blanket today.

"I'm glad," he said.



They did not all go away at once.

Byakuya asked Senku about the work still drying in the afternoon sun. Jiji helped Momo carry the empty plates. Coco's mother went back to the window with her daughter. Seika and Nijika stayed talking with the band. The room found ordinary things to do around the account he had given it.

Mau stayed on the bench with Frieren.

"Still my fault?" he asked.

She looked at him. Then she put her head against his shoulder.

"The blushing," she said.

He smiled, a little.

"You could do it again."

"I will."

Across the table, Gojo reached for the last piece of bread. Shoko took it before he could. He looked at her, offended, and she broke it in two and gave him the smaller half.

"Respect your elders," said Mau, very quietly, to Frieren.

Her fingers tightened round his.

"Eat your bread."

He did.
