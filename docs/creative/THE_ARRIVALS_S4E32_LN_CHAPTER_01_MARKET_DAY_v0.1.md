*When: Day 214, the fourth run to Ashby. Jiji has stopped wearing the blanket and started following Okarun everywhere, which Okarun says is worse. Byakuya and Senku have a telescope lens half ground. Coco's mother has learned the names of everyone at the long table and uses them. It is midsummer, hot and still.*

The gatekeeper at Ashby did not let them in.

He stood in the middle of the gate with a halberd across his chest and a paper in his other hand, and he did not look at Hollis. He looked at a place just over Hollis's shoulder, the way people look when they are about to do something they know is wrong and want very badly not to have to see the face of the person they are doing it to.

"Reeve's order," said the gatekeeper. He held up the paper. "Posted this morning. No goods that can't be traced to their maker. No carts carrying them through this gate." He swallowed. "Market or no market."

Hollis looked at the paper.

It had a seal at the bottom. Red wax. The reeve's. And above it, in a clerk's neat hand, with ink on the cuffs of whoever had written it, the words. *Untraceable goods.* *For the safety of the town.* *By order.*

"Traced," said Hollis. Cheerfully. "They're traced. Old woman on the coast road. Makes the cream in her kitchen. Her brother does the glass. I've told everyone."

"Show me the coast road," said the gatekeeper. Still not looking at him. "Show me her kitchen."

Hollis did not say anything.

"Pell says," said the gatekeeper, very quietly, "there's no old woman. There's no coast road. There's nobody making glass on the coast. He sent a man to look." He lowered the paper. "He says it's coming from the hills. From the monster town. He says you're carrying for them." He did not look up. "I'm sorry, Hollis. I've known you twenty years. I'm sorry. But I've got a family."

Behind the cart, Brenna had not moved. She stood with her crossbow over her shoulder, very straight, the way she had stood at a lord's gate for twelve years. Beside her, Anko, in her brown coat and her cap, with her hands in her pockets, had gone very still.

Eren was not there. Eren was at the ford with Mikasa and Himmel and Aira. Fern had said: not into the town again. Not after the man in the coat looked at him for a breath. Eren had not argued.

"Then I'll sell outside the gate," said Hollis. "On the road. Nobody's posted anything about the road."

The gatekeeper looked at him at last.

"Don't," he said.

"Why not?"

The gatekeeper did not answer. He looked past Hollis, up the road, toward the town. Toward the square, which you could not see from here, but which you could hear. A voice. Carrying. Reasonable. Sad.

*...neighbours...*

"Because they're coming out," said the gatekeeper. "To see you. He told them you'd be here today. He told them to come and *see*."



They came out of the gate in a crowd.

Not a mob. Not at first. Forty people, perhaps. Fifty. Men and women from the market, in aprons and work clothes, with baskets on their arms that they had brought to market and had nothing in. They came out through the gate past the gatekeeper, who stepped aside and did not stop them, and stood in the road in front of Hollis's cart and looked at it.

At the canvas. At the shape of the crates under it. At the six people round it, with spears and a crossbow and an unpicked soldier's coat.

And in the middle of them, on foot, in his clerk's coat with ink on the cuffs, Master Pell.

He did not climb on anything. He did not shout. He walked out of the gate into the road with the crowd round him, as if he were one of them, and stopped a little way in front of the cart, and looked at Hollis with his pleasant, tired, ordinary face.

"Hollis," he said. Sadly. "I've bought from you for twenty years."

"You have," said Hollis.

"And I trusted you."

"You could still," said Hollis.

Pell shook his head slowly. He turned, very slightly, so that he was speaking to the crowd as much as to Hollis.

"There's no old woman on the coast road," he said. "I sent my cousin's boy. Three days down and three days back. He asked at every farm. There's nobody making cream. There's nobody blowing glass." He looked back at Hollis. "So where does it come from?"

The crowd was very quiet.

Hollis did not say anything.

"I'll tell you where," said Pell. Reasonably. "It comes from the hills. From the place where the light came up. From the place where the grass died." He did not raise his voice. "And you've been carrying it down here, into our town, into our houses, onto our hands. Their cream on our hands. Their glass on our lamps." He shook his head. "And we've been buying it. Because our hands were cracked, and our lamps were broken. And they knew that. They knew we'd buy it."

A murmur went through the crowd. Low.

"What does it do?" said a woman at the front. Frightened. "The cream. What does it *do*?"

"I don't know," said Pell. Gently. "Nobody knows. That's the point. That's why we don't buy what we can't trace."

At the front of the crowd, a woman had her hand over her mouth. Anko knew her. The woman with the lamp chimney, from the first run. She was standing very still in the road, staring at the cart. Her hands were clasped in front of her.

Her hands were soft. Healed. No cracks.

Anko saw her look down at them. As if she had never seen them before.

"It doesn't do anything," said Hollis.

Everyone looked at him.

He had taken his hat off. He was holding it in both hands, in front of him, the way he held it at the long table. His face was not cheerful any more. It was just tired.

"It doesn't do anything," said Hollis, "except heal your hands." He looked at the crowd. At all of them. "That's all it does. You've been using it for three weeks. Look at your hands. Go on. Look at them."

Some of them did. Despite themselves. They looked down.

"And the glass," said Hollis, "doesn't do anything except stop your houses burning down." He looked at the woman with the chimney. "Does it, Merren? Has your house burned down? Since the chimney?"

The woman with the chimney did not say anything. Her face was white.

"So where does it come from?" said Pell.

Hollis looked at him.

He stood in the road in front of his cart with his hat in his hands, and looked at the clerk who had bought from him for twenty years. And Anko, at the back of the cart, saw him decide. She saw it go across his face, quite clearly, the way you see a man decide to step off a ledge.

"From people," said Hollis.

The road went very quiet.

"From people," said Hollis again. "Who make soup. Who pay on time. Who sit at a long table and work out what's real and what isn't." He did not look away from Pell. "A young man who broke twenty-nine chimneys out of forty, and cried over every one, and kept going. A man who came back from the dead to build an oven so she'd stop breaking them. An apothecary who tests her mushrooms on her own arm." He turned his hat round in his hands. "That's where it comes from. From people. Up there. Who knew your hands were cracked and your lamps were broken, and made you something. And never asked for anything back. And let me tell you it was an old woman on the coast road, because they knew you'd never buy it if you knew it was them."

Nobody said anything.

Pell looked at him for a long moment, with his pleasant, tired face.

"So you admit it," he said quietly.

"I admit it," said Hollis.

Pell nodded. Slowly. Sadly. He turned to the crowd.

"You heard him," he said.

Somebody threw a stone.



Anko never saw who. Nobody did. It came out of the back of the crowd, the way stones always come, from nowhere and everyone. A grey stone, the size of a fist. It went over the heads of the people at the front, and over the mule, and hit the side of the cart with a crack like a bone breaking.

The mule screamed.

And then everything happened at once.

The crowd surged. Not at the people. At the cart. Hands on the canvas, pulling it back. Hands on the crates. Somebody had the first crate of cream off the cart and was throwing it down onto the road, and the pots were breaking, white cream spattering the dust. Somebody else had a jar of honey. Somebody was shouting *burn it, burn it, don't touch it, burn it*. Somebody else was shouting *no, no, my hands, it fixed my hands*. The mule was rearing in the shafts. Dunmore had it by the halter and was hauling it down.

"*Back*," Brenna was shouting. "*Back from the cart! BACK!*"

She had stepped in front of it. Between the crowd and the crates. With her crossbow in her hands. Not raised. Held across her body, the way she had held a halberd across a lord's gate. She stood there and did not move, and the crowd broke round her like water round a rock.

"*Don't*," said Brenna. "*Don't. These are your neighbours. Don't.*"

The second stone hit her.

It hit her above the left eye. Anko saw it. A sharp grey stone, thrown hard, from close. Brenna's head snapped back. She went down on one knee in the road. Her crossbow fell out of her hands into the dust.

She got up.

Anko would remember that afterward. More than anything else. Brenna, with blood running down the side of her face from above her eye, getting up off her knee in the road, without her crossbow, and standing in front of the cart again. With her empty hands out. Between the crowd and the crates.

"*Don't*," said Brenna. Her voice was very calm. "*Please. Don't.*"

Cobb was beside her.

He had come round the cart. The farm boy, with his spear. He did not raise it. He stood beside Brenna in front of the cart and held the spear across his body the way she had held the crossbow, and his face was white as paper, and he was shaking so hard the spearhead rattled. And he did not move.

Then Tam was beside him. And Aldric. And Wren, with her bow, small and thin, not drawing it, standing in front of the cart with her bow held across her like a bar.

Six people, in a line, in front of a cart full of cream and honey and glass, with their weapons across their bodies and not raised. Facing a crowd from the town they had come from.

The crowd stopped.

Not all at once. A ripple. The ones at the front saw the six faces. Saw Cobb, who was the Hannet boy's cousin, and had sold eggs in that square every fifth day of his life. Saw Tam. Saw Wren, the miller's granddaughter, the one who never spoke. Saw Brenna, who had held the lord's gate for twelve years and turned away three bandit raids, with blood running down her face and her hands out empty.

"*Brenna*," said someone in the crowd. Shocked. "That's Brenna."

"That's the Hannet boy—"

"What are you *doing*—"

"Standing here," said Cobb. His voice cracked. "That's all. Just standing here."

Nobody threw anything else.



They got the cart turned round. Dunmore did it, with the mule, while the crowd stood in the road and watched and did not stop them. Half the crates were on the road. Broken pots. Smashed honey jars. White cream and gold honey mixed in the dust. Three of the eighteen chimneys were still whole, in their basket. Hollis picked the basket up himself and held it against his chest like an egg.

Pell had not moved. He stood in the road in front of the gate in his clerk's coat, with the crowd round him, watching them go. His face was pleasant, and tired, and very sad.

Anko looked at him as the cart went past.

At the man with ink on his cuffs and soft hands, who read a page someone else had written. She looked at his face. And for one moment, as the cart went by, she saw something in it that was not sadness. It was very small and very quick. It went across his face and was gone.

It was fear.

Not of them. Of something behind him. Something he was looking past them at, up the road, toward the hills.

Anko wrote it down later. *Pell. Afraid. Not of us.*

At the edge of the crowd, the woman with the chimney, Merren, stood with her soft healed hands clasped in front of her. As the cart went past, she looked up at Hollis. Her face was white. Her mouth opened. She did not say anything.

Then, very quickly, as the cart passed her, she put out one hand and touched the side of it. Just for a moment. Her soft hand, on the wood.

And took it back.



They did not talk on the river road. Not for an hour.

At the ford, Mikasa saw the cart coming from a long way off, and saw it was moving wrong, and saw the blood on Brenna's face. She was across the ford before anyone else had moved. Then Eren. Then Himmel. Then Aira, who went straight to Cobb, who was walking beside the cart with his spear and his white face and had not said a word since the gate.

"What happened?" said Mikasa.

Hollis told her. All of it. The gatekeeper. The paper. The crowd. Pell. *From people.* The stones. Brenna getting up. The six in a line.

Mikasa listened without moving. When he had finished, she looked at Brenna, sitting on the riverbank with Anko holding a wet cloth to her eye, and at Cobb, sitting beside her with his spear across his knees, shaking.

"You stood there," said Mikasa to Cobb.

Cobb looked up at her.

"In front of the cart," said Mikasa. "With your spear across. Not raised."

"Brenna did it," said Cobb. His voice was very small. "First. I just—I just did what she did."

Mikasa looked at him for a long moment.

"That's how it starts," she said. "You do what the person in front of you does. And then one day you're the person in front."

Aira, beside Cobb, did not say anything. But she put her hand on his shoulder and left it there.



They came through the low woods at four in the afternoon.

It was there.

Not far. Not a hundred paces off, through the trunks, keeping level. Close. Thirty paces. Twenty. They could see it, between the bare grey trees. A shape. Taller than Himmel. Taller than the last time. Grey. Shapeless. Covered in faces.

And almost all of them were looking.

Not turned away. Not muttering. Looking. Hundreds of small grey faces, all over it, front and back and sides, all turned toward the road. Toward the cart. Toward the six round it and the four from the house and the man in brown at the back. Wide-eyed. Unblinking. Silent.

It did not come out.

It walked beside them, through the trees, twenty paces off, the whole length of the woods. Every step they took, it took. It did not mutter. It did not speak. It just walked, and looked.

Himmel had his sword out. Mikasa had both grey blades out. Aira had the old blade with the nick in it out, and was walking between Cobb and the trees.

Nobody said anything.

At the edge of the woods, where the trees ended and the fields began, it stopped. It stood at the last tree, in the shadow, taller than any of them, with all its faces looking.

And then it spoke.

Not in the many voices. Not in the mutter. In one voice. Clear. Reasonable. Sad. A voice that every one of them who had stood at the gate of Ashby that morning recognised at once.

*Don't buy what you can't trace,* it said.

In Pell's voice.

Then it turned round and went back into the trees.



They told the long table that night.

All of it. The gate. The paper. The crowd. *From people.* The stones. Brenna getting up. The six in a line. The woman who touched the cart. And the woods. The faces, almost all of them looking. And the voice.

Pell's voice. From the devil's mouth.

The long table was very quiet when they had finished.

Maomao had stitched Brenna's eye. Shoko had looked at it after and said Maomao's stitches were better than hers, which made Maomao go pink. Brenna sat at the end of the bench nearest the door with a bandage over her eye and a bowl of soup she had not touched. Cobb sat beside her. He had not eaten either.

Fern had the ledger open. She was writing. She stopped.

"Half the goods," she said. "Lost. Three chimneys sold." She did not look up. "Brenna's eye. The cart damaged." She paused. "And the gate's closed. Ashby won't let him in. Not with our goods." She put down her pen. "So the route's closed."

"It's not closed," said Hollis.

Everyone looked at him.

"There are ten other towns," said Hollis. He sounded very tired. "Ashby's the nearest. Not the only one." He turned his hat round on the table. "Pell's in Ashby. He's not in all of them." He paused. "Not yet."

"Not yet," said Anko quietly.

She had her notebook open. She did not look up from it.

"He was afraid," she said. "Pell. At the end. When the cart went past." She turned a page. "Not of us. Of something behind him. Up the road. Toward the hills." She looked up. "Somebody's writing his pages. And he's afraid of whoever it is."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

At the far end of the table, Eren had not said a word all evening. He sat beside Mikasa, with his hands flat on the table, very still. He had been very still since the ford. Since he saw the blood on Brenna's face, and Cobb's white face, and the six people who had stood in a line.

He was looking at the window. North. Toward the black hills, which you could not see at night.

Mikasa was looking at him.

She did not say anything. But under the table, her hand found his, and held it. And she felt how cold it was. And how it did not move.
