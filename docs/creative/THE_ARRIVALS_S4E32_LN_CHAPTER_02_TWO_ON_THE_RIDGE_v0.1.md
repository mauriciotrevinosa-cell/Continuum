*When: The night of Day 218 into the morning of Day 219. Brenna has a scar coming above her left eye and says it suits her. Hollis has taken the patched cart east, to Fallow, the second town, where Pell does not speak yet. Nazuna has kept the north field for fifty-five nights. Two refuge families are left in the cottages on the north road, with three children.*

Nazuna saw them at one in the morning.

Two shapes, on the north ridge, against the stars. Where the shepherd's track went over. Tall. Upright. Still.

She did not fly out to look this time. She knew what they were. She sat on top of Anko's red stake in the north field with her knees drawn up and looked at them for a long time, and something cold went down the back of her neck that had nothing to do with the night.

*Two*, she thought.

Then she went south, fast, for Anko.



The kitchen was full by two.

It was a different kitchen from the first time. Nobody was surprised. Nobody had to be told what a titan was, or how it moved, or where to cut. They came down the stairs in the dark already dressed, already carrying what they needed, and sat down at the table, and Diablo poured tea, and Fern opened the ledger to a page she had written five weeks ago. *Titans. How.* In her small upright hand. The heel. The knee. The nape. Milim carries. Rimuru's shadow at dawn.

"Two," said Fern.

"Two," said Nazuna, from the counter. "Side by side. On the track. Facing south." She was not eating anything. "One's bigger than the last. One's smaller."

"Smaller's worse," said Eren.

Everyone looked at him.

He was sitting at the end of the table beside Mikasa. Very still. His hands flat on the board. He had come down the stairs before anyone tapped on his door. He had been awake. He did not seem to have slept since Ashby.

"In my world," said Eren. "The small ones. Some of them. They don't walk. They run." He did not look up. "We called them abnormals. They don't do what the others do. They don't go for the nearest person. They go for—" He stopped. "They go for wherever there are the most people. Or the smallest. Nobody ever worked out why. They just run. Straight at it. Faster than you'd think anything that size could run."

The kitchen was quiet.

"Where are the most people?" said Fern. "Outside the line."

Nobody answered.

Then Aira, at the far end, in her big coat, with the old blade with the nick in it across her knees, said quietly: "The cottages."

The two cottages on the north road. Forty paces from where the street ran out. Outside the house. Inside the line. With two families in them, and three children.

"Inside the line," said Rimuru. "The protection—"

"Has never been tested on one of those," said Anko.

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

"Then we don't let it get there," said Mikasa.

"And if it does?" said Aira.

Mikasa looked at her across the table.

"Then you're there," she said.

Aira did not say anything. She looked down at the old blade across her knees. Then she nodded.

"I'll be there too," said Gojo.

He had come down last. He was leaning in the kitchen doorway with his blindfold on and his hands in his pockets and his hair sticking straight up from sleep. Nobody had tapped on his door either.

Fern looked at him.

"Not in front," said Gojo, before she could say it. "Beside." He tilted his blindfolded face toward Aira. "Beside her. At the cottages. If the small one runs, it runs at her. And I'll be beside her." He paused. "I can't kill it. I can barely make a marble. But nothing touches me. And nothing touches anyone standing behind me." He shrugged. "So I'll stand there. Beside the door. And she can cut whatever comes."

Fern looked at him for a long moment.

Then she wrote it down. *Gojo: cottages. Beside Aira. (Not in front.)*

"Eren," said Fern.

Eren looked up.

"The cottages too," said Fern. "Not the field." She did not look away from him. "If the small one runs, the children have to come out of the cottages and into the house. Fast. In the dark. Somebody they know has to bring them. Somebody who knows exactly how fast those things run." She paused. "That's you."

Eren looked at her.

"You don't transform," said Fern.

"I know," said Eren.

"You promised."

"I *know*," said Eren. His voice cracked. "I promised. I'll keep it."

Mikasa's hand was on his, under the table. Holding it. Very tight.



The first light came at ten to six, the way it always did now that it was summer. Gold, over the hills, all at once.

They were in position. Rimuru at the north end with his shadow stretched across the frost-less grass toward the ridge. Anko. Nazuna overhead. Himmel and Stark in the long grass at the foot of the track. Milim and Mikasa behind them. Frieren at the red stake with her staff. Yuta beside her, with the ring, and his cursed energy up, very thin, very steady.

And at the cottages on the north road, forty paces from where the street ran out: Aira, on the step of the bean-girl's cottage, with the old blade out. Gojo beside her, with his hands in his pockets and his blindfold pushed up. And Eren, at the door of the other cottage, with his hand on the latch, and two families inside who had been woken at five and told to dress the children and wait.

The light touched the ridge.

Both titans moved at once.

The big one turned its head, slowly, the way the first one had. Toward the north field. Toward the people in it. It smiled, wider. And began to walk down the track, stiffly, enormous, toward Himmel and Stark in the grass.

The small one did not walk.

It ran.

It came off the ridge all at once, as if something had been let go. Smaller than the other, five metres, perhaps six. Thin. Long-limbed. Its arms swinging. Its head thrown back. Its mouth open wide in a smile that went from ear to ear. And it ran. Not down the track. Not toward the north field. It ran across the slope, sideways, at an angle, past the big one, past Himmel and Stark, past all of them, down the ridge and across the open ground. Faster than anything that size should have been able to run.

Straight at the cottages.

"*Abnormal*—" Eren's voice came up the road from the second cottage, high and cracked. "*It's going for the cottages, it's—*"

"*I see it*," said Mikasa. She was already moving. "*Milim—*"

"*The big one*," said Himmel, from the grass. Very calm. "*Mikasa. The big one's on us. We need you here.*" He was already on his feet, sword out. "*The small one's theirs.*"

Mikasa stopped.

She stood in the north field with both grey blades out and looked at the small titan running across the open ground toward the cottages. Toward Aira on the step. Toward Eren at the door. Toward the children.

Then she looked at the big one, coming down the track, reaching for Himmel and Stark.

Her face did not do anything.

"*Milim*," she said. "*The big one. Now.*"

Milim took her.



At the cottages, Aira stood up off the step.

The small titan was coming. She could see it. Across the open ground, its long thin arms swinging, its head thrown back, its smile. Two hundred paces. A hundred and fifty. It was faster than anything she had ever seen. Faster than Turbo Granny. Faster than Okarun at his fastest, when the speed was in his legs.

She was afraid.

She had always been afraid. Of everything. Ghosts. Things in the dark. Things with too many arms. She had been afraid since she was very young, and she had stood in front of things anyway, because that was what she did. She had told Cobb that, at the ford. *You just stand in front of it anyway. And after a while being afraid gets boring.*

It was not boring.

"Gojo," she said. Her voice came out very small.

"I'm here," said Gojo.

He was beside her. Not in front. Beside. A pace to her left. With his hands in his pockets and his blindfold pushed up and his bare blue eyes on the titan running at them.

"It's going to reach the door," said Aira.

"It's going to try," said Gojo.

"I can't reach its neck. I can't fly. I've got one old blade with a nick in it."

"You don't have to reach its neck," said Gojo. "Mikasa does necks." He took one hand out of his pocket. "You do ankles. Same as Stark. One ankle. When it's close. And I'll make sure it's close."

The titan was a hundred paces away.

Behind them, the door of the second cottage banged open. Eren. With a child on his hip, and the bean-girl by the hand, and a mother behind him with another child, running. Up the road. Toward the house. Toward the place where the street began.

"*Run*," Eren was saying. "*Don't look back. Don't look at it. Just run.*"

The titan's head turned.

Toward them. Toward the running children. Toward the most people, and the smallest.

It changed direction.

Not toward Aira's door. Toward the road. Toward the children running up it. Fifty paces. Forty.

"*No*—" said Aira.

Gojo stepped forward.

Not in front. Not away from her. Sideways. Into the gap between the road and the titan. And he held up his hand, palm out, toward the running thing. And something appeared over his palm.

Not a marble. Bigger. The size of a plum. Blue. Deep blue. And it pulled.

The titan's leading foot, coming down, went sideways. A hand's breadth. Two. Pulled toward the blue. Its whole long thin body swerved, mid-stride, off the line it had been running, away from the road, toward Gojo. Toward the step. Toward Aira.

"*Now*," said Gojo.

Aira went.

She did not think. She did not have time. She came down off the step as the titan's foot came down beside it, enormous, shaking the ground, and she went in low under its swinging arm, the way Mikasa went under things. And she cut. With the old blade. With the nick in the left. At the back of its ankle. At the cord. The way Eren had said, in the kitchen, five weeks ago.

The steel went in.

All the way.

Steam burst out, white and hot, and the titan's leg went out from under it, and it fell. Forward. Sideways. Onto its long thin arms. Onto its face. Into the road. Ten paces from the place where the street began. Ten paces from the children running.

It lay there for a moment, in the road, with its smiling face in the dust. Its arms were already moving. Its ankle was already steaming, healing.

It lifted its head.

And something small and pink and very, very fast came out of the gold sky over the north field like a stone from a sling. And dropped someone onto its back.

Mikasa landed running.

She had come from the big one. The big one was down, on its knees at the foot of the track, with its nape cut, steaming away to nothing. She had cut it, and Milim had caught her before her feet touched the ground, and they had come. Faster than anything.

She ran up the small one's spine. Three steps. It was not a hill, this one. It was a ramp. Short. She reached the top.

She cut.

Two strokes. Crossing. Deep.

The small titan stopped.

Its head dropped back into the dust of the road. Its smile went slack. Its long thin arms went still. Steam began to pour off it, white and thick, rolling up the road toward the house.

Ten paces from where the street began.



Aira was on her knees in the road.

She did not remember going down. She was on her knees, with the old blade in her hand, and the steam rolling past her, and the titan's enormous slack face a few feet away, smiling at nothing, going soft at the edges.

Gojo was crouched beside her.

He had his hand on her back. Flat, between her shoulder blades. He was not saying anything bright. He was looking at the titan's face, going.

"You did ankles," he said quietly.

Aira looked at the blade in her hand. At the nick in the left. At the steam coming off it.

"I did ankles," she said.

"I made it close," said Gojo. He held up his hand and looked at it. "Plum. That was a plum." He sounded astonished. "Two months ago it was a cherry."

"*More as the threat grows*," said Aira. Shakily. "Ciel said."

"Yeah," said Gojo. He looked at the titan, almost gone. "Yeah. I guess that was a threat."

Up the road, at the place where the street began, Eren had stopped running.

He was standing with a child on his hip and the bean-girl's hand in his, and the mother behind him, and the other family behind her, all of them, in the road, at the very edge of the settlement. They had made it. The street was ten paces behind them. The house was behind that.

He had turned round.

He was looking back down the road. At the steam. At the small titan's slack face in the dust, ten paces from where he stood, going soft, going away. At Mikasa standing on its back, with both grey blades out, looking at him.

At how close it had come.

Ten paces.

He did not say anything. His face was white. He stood there in the road with a child on his hip and a little girl holding his hand, and he did not move, and he did not say anything at all.

The bean-girl looked up at him.

"Is it gone?" she said.

Eren looked down at her.

"Yes," he said. His voice did not sound like his.

"Aira did it," said the bean-girl. "I saw. Aira cut its foot."

"Yes," said Eren.

"And the tall man made it fall over."

"Yes."

The bean-girl considered this. Then she let go of his hand and ran back down the road, through the last of the steam, to where Aira was kneeling in the dust, and threw her arms round Aira's neck.

Eren watched her go.

He did not follow. He stood at the very edge of the settlement, at the place where the street began, with the other child still on his hip. And he looked at the road. At the open ground beyond it. At the ridge. At the patch of black earth at the foot of the track where the big one had been, and the new patch in the road where the small one was going. At the line of lime across the bridge road, that the Goddess's protection stopped at, that had never been tested on one of those.

Ten paces.

Mikasa had jumped down off the titan's back. She was walking up the road toward him. Through the steam. With her blades still out.

She stopped in front of him.

"Eren," she said.

He did not look at her. He was looking at the road.

"It came through the gap," he said. Very quietly. "Between the field and the cottages. There's nothing there. Just open ground. It ran straight through." He swallowed. "If Gojo hadn't been there. If Aira had been a second slower." He looked down at the child on his hip. "Ten paces."

"I know," said Mikasa.

"There's nothing between them and us," said Eren. "Nothing at all. Just a line of lime that nobody knows if it works. And open ground." He was very still. "In my world, there was a wall."

Mikasa did not say anything.

She looked at him. At his white face, looking at the road. And something went across her own face, very fast, that she did not let anyone see.



The other family left that afternoon.

The one from the second cottage. The one Eren had brought out with a child on his hip. The father loaded the cart at noon, in silence, and the mother stood by the door with her arms round the children, and when Eren came down the road to help, the father looked at him and said, very quietly, *I'm sorry. Ten paces. I can't.* And Eren said *I know*. And helped him tie the ropes.

They went south, toward the ford and the river towns, where nobody knew what they were.

The bean-girl's family did not go.

Her mother came out of the first cottage while the other cart was being loaded. She stood on the step where Aira had stood that morning, with her arms folded, and watched. When the cart had gone, she turned round and looked at Aira, who was sitting on the woodpile at the end of the road with the bean-girl in her lap, cleaning the old blade with a rag.

"We're staying," said the bean-girl's mother.

Aira looked up.

"She's safe here," said the mother. Her voice was not quite steady. "Safer than anywhere. You stood at my door. Twice. In the dark, with a lantern, and then this morning with that." She nodded at the blade. "With a titan coming." She looked at her daughter in Aira's lap. "Where else would anybody stand at my door?"

Aira did not say anything.

She looked down at the bean-girl in her lap. At the old blade in her hand, with the nick in the left. At the steam-mark in the road, ten paces from where the street began.

"Nobody gets past me," said Aira quietly. "Not to this door."

"I know," said the bean-girl's mother. "That's why we're staying."

One refuge family left in the settlement. With one child.



Eren stood at the north end until dark.

Where the street ran out. Where Mikasa stood for an hour every afternoon. He stood there with his hands at his sides and looked at the open ground between the north field and the cottage. At the gap. At the line of lime across the road. At the ridge, black against the evening, where two shapes had stood in the night.

He did not move for a long time.

Mikasa came and stood beside him at sunset. Not half a step. Beside.

She did not say anything. She looked at the open ground too.

"I hated them," said Eren, after a long time. Very quietly. "The walls. In my world. My whole life. I hated being inside. I wanted to see what was on the other side. The sea. The sand. All of it." He paused. "I used to stand at the wall and hate it so much I couldn't breathe."

"I know," said Mikasa.

"And then I saw what was on the other side," said Eren.

Mikasa did not say anything.

He turned his head and looked at her. At her face in the last of the light. At the red scarf. At the girl who had stood beside him at every wall in two worlds, who knew how his story ended in one of them, who had counted his years on her fingers alone.

"I've got tomorrows now," said Eren. "Thousands of them. You said. Every day I've got." His voice cracked. "And I want all of them. Every one. Here. With you." He looked back at the open ground. At the gap. "And a titan ran through there this morning. And got ten paces from a little girl. And there was nothing in the way."

Mikasa looked at him for a long time.

She knew what he was going to say. She could see it in his face. She had seen it in his face once before, in another world, at the end. Not this. Something like it. A boy looking at a wall, deciding.

She did not say anything.

She put her hand in his. And stood beside him at the north end, in the dark, until he was ready to go in.
