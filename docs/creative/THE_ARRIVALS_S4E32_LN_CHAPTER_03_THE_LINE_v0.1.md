*When: The night of Day 220. One refuge family is left on the north road, with one child. Hollis is in Fallow. Eren has not slept since the titans; Mikasa has not slept since Eren. The steam-mark in the road, ten paces from where the street begins, is still there.*

It came out of the low woods at dusk and walked north.

Anko saw it go, from the roof. A shape at the edge of the trees, in the last of the light, taller than a man. Grey. And instead of turning back into the woods when it reached the edge, the way it always had, it did not turn. It came out. Into the open. Into the fields. And walked.

Not toward the settlement. Round it. Along the line.

She watched it from the ridge of the roof with her notebook open on her knee and did not write anything. It walked slowly, the way it always walked, shapeless and patient, just outside the white line of lime that ran round the settlement through the long summer grass. Past the barn. Past the timber yard. Past the dead earth of the south field, which was green now, almost all the way across. All the way round the east side, in the dusk, keeping exactly outside the line. Never touching it. Never stepping over.

Until it reached the north road.

And stopped.

It stood in the road, just outside the line of lime, in the last grey light. Ten paces from the cottage where the last refuge family lived. Twenty paces from where the street began. Facing the settlement.

And it was very large now.

Anko took her hand out of her coat and put her notebook away and went down off the roof to wake Fern.



By full dark, the whole house was at the north end.

Nobody had told them to come. Nobody had needed to. Word went round, the way it always went round. And one by one, they came out of the real house and up through the settlement in the dark, quietly, carrying lanterns, until the whole of the north end of the street was full of people standing very still and looking at the thing in the road.

It had not moved.

It stood just outside the white line, in the road, in the light of forty lanterns. It was taller than the small titan had been. Taller than the porch of Arrival House. Grey. Lumpy. Shapeless. Covered, every part of it, in small grey faces the size of a hand.

And every one of them was looking.

Not some. Not most. Every one. Not one face on it was turned away any more. Hundreds of them. Front and back and sides. All turned toward the settlement. Toward the street. Toward the lanterns and the people holding them. Wide-eyed. Unblinking.

It was not muttering. It was not moving. It was just standing there, at the line, looking.

Mikasa had her grey blades out.

She stood at the very front, at the place where the street ended and the road began, a few paces inside the line. Eren was beside her. Himmel on her other side, with his sword. Aira was not there. Aira was inside the last cottage, ten paces from the thing, with the door shut and the old blade in her hand and her back against it, and the bean-girl and her mother behind her.

"Steel works out here," said Mikasa. Very quietly. "On things from the woods. Outside the line." She did not take her eyes off it. "I can cut it."

"No," said Fern.

Mikasa did not look round.

"It's outside the line," said Fern. She was standing a little way back, with the ledger closed against her chest. "If you go out to it, you're outside the line too. In the dark. With whatever else is out there." She paused. "We don't know what else is out there. We know someone's writing the pages. We know someone's afraid of whoever it is." Her voice did not change. "You don't go out."

"It's ten paces from the cottage," said Mikasa.

"I know."

"There's a child in there."

"I know," said Fern. "And Aira's at the door. And it hasn't crossed the line." She looked at the thing in the road. "Has it tried?"

Nobody answered.

"*It is not trying,*" said Rimuru.

He was standing beside Fern in human shape, with his eyes half closed. He had a thread out. Not into the thing's shadow. There was no shadow, in the dark. Into the lime. Into the line itself, where it ran across the road at the thing's feet.

"*It is not trying to come in,*" said Rimuru, for Ciel. "*It has not touched the line. It is not pressing against it. It is not testing it.*" A pause. "*It walked the whole way round, outside, and it never once tried to step over. And now it is standing here.*" Another pause. "*It is showing us.*"

"Showing us what?" said Anko.

"*That it can,*" said Ciel. "*That it can come out of the woods now. That it is large enough. That it can walk round us, all the way, and stand at our door.*" A pause. "*And that it is looking.*"



It spoke at ten o'clock.

Nobody was ready for it. It had been silent for two hours, standing in the road with every face looking, and the house had been standing at the north end for two hours looking back. And then, without moving, every mouth on it opened at once.

And it said, in hundreds of voices, together:

*Close the gate on them.*

The north end went completely silent.

*Close the gate on them,* said the thing in the road. *No salt. No oil. No glass. Nothing in, nothing out. Let them eat their own soup.*

It was not Pell's voice this time. It was all of them. Men and women and old people and children. The voices of a square full of frightened people, saying something they had heard said, at their tables, in their beds, until it was theirs.

*Starve them out,* it said. *Starve the monster town. Then they'll go back where they came from.*

Hollis was not there to hear it. He was in Fallow. But Fern, standing at the back with the ledger, closed her eyes for a moment when it said *no salt*.

*The one with the shadow,* said the thing in the road.

The north end did not breathe.

*The one with the shadow made a sun. The one with the shadow killed the grass. The one with the shadow will burn us all.* The faces did not move. *Why do they let him live. Why do they let him walk around. Why don't they give him to us.*

Nobody turned round. Nobody looked at Mau.

He was standing near the back. Beside Frieren. With his hands at his sides.

He did not step back.

He felt it. The thing behind the walls, lifting its head. Not the wheel. Not the eyes. The old thing. The thing that had stood up in the plaza and the south field and every time something came out of the dark. It wanted him to go forward. To the front. To the line. To stand in front of the thing in the road, between it and the cottage and the people, the way he always stood. To *do* something.

And under that, quieter, something else. The thing that had stood in the path to the washing court, with every hand going to a blade. That wanted him to step back. Behind the others. Out of the lantern light. So the faces would have nothing to look at.

He did neither.

He stood where he was, at the back, beside Frieren, in the light of forty lanterns, and let it look.

Frieren's hand was in his.

She did not say anything. She held his hand and looked at the thing in the road with her serene face. Her grip did not tighten. It did not loosen. It just held.

"It's not you it's looking at," said Frieren. Very quietly. Without turning her head. "It's what they think you are."

"I know," said Mau.

"It doesn't know you."

"I know." He was looking at the thing. At the hundreds of small grey faces, all looking. "Mikasa told me. It's afraid of a story."

Frieren was quiet for a moment.

"Then let it look at the story," she said. "And you stand here and be the person. And it'll have to look at both."



It did not leave.

The house did not leave either.

They stood at the north end all night. Not all of them. The children of the fort were sent to bed at midnight, and went, and lay awake behind the curtain listening. Seiko was taken in by Momo at one. The witches went back to the north wing, except Echidna, who stood at the very back of the crowd in her black dress until dawn with her pale hands folded and did not say a word to anyone. Byakuya stood beside Senku with his arms folded and his big face very serious. Coco's mother stood beside Coco with her soft hands on her daughter's shoulders and did not look away from the thing once.

Gojo stood at the front, near Mikasa, with his blindfold pushed up and his bare eyes on it. He did not say anything bright all night.

Mikasa did not move. Eren did not move.

Mau did not move.

He stood where he was, at the back, beside Frieren, in the lantern light, all night. And the thing in the road looked at him. Not all of it. But some of the faces. He could feel which ones. The ones that had said *the one with the shadow*. They had turned, slowly, through the night, the way sunflowers turn. Until a great many of them were not looking at the street, or the lanterns, or the cottage ten paces away. They were looking at him.

He let them.

Ten paces from the thing, inside the cottage, behind the door, Aira had her back against the wood and the old blade in her hand. She could hear it. Every word. She had heard it say *give him to us*. She had felt the bean-girl, behind her, go very still.

"Is it going to come in?" said the bean-girl. Very small.

"No," said Aira.

"How do you know?"

Aira did not answer at once.

Through the crack between the door and the frame, she could see a sliver of the street. Lanterns. People. And at the back, beside a white-haired elf, a young man standing very still, in the light, not moving, with hundreds of small grey faces turned toward him.

He had been standing there for hours.

"Because it's not looking at your door," said Aira. "It's looking at him." She swallowed. "He's making it look at him."

The bean-girl was quiet for a moment.

"Why?" she said.

Aira looked through the crack at the young man at the back of the street. At the elf holding his hand.

"So it doesn't look at you," she said.



At the first grey of dawn, it turned round.

All at once. Every face. As if a door had closed. The hundreds of small grey faces that had been looking at the street all night turned away. Away from the lanterns. Away from the cottage. Away from Mau. And the thing in the road turned its whole body round, slowly, and walked away.

South. Along the line. Back the way it had come, all the way round the outside of the settlement, past the timber yard and the barn and the green south field, in the grey light. Never touching the lime. Never stepping over.

Into the low woods.

Gone.

The north end let out its breath. All of it. Forty people at once.

Anko walked out to the line.

She went to the place in the road where it had stood all night, ten paces from the cottage, and crouched, and looked at the lime. The white strip across the road. A hand's width.

It was untouched. Not a mark on it. Not a smudge. On the far side, in the dust of the road, there were prints. Shapeless. Deep. The impressions of a great many small faces, pressed into the dirt, where it had stood. They came right up to the line.

And stopped. Exactly at it. Not one print on the near side.

"It didn't cross," said Anko.

She said it to herself. Very quietly. She took out her notebook and opened it to page four.

*Day 220. It came out of the woods. Walked the whole line. Stood at the north road all night. Every face looking.*

*It didn't cross. Didn't try.*

*Close the gate on them. Starve them out. Give him to us.*

*It came to look.*

She looked at the last line for a long moment. Then, under it, she wrote:

*The line held. It was never tested. It still hasn't been.*

She closed the notebook.



Mau sat down on the edge of the street when it was gone.

He did not decide to. His legs just stopped. He sat down on the edge of the boards at the end of the street, in the grey dawn, and put his elbows on his knees and his head in his hands.

Frieren sat down beside him.

She did not say anything. She put her hand flat on his back, between his shoulder blades, and left it there.

After a while, someone else sat down on his other side. Mikasa. With her grey blades back in their sheaths. She had not said a word all night. She did not say anything now. She sat beside him on the edge of the street and looked at the empty road where the thing had stood.

"You stood there," she said at last. "All night. In the light."

"Yes."

"It was looking at you."

"Yes."

Mikasa was quiet for a moment.

"Aira told me," she said. "Through the door. Just now." She did not look at him. "She said it wasn't looking at the cottage. Because it was looking at you." She paused. "She said you were making it."

Mau did not say anything.

"That's going in front," said Mikasa. "That's still going in front."

"I didn't go out," said Mau. Into his hands. "I didn't go to the line. I didn't fight it. I just—stood there." He lifted his head. "Fern said don't go out. So I didn't go out. I stood at the back and let it look." He looked at the empty road. "That's all I could think of. Something that wasn't going out."

Mikasa looked at him for a long time.

"It worked," she said.

She got up. She looked down at him. Then at the road. Then, for a long moment, at Eren, who had not moved from the very front of the north end all night, and was standing there still, at the place where the street ended, looking at the open ground and the line and the low woods, with his hands at his sides and his face white.

She went and stood beside him.

He did not look at her. He was looking at the road.

"Every face," said Eren. Very quietly. "This time. Every one of them was looking." He swallowed. "It walked all the way round us. It knows where every bit of the line is. It stood at our door all night and told us they're going to starve us." His voice did not change. "And next time it's bigger."

"I know," said Mikasa.

"And the titans. Two, this time. Next time—" He stopped. "And the clerk. And the man in the coat. And whoever's writing the pages." He looked at the steam-mark in the road, ten paces from where the street began. "And there's a line of lime. That nobody knows if it works. And open ground."

Mikasa did not say anything.

Eren stood at the very edge of the settlement in the grey dawn and looked at the open ground for a long time.

Then he turned round.

He looked back up the street. At the house. At the forty people at the north end, with their lanterns going out. At Frieren beside Mau on the edge of the boards. At Fern with her ledger. At Gojo, with his blindfold pushed up. At Rimuru, with his eyes half closed and his thread still in the lime. At Coco's mother with her hands on her daughter's shoulders. At Byakuya and Senku. At Himmel. At all of them.

At the last cottage, ten paces away, where the door was opening, and Aira was coming out with the bean-girl on her hip, blinking in the dawn.

"Mikasa," said Eren.

"Yes."

"I'm going to say something at breakfast," said Eren. "In front of everyone." He did not look at her. "And you're going to hate it."

Mikasa looked at him.

She looked at his face. At the white set face of a boy at a wall, deciding. She had seen it once before. In another world. Not this. Something like it.

She did not ask what.

"I won't hate it," she said quietly. "I'll hate that you have to say it."

Eren closed his eyes.

"Yeah," he said. "Me too."
