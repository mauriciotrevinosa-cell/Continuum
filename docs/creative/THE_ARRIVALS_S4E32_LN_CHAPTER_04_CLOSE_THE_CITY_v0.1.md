*When: Day 221, breakfast, an hour after the thing in the road walked back into the woods. Nobody in the house has slept. Rem is pouring anyway. Hollis is still in Fallow and does not know yet. Fern has the ledger open to a clean page, because she knows something is coming, and does not know what.*

Nobody ate.

Momo made porridge anyway, because Momo always made porridge, and Rem went round with the brown teapot, because Rem always went round. And then, halfway down the long table, Mau got up and took the teapot out of her hands without a word and finished the round himself, badly, and Rem sat down in the gap at the end of the bench and did not argue. But nobody ate. Forty people sat at the long table in the grey morning light, with porridge going cold in front of them and tea they did not drink, and did not say anything.

The windows were open. The air was already warm. Through them, if you listened, you could hear the bean-girl in the yard, laughing at something Aira had said. It was the only sound in the house.

Eren stood up.

He did it without hurry. He put his spoon down beside his untouched bowl and stood up from the bench, beside Mikasa, and the long table went quiet from his end outward, the way it did. Not that it had been loud.

He did not look at anyone. He looked at the table. At his hands, flat on it.

"I want to close the city," said Eren.



Nobody said anything for a long time.

"Close it," said Fern, at last. Very carefully. "What does that mean."

"A wall," said Eren.

He said it to the table.

"On the line," he said. "On the lime. All the way round. Where the Goddess's protection stops, or where we think it stops, because nobody's ever tested it." His voice was very level. "Stone, if we can. Timber and clay if we can't. Rimuru built the house by hand after Foundation was spent. Byakuya built a village from nothing. Senku can make anything." He did not look up. "A wall. With gates. Two. North and south. That shut. And a walk along the top, so someone can stand on it and see what's coming."

The long table was very still.

"No more runs," said Eren. "No more carts down to the river towns. No more Hollis standing in front of a gate while a clerk points at him. No more Brenna getting a stone in the face." He paused. "Every time we go out there, we give them something to look at. Every time they look at us, they're afraid. Every time they're afraid, it gets bigger. That thing in the woods." He swallowed. "Last night every face on it was looking. Every one. It walked all the way round us, and it knows where every bit of the line is, and it stood at our door all night and told us they're going to starve us out."

Nobody said anything.

"So we stop giving them something to look at," said Eren. "We bring everything inside. We grow everything inside. Daphne's fields. The bees. Everything. We stop going out. We stop answering." His hands had closed on the table. "If they never see us, there's nothing to say. If there's nothing to say, there's nothing to be afraid of. And if there's nothing to be afraid of—" He stopped. "Maybe it starves. Instead of us."

The silence went on.

At the end of the bench nearest the door, Brenna stood up. With the stitches above her eye, and her crossbow left leaning against the wall behind her.

"If you close it," she said. "Where do we go?"

Eren looked at her for the first time.

"Inside," he said.

Brenna looked at him for a long moment.

"I've got a sister," said Cobb, beside her. Very small. "On the east side. With sheep." He was looking at his hands. "If there's a wall. Do I see her?"

Eren did not answer.

"I held a gate for twelve years," said Brenna. Quietly. "For a lord. Who stopped paying me in the autumn." She touched the stitches above her eye. "I know what walls are for. I know what gates are for." She looked at Eren. "They're for keeping things out. And they keep things in. Both. Always both." She sat down again. "I'm not saying no. I'm saying I know."

Eren looked at her. Then back down at the table.

"I know," he said.



"You hate walls," said Mikasa.

She did not stand up. She was sitting beside him on the bench, very straight, with her hands in her lap and the red scarf round her neck, and she said it very quietly. To him. Not to the table. But the long table was so silent that everyone heard.

Eren did not look at her.

"I know," he said.

"You spent your whole life," said Mikasa, "hating them. Standing at the wall in our world and hating it so much you couldn't breathe. You told me. You told everyone. At this table. In the first winter." Her voice did not change. "You wanted to see what was on the other side. The sea. The sand. The fire. All of it."

"I know," said Eren.

"You wanted to be free," said Mikasa.

Eren's hands were shaking on the table.

"I saw what was on the other side," he said. His voice cracked. "I saw it. All of it. The sea and the sand and the—" He stopped. "And here I've seen it too. It's titans that run. And a clerk with someone else's words in his pocket. And a thing made of what frightened people say, that walks round us at night and says *give him to us*." He looked at the steam-mark you could not see from here, ten paces from where the street began. "And a little girl, ten paces from a smile."

He turned his head. At last. And looked at Mikasa.

"I've got tomorrows now," said Eren. "You said. Every day I've got. Thousands." His eyes were wet. "I never had them before. Not one. I always knew exactly how many. And now I've got thousands, and I want them, Mikasa. I want every single one. Here. With you. With all of you." His voice broke. "And yesterday morning a titan ran through the open ground and got ten paces from that little girl, and there was *nothing in the way*."

Mikasa looked at him.

She did not say anything. Her face did not do anything. She looked at him for a long time, the way she had looked at him across a sea once, knowing too much.

"That's why," said Eren. Very quietly. "That's the only reason. I hated walls because I didn't have anything worth keeping in." He looked back down at the table. "Now I do."

The long table was completely silent.



Himmel spoke.

He was at the far end, where he always sat, facing the door. He had not said anything. He had sat with his hands on the table and listened to all of it, with his face very grave.

"In my world," said Himmel quietly, "we never closed anything."

Eren looked at him.

"Ten years on the road," said Himmel. "Every town we came to was frightened of something. Demons. Dragons. Things in the woods. And every time, someone in the town said *build a wall*." He was not looking at Eren. He was looking at his own hands. "And every time, we said no. We'll walk out and meet it." He paused. "That's what heroes do. We walk out."

"And did it work?" said Eren.

Himmel was quiet for a long time.

"Mostly," he said. "Mostly it worked." He looked up. "And sometimes we came back to a town we'd saved and found it had built the wall anyway, after we left. Because we'd gone. Because heroes always go. And the people who stay have to live there after." He looked at Eren down the long table. "I don't know if you're wrong, Eren. I've never been the one who stays."

Nobody said anything.

"I can draw it," said Rimuru.

He was in human shape at the cold-hearth end. He had not slept either. He had held a thread in the lime all night, reading it, feeling for whether the thing would cross. His face was grey with tiredness. He was looking at the table.

"The wall," said Rimuru. "I could draw it. Today. I know how." He did not look up. "I built a whole nation once. Walls. Gates. Roads. Everything." He paused. "In my world, it worked. It kept people safe."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Milim asked me once," he said. "At this table. On story night. If we were still doing the city thing." He looked up at last. Down the table. At Milim, who had gone very still on the bench, with her hands in her lap. "And I said no. A home." He looked down again. "I meant it. I still mean it." He was quiet. "A wall doesn't stop it being a home. I know that." He swallowed. "But it's the first thing that makes it a city."

Milim did not say anything. But her lip was wobbling.

At the cold-hearth end, beside Rimuru, Gojo had his blindfold pushed up. He was looking at Eren with his bare blue eyes. He did not say anything bright.

"Walls didn't help me," he said quietly. To nobody. "In my world. They put me in a box. A very good box. The best box anyone ever made. To keep me in." He looked at his hands. "It worked. For a while." He paused. "The people outside still died."

Shoko put her hand flat on the table beside his.



Fern had not written anything.

She sat at the head of the long table with the ledger open to the clean page and her pen in her hand, and she had not written a single word. She was looking down the table. At all of them. At Eren, standing. At Mikasa, beside him. At Brenna and Cobb at the end of the bench. At Himmel. At Rimuru. At Gojo. At the forty people at the long table, with their porridge going cold.

At the two people halfway down the bench who had not spoken.

Mau and Rimuru.

The two faces. The ones the outside world talked to. The ones who had shaken Hollis's hand, together, because the rule from the council said the world spoke to two faces and never to one. A wall was something the world would see. A wall was something the world would talk about, in every square from Ashby to Fallow. A wall was a face.

Rimuru had spoken.

Fern looked at Mau.

So did everyone else.

He was sitting beside Frieren, with the brown teapot still on the table in front of him where he had put it down after the round. He had not said anything. He had listened to all of it. To Eren. To Brenna. To *you hate walls*. To *I've got tomorrows now*. To Himmel and Rimuru and Gojo.

He thought about the thing in the road. All its faces. Looking at him, all night, while he stood in the lantern light and let it.

He thought about Hollis, standing in front of a gate with his hat in his hands, saying *from people*.

He thought about Merren, the woman with the chimney, putting out one soft healed hand to touch the side of the cart as it went past.

He thought about a little girl, ten paces from a smile.

"Not tonight," said Mau.

The long table looked at him.

"Not this morning," said Mau. He did not look at anyone. He looked at the teapot. "Nobody's slept. Nobody's eaten. Hollis isn't here, and it's his road. Cobb's sister is on the east side. Brenna knows what gates are for." He paused. "And Eren's right. And Himmel's right. And Rimuru's right. And they can't all be right at once." He looked up. At Eren, standing. "So we talk about it. All of us. Properly. Round this table. When we've slept." He looked at Fern. "Not tonight."

Fern looked at him for a long moment.

Then she looked at Eren.

Eren was still standing. His hands were flat on the table. He was looking at Mau with an expression that was very hard to read.

"Not tonight," said Eren slowly.

"Not tonight," said Mau. "But we talk about it. I promise." He paused. "You said it first. Before. In front of everyone." He looked at Eren. "That's the right way. You didn't build it in the night and tell us after."

Eren looked at him for a long time.

Then, very slowly, he sat down.

Mikasa's hand found his under the table. He did not look at her. But his hand turned over and held on to hers.



Fern picked up her pen.

On the clean page of the ledger, in her small upright hand, at the top, she wrote:

*Day 221.*

And under it:

*Proposed: close the city. (Eren.)*

She looked at it for a long time.

Then, under it, she wrote:

*Not tonight. (Mau.)*

And under that, after a long pause, she drew a line across the page. And left the space below it empty. The whole rest of the page. Clean. Waiting.

For the house.

She closed the ledger.

"Eat your porridge," said Fern. "All of you. It's cold. Eat it anyway."

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then, at the end of the bench nearest the hearth, Rem picked up her spoon. And ate. And beside her, Ram picked up hers. And then Seika. And Nijika. And Momo, at the kitchen door, who had made it. And the fort, behind its curtain. And Brenna, with her stitches, and Cobb beside her. One at a time, down the long table, forty people picked up their spoons and ate cold porridge in the grey summer morning, without saying anything.

Eren ate his. Mikasa ate hers. Their hands were together under the table, so they ate with one hand each, badly.

Mau ate his.

Frieren, beside him, did not eat hers. She was looking at him.

"Not tonight," she said. Very quietly. So only he could hear.

"I know," said Mau.

"You said that once before," said Frieren. "At the end of the Trial. About the Goddess. *Not tonight.*" She looked at her cold porridge. "And then it was a long time before tonight came."

Mau did not say anything.

"This one's going to come sooner," said Frieren.

"I know," said Mau.

He looked down the long table. At Eren, eating cold porridge with one hand. At Rimuru, with his head on his arms at the cold-hearth end, asleep at last. At Himmel, facing the door. At Brenna, touching the stitches above her eye. At Coco's mother, with her soft hand on Coco's inky one. At the open window, where you could hear the bean-girl in the yard, laughing.

At the ledger, closed, at the head of the table, with a whole empty page in it.

"I know," said Mau again. "That's why we have to get it right."

Out in the yard, under the ink tree, the bean-girl laughed again. And Aira, sitting on the bench with the old blade across her knees, laughed too.

And far to the south, in the low woods, something that smelled of wet paper stood very still among the trees with every face it had turned toward the settlement.

Waiting to see what they would build.
