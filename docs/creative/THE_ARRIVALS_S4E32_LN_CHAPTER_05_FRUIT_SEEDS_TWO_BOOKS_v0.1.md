# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 25
## Chapter 26 — Fruit, Seeds, Two Books

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 25
**Reading order:** 26
**Continuity state:** Day 214, same evening as Market Day | involuntary displacement to Ashby during the house report; Ciel immediately informs Rimuru | ordinary-money purchases, two flower grimoires | brief assisted Infinity against a thrown stone, strain and recovery limits remain | Pell's alleged hero account is propaganda, not proof of its source; Mau leaves an invitation, not an arranged meeting | return leak, family report and health check | Daphne's seeds, Frieren's gifts and ink-tree reading | Gojo/Shoko share old humour | Ashby trade ban and unknown agenda remain; future Sukuna conversation unplanned

*When: Day 214, in the long midsummer evening. Hollis's account has reached the table. Ashby's last stalls are packing up. The broken goods by its gate have not all been swept away.*

Anko had begun repeating the words from the woods, making sure Fern had them exactly.

Mau had been looking at the place where Brenna's bandage met her hair.

At the small red mark coming through it.

Then there was glass under his shoe.

He did not understand that part at first. The light was different. The air smelled of dust and something sweet going sour, and a horse was making a tired noise somewhere close to him. He had not stood up. His hand was still curled as if it were resting against the edge of the long table.

There was no table.

There was a broken jar on the ground. White cream in the wheel ruts, drying at the edges. A piece of thin glass caught the last light beside it.

Mau looked up.

The guard at the gate was staring at him.

*Ciel.*

*I saw. Master has been told.*

Not *I'll tell him*. Already. Mau let out a little of the breath he had been holding.

*Where am I?*

"Ashby," said the guard, because Mau had asked the question aloud too. Then the man seemed to hear his own answer and remember that it was not the important part. "You weren't there."

Mau looked at the cream again.

"No."

"You came out of—"

"I know."

"Stop."

Mau had not meant to move. He turned toward the voice, the guard put out a hand, and the street moved instead.

For half a heartbeat there was a pressure behind Mau's eyes, like the beginning of a headache.

Then he was looking at the back of the guard's head from well beyond the gate.

The guard turned very slowly.

Neither of them said anything.

*That was not a second decision,* Ciel said. *Don't try to repeat it.*

*I wasn't trying the first time.*

*I know.*

The guard was still holding out his hand at the place where Mau had been. Someone beside a handcart asked him what was wrong. He did not answer.

Mau looked down the street.

There were vegetables under a striped cloth. Apples in a shallow basket. A woman was putting the unsold oranges into a crate, very carefully, moving the bruised ones aside.

He put a hand in his pocket and found the little purse he had taken out earlier to pay for workshop supplies. Ordinary money. Not the warm thing waiting in his room.

A bad idea arrived, complete.

He knew it was bad. That was not the same as not having it.

"How much for those?" he asked the woman.



The first bag was for the house.

He told himself that while the woman counted the apples. He would have to eat while somebody worked out how to get him back. Everyone would have to eat. The house did not have oranges this week. Here were oranges.

It was a very poor argument. It was the one he had.

"And the carrots," he said.

She looked at the money in his open hand, then at his face. She had not seen what happened at the gate. The man at the next stall had. He was looking past her at the guard, who had finally remembered how to walk.

*I don't know how to get back,* Mau told Ciel.

*Master is arranging help. Stay where you can be found.*

*I'm at the fruit stall.*

*He knows. Please remain at the fruit stall.*

The woman put a small packet on top of the carrots.

"Seed, if you want the other sort. These are for eating."

Mau looked at the packet.

Daphne would know what to do with that.

"Yes," he said. "Some of those too."

The woman named a price. He paid it. She watched him count, and took only what she had asked for.

Behind him somebody said, "Was that him?"

"Who?"

"The one from the hills."

"You've never seen him."

"Neither have you."

Mau put the seeds into the top of the bag, where they would not get wet.

The next shop was not far away. He could see its door from the stall. It sold stakes, twine, pots, small tools and things for people trying to persuade plants to grow in unhelpful places. There were books in the window.

He stopped with an orange halfway into the second bag.

*Mau,* said Ciel.

*I can see it from here.*

*That was not the arrangement.*

He looked at the books again.

"One minute," he said, to absolutely nobody who would agree to it.



The shopkeeper thought he wanted the planting tables.

"No," said Mau. "The little one behind it."

The man took it down. It was thin, with a worn green cover and a flower drawn on the front in an ink that had faded unevenly.

"Petals," he said. "Changing the colour. Takes longer to learn than it saves you buying dye."

Mau opened it carefully. A drawing of the same flower repeated down a page, the petals marked with little instructions. Not a weapon. Not a binding. A small, patient piece of magic that somebody had thought important enough to write down.

He could already see her face.

"And that one?"

"Keeping a cut flower. Stops it wilting, if you do it properly. Doesn't grow a new one."

There were two diagrams on the open page, one of a stem in a cup and one of a flower laid flat. Frieren would tell him why the difference mattered. She would begin with something like *obviously* and then explain for twenty minutes, and he would forget to ask a question because he liked the way she looked while explaining.

Mau put the rest of the money on the counter.

It was enough. Barely.

The shopkeeper wrapped both books together. Mau had just tucked them safely between the bags when the sound outside changed.

Not louder. Closer together. People making room for somebody who expected them to.

*Ciel.*

*Yes.*

*I'm going to need help keeping the distance stable.*

*You are not cleared for this.*

*I know. I'm not attacking anybody. But something's about to happen.*

He found the familiar shape and held only that. The narrow separation. No reaching for anything behind it, no invitation to the eyes to keep looking. Ciel steadied what he was trying to hold; she did not open a new door in him.

The pressure behind his eyes grew sharper.

"You," said Pell, from the shop entrance.



Mau stepped outside because the shopkeeper was behind him.

Pell had brought enough people to fill the little space between the shop and the stall. Not all of them looked angry. Some looked curious. Some looked frightened already. The guard was at the edge, still trying to decide what he had seen.

"You're the person who made a sun."

Mau blinked.

"Technically, it wasn't a sun."

Pell turned halfway toward the crowd.

"He admits it."

Mau shut his mouth.

Too late, apparently.

"He comes here himself," Pell said. His hand had gone to the paper inside his coat. He drew it far enough out to open the first fold, looked down once, and looked back up. "Our town refuses his goods, so their leader comes in person. To make sure we understand what happens to people who say no."

Several people moved back.

Mau looked at the bags in his hands.

Then he began to laugh.

He knew, almost at once, that this was another poor decision. Pell's face changed as if he had been given something useful.

"You've got an imagination," Mau said. "Or somebody told you exactly what to say."

Pell did not answer that.

"First. Why do you think I want to destroy you? Second. If that was what I came for, why would I buy this?"

He lifted one bag a little. An orange rolled against the top. He caught it with his thumb before it fell out.

"Fruit. Vegetables. Some seeds. And books for the most incredible person in the world. That's what I've got."

Nobody laughed with him.

"What have you got?" Mau asked. "Apart from the paper."

Pell's hand moved.

"Don't hide it. I saw it. I've read the bit you just unfolded. It's the same speech they told us about at home. From what I hear, you bring a version of it every market day."

The space outside the shop grew very quiet.

Mau had not looked through Pell's coat. He had not needed to. There had been three familiar words at the top of the page. *Can't be traced.* Hollis had said them at the table a few minutes ago, in another place.

"There's a thing in the woods repeating what you say," he went on. "Maybe there's more than one. I don't know. I haven't met every monster out there, and I'd rather not. But the fear is feeding one we do know about. So why keep making it?"

"Listen to him," said Pell.

"I am asking you. Where's the proof I came here to hurt anyone? If I wanted to do what you're saying, why would I let you finish the speech? Why would I be standing here buying apples?"

A woman pulled a child closer to her skirt.

Mau saw. The words sounded different once they were outside his mouth. He did not know how to take them back without giving Pell the next sentence too.

"There," said Pell, very softly. "You hear how he speaks."

He faced the others properly now.

"As if we should be grateful he lets us speak. As if a purchase makes him harmless. There are people who saw what he did in the other town. Who saw those same blades in that field twice. A city broken, and now we are meant to trust him because he has a bag of fruit."

Mau stopped smiling.

"A brave warrior from one of their own worlds went to speak to them," Pell said. "They attacked him. Nearly killed him. He survived. Is that what happens to anyone who asks the wrong question?"

For a moment Mau heard nothing else.

Not the shops. Not the crowd. Not the guard.

The warrior who had come to speak.

The stone came from somewhere on his right.

He saw it late. A grey irregular shape, turning in the air, and a hand dropping behind somebody's shoulder. It reached the space in front of his cheek and kept failing to finish the journey.

Mau looked at it.

The market went still.

It was a small stone. It had been a small stone when it hit Brenna too.

"That wasn't very kind," he said.

Somebody made a sound and backed into the stall. The woman with the child was trying to leave without turning her back. Pell's hand had closed on the paper so hard that the folded edge was bending.

Mau let the stone fall into the dust. Holding the distance had begun to make his fingers shake. He kept both bags low so that nobody would think he was raising a hand.

"So that's the story," he said.

He looked at Pell for a long moment. Then he laughed again, once, without much amusement.

"If you see that hero you say we attacked, tell him I want to talk to him. He knows where to find me."

He did not wait for an answer.

He walked toward the edge of the crowd. People let him through. That was not trust. He knew the difference.

All he wanted suddenly was the smell of the old inn. Wood, soup, a damp coat left too close to the hearth. The place he had first learned that a room could wait for him. Then the house they had made after it, and the long table, and her book beside somebody else's plate.

*Home,* he thought. *Please.*

The street disappeared.



An apple hit the common-room floor and rolled under Fern's chair.

Everybody turned.

Mau was standing beside the table with two bags, a crumpled paper parcel, and the expression of somebody who had not meant to arrive holding any of them.

Rimuru was on his feet already. There was a map spread in front of him and Diablo, with Hollis pointing at a road. Frieren had her staff. Shoko had come to the door. Nobody had finished the soup.

"Um," said Mau.

For a moment nobody helped him.

"Sorry."

"We know about the first part," Rimuru said. "Ciel told me. What was the rest?"

"I was listening. And then I was there. I didn't know how to come back."

"We were working on that."

"I know."

Mau looked down at the bag.

"I brought fruit."

No response.

"And seeds. Fruit for eating. Seeds for growing."

Daphne was beside him before he finished the sentence.

"You're forgiven," she said, taking the little packets out of the top.

Shoko looked at her.

"For the shopping," Daphne added, studying the writing. "I haven't heard the rest."

Mau put the bags on the table before his hands could drop them. Shoko caught his wrist. He stayed still while she looked at him, then at Rimuru.

"Used it?"

"Briefly," said Mau. "Someone threw a stone."

Frieren's hand tightened on the staff.

"It didn't reach me."

"I can see that," said Shoko. "Sit down. This hasn't cleared you for anything."

"I know."

He sat. Ciel was already telling Rimuru what she had observed. No new opening had been requested. The displacements had not become a route they knew how to use. The strain from holding the distance was still in his face.

He began taking things out of the bags.

And he told them.

The guard. The second jump. The fruit stall, and the shop, and Pell arriving. He did not try to make the shopping sound like a plan they should have approved. He repeated his own words as well as Pell's. Anko stopped him twice to ask which was an exact sentence and which was the way he remembered it.

"And then he said a hero came to talk to us," Mau said.

Gojo's cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Nearly killed by us. Survived. You know. A peaceful conversation."

"Sukuna," said Yuta.

"That's what it sounds like. He didn't give a name."

Yuta was looking at him now. So was Frieren.

"I told him if he saw that supposed hero again, he could tell him I wanted to talk."

"Mau," said Anko.

"He knows where to find me. That's what I said. Then I thought about coming home, and here I was."

He put an orange beside Brenna's untouched bowl.

She looked at it, then at him.

"You don't have to eat it now," he said.

Her hand came down over it. She did not move it away.

Anko closed the notebook for a moment.

"I don't think everything you said was a good idea."

Mau did not answer.

"But it could have been worse. We let it settle. We see what comes back. And next time you find yourself somewhere you didn't choose, you wait for the people coming to help you. You don't add errands."

He nodded.

Rimuru folded the map without putting it away. Fern wrote the purchases separately from the broken goods. Hollis had not suddenly acquired permission to bring a cart through Ashby's gate. The woman's money and the clerk's accusation had not changed that.



Frieren waited until Shoko had finished.

She had heard Ciel's report through Rimuru. She knew he had not chosen to disappear. She had also stood with her staff in her hand while somebody put a road on a map, and he had come home with oranges.

Now he was helping Momo clear a space for them.

Frieren looked at the oranges. Then at him.

He knew that look.

He went back to the little parcel he had kept dry, took off the paper, and came to her with both hands behind his back.

One ear moved.

"What?" she asked.

"Do you really think I'd go shopping and not bring you something?"

"You weren't supposed to go shopping."

"That's not the question."

The other ear moved.

He brought the books round.

For a second she was very still.

Then she took them, turned the first one over, opened it, saw the diagram, and looked up at him with an expression that made the whole foolish journey hurt in a different place.

"Both?"

"One changes the petals. One keeps the flower. That's what he said. You'll have to tell me if he was right."

She had already taken his sleeve.

"Come here."

"Shoko said sit down."

"You will."



There was a lamp on the bench beneath the ink tree.

Frieren moved it to the flat root beside the seat, put the second book where it would stay dry, and arranged Mau against the back before he could decide how to arrange himself. Then she lay along the bench, with her head in his lap and the first grimoire open above her.

He looked down at her.

She turned a page.

He put his fingers in her hair, gently, at the place above her ear.

The ear went pink.

"You saved yourself this time," she said.

"I know."

He did not ask whether she meant the book or something else. She caught his free hand for a moment and kept it against her cheek. Then she let him have it back so that he could keep doing what he was doing.

"This is a very inefficient way of setting the colour," she said.

"Is it?"

"Listen."

He listened.



Gojo watched from the open common-room door with his tea.

"My miniature," he said proudly. "Defying the world. Winning the girl."

Shoko stopped beside him.

"When were you ever like that with anyone?"

He turned his head toward her with the look of a man whose own side had fired at him.

"I paid for things."

"You were the richest one of us and you wouldn't buy popcorn. You begged Suguru for his."

"He had too much."

"You told him that before he'd opened it."

Gojo opened his mouth, found nothing helpful in it, and laughed. Shoko laughed too. For a moment it was easy, hearing the old name between them without having to run from it.

Under the tree, Frieren pointed at something in the book. Mau leaned down to see. She said something that made him smile, and his hand went back to her hair.

"Take notes from the boy," Shoko said.

Gojo looked at her.

She did not rescue him from the pause. A little colour had come into his face, visible even in the lamplight. He took a drink of tea he had not needed.

"I'm going upstairs," she said.

"Mm."

She left him at the door.

Inside, Anko opened the notebook again. Under Pell's new account she wrote the exact words Mau could remember, then drew a line beneath *a brave warrior came to speak*.

They already knew someone had an agenda. Now they had another story that someone wanted told.

Gojo stood with his cup and watched the two people beneath the tree. Frieren was reading aloud. Mau was listening, with both feet on the ground.
