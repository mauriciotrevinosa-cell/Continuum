# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 26
## Chapter 4 — Her Own Afternoon

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 26
**Reading order:** 4
**Continuity state:** Day 224, after Shoko's clinic shift | routine care and a practical Maomao/Kusuri disagreement are handed over properly | Shoko chooses an ordinary afternoon with Gojo | shared humour, small frustration and returning personal wants coexist with their grief | no technique development, new medicine, coin petition or smoking supply

*When: Day 224, late afternoon into supper. Nobody has begun the wall. The clinic has its ordinary patients, the watches have their names, and Shoko's shift ends at four.*

The labels were different because Kusuri had said the difference was obvious.

Maomao put both jars in front of her.

"With the lids on."

Kusuri looked at the jars.

One held the good cream. The other held a preparation for work-roughened hands which had not passed Maomao's tests and which Kusuri had been told to keep off the common shelf. In the open jars they were different enough. One smelled faintly of honey; the other had a bitter green edge to it. With the lids on, in the lamplight, they were two small pots with Kusuri's writing on them.

"I know which is which."

"You aren't the person who will fetch it."

"Then I'll fetch it."

"Every time. In the dark. When you are asleep."

Shoko, washing her hands, watched Kusuri look from one jar to the other.

"Make the unapproved one feel different," she said. "A cord round the neck. Something you can find without reading."

Maomao looked at her.

"And keep it off the shelf," Shoko added.

"That too."

Kusuri took both pots away, muttering something about competent assistants. Five minutes later she came back with a cord round one and an expression daring anybody to approve of it.

Jinshi was in the doorway with the clean water. He put it down where Maomao had pointed the first time, without making her point again.

"Shall I take those?"

"The dirty cloths," said Maomao. "Not the jars."

"I can distinguish jars from cloths."

"Good."

He looked at Shoko. She dried her hands and pretended she had not heard either of them.

There were no urgent patients. The man whose palm had split at the kiln had been cleaned and bandaged. He had received instructions which he appeared to believe applied to someone else's hand. Maomao had written them down and made him repeat them before he left. Mau's scheduled check was done. The notes were beside the bed, where either doctor could reach them, and the next supplies were laid out.

Shoko looked at the light in the window.

Then she took off her coat.



Gojo was not waiting outside the clinic.

This was what he told her when she came out and found him leaning against the wall opposite it.

"Then you won't mind if I go."

"Coincidental destination."

"You don't know where I'm going."

"I'm very adaptable."

She walked past him. He fell in beside her, tall enough to block the draught from the open door without doing anything useful on purpose.

He looked at her empty hands.

"No notebook?"

"No."

"No bag full of things that make people scream?"

"I could go back for it."

"I like this arrangement."

She had decided, while drying her hands, that she wanted to sit somewhere she could not see a clinic door. There were several places she could have chosen. She went to the river because it was still warm and because the water made a sound that asked nothing of her.

They stayed on the familiar path inside the marked ground. Gojo looked at the survey cord lying along one stretch of grass. Rimuru and Senku had left little labelled pegs where they had finished measuring. No wall. Not even a ditch.

"How many afternoons do you think that takes?" he asked.

"Building it?"

"Talking about it."

"You could ask how many people don't want to be trapped in your good idea."

He looked at her.

"Not my good idea."

"I know."

He was quiet for a few steps. Then he said, "Fair."



Shoko chose a place halfway down the bank, where the grass was dry and a willow made a strip of shade. Gojo crouched as though he meant to inspect the ground for her.

She sat down before he could.

"It's grass, Satoru."

"Could be suspicious grass."

"You'll let me know."

He sat beside her. For a while that was all they did.

The first quiet was uncomfortable. She could hear all the sounds that might have been somebody coming to fetch her. Feet on the path. A call from the washing court. A door shutting somewhere behind the trees. Each time, a part of her got up before the rest of her knew why.

Gojo did not ask whether she was relaxing.

He picked up a flat stone and turned it over in his hand.

"Don't improve the river," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking at it like a problem."

"I was looking for somewhere to throw this."

She held out her hand.

He gave her the stone. She tossed it underhand. It hit the water once, bounced badly, and sank.

Gojo looked at the little ring it left behind.

"Very strong opening."

"Your turn."

He found another. He took an unnecessarily long time choosing it. Then he threw it, and it went six times, small and quick and maddeningly neat, before it vanished into the broken light.

Shoko looked at him.

"Nothing strange," he said at once, holding up both hands. "Just throwing."

"I didn't ask."

"You had your asking face."

"I had my annoyed face. They're different."

He smiled at the water. She had seen that smile across a classroom desk, usually immediately before something became everybody else's problem.

She found a third stone. It was too thick, and when she threw it, it made a very satisfactory splash.

"That one's for distance," she said.

Gojo leaned sideways to avoid the drops. One caught him on the cheek anyway. He wiped it away with a finger and looked at it as if she had thrown an insult.

"You're allowed to lose."

"So are you."

He laughed. She found a better stone.



By the time they stopped, Shoko had managed three skips and a short argument about whether the last one counted.

Gojo had sand on the heel of his hand. She had grass pressed into her sleeve and water up one wrist. The path had carried several people past them, none of whom needed a doctor. One of the fort boys, carrying a basket, had stared at Gojo's blindfold until Gojo turned his head and asked whether there was something on his face. The boy had thought about it earnestly, then said yes.

Shoko was still laughing about that when Gojo took the blindfold off and folded it beside his leg.

"I was hoping you'd defend me."

"He was right."

"You've known me too long."

She looked at him then. Properly. Not to check his pupils or the bruising that was no longer there. His hair had fallen into his forehead. There was a dark little place on his sleeve where her stone had splashed him.

"Sometimes it doesn't feel like that," she said.

He stopped picking sand from his palm.

"Knowing you. There were years when I knew what you could do better than I knew what you did on an ordinary afternoon."

He looked at the water.

"Worked. Mostly."

"I know."

"Dragged the kids somewhere. Bought things I didn't need. Annoyed Nanami."

"I assumed that one."

His mouth moved. For a moment she thought he would make it another joke.

"Didn't always notice I was doing it," he said.

She put her hands flat in the grass behind her and leaned back.

"I don't want to start knowing you only when something's gone wrong again."

Gojo looked at her. She kept looking out over the water, giving him the chance to hear it without having to perform an answer.

He put down the stone he had been turning in his fingers.

"We can be very boring," he said.

"I don't believe that."

"I can learn. I'm told I'm gifted."

She smiled. He watched her for half a breath too long and looked away when she caught him.

That pleased her more than it ought to have.

"You've got sand," she said.

"Everywhere."

"Give me your hand."

He did. She brushed the grit off his palm with the side of her thumb. There was nothing to mend. She took her time anyway.



They stayed until the shade moved off her shoes.

She had reached for the cigarette once, from habit, and left it in her pocket. There was no decision in that, and she did not make it one. She had wanted to throw another stone more than she had wanted to hold it.

When they got up, her leg had gone numb. Gojo offered his arm without saying anything triumphant. She used it, stood until the pins and needles passed, and did not let go immediately afterwards.

"Same time?" he asked as they went up the bank.

"Don't turn it into a shift."

"Right. Unscheduled professional boredom."

"You're getting worse at that word."

At the house door, Maomao was speaking to the man from the kiln. He had come back with the bandage unravelled. Jinshi was holding the clean water again, with an expression suggesting that this was not the most difficult task he had been given that day.

Shoko slowed. Maomao looked up.

"I've got it," she said.

Shoko nodded and went inside.

At supper, Gojo tried to take the better half of the fish from the plate between them. She caught his wrist before he got it.

"You've known me too long," he said.

"Yes."

She took the fish. He let her.
