# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 26
## Chapter 7 — Fallen Timber

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 26
**Reading order:** 7
**Continuity state:** Day 228 | first timber collection for the agreed earth-and-timber defence; an escorted work party beyond the marked line | Sekhmet distinguishes fallen wood from usable wood; old trees remain | ordinary hauling, scarce sound lengths and pegs | Minerva and Maomao treat a pinched hand without magic | work stops before dusk; the house remains the same house

*When: Day 228, two mornings after the decision. There are stakes inside the white line, a short trench, and no wall yet. The north road is open under watch; nobody has agreed that the woods are safe.*

Sekhmet rejected the first tree without opening her eyes.

It was a very large tree. That had been its principal attraction. It lay beyond the timber yard, across a dip in the ground, with half its roots in the air and the other half still holding a great clot of earth. The work party had spent twenty minutes looking at it. Byakuya had walked the length of it twice. Wakana had put his hand on the bark and stood there doing the quiet arithmetic he did before touching anything with an axe.

Then Minerva set Sekhmet down on the folded blanket on the empty handcart, and Sekhmet said, "No."

Byakuya looked round.

"It's already down."

"Yes."

"That's what you said. Take the fallen ones."

"I did not say," said Sekhmet, "that they were all good."

She lifted one hand out of the blanket. Pointed, without looking, at a long break in the bark near the roots. Then the hand went back.

Wakana went to the break. He pushed his knife into the pale wood beneath it.

It went in too far.

He took the knife out, rubbed what was on the point between his fingers, and shook his head.

"Inside's gone."

Byakuya crouched beside him. The big smile he had brought out of the house that morning became a smaller, practical expression. He tried a piece himself. It came away in his hand like bread left in water.

"Well," he said. "That saves us half a day of being wrong."

Sekhmet did not answer. She appeared to be asleep again.

They were a little way beyond the marked boundary, on the old timber route. Mikasa stood where the ground rose, looking between the trees, and Stark waited at the other end of the dip with his axe. Neither was carrying wood. That had been settled before the party left. People watching could not also have a trunk on their shoulders.

Senku had disliked the number of empty hands in the group.

Fern had asked him which pair of eyes he wished to remove.

There had been no further discussion.



The second tree was smaller. It had fallen between two standing ones and caught halfway down, so that one end lay on the ground and the other hung above it.

"Don't go under," said Mikasa.

Nobody did. Byakuya had already taken one step that way, and stopped with it still in the air, and put it somewhere else.

They looked at the tree for a while.

The little marks on Rimuru's drawing, laid on the cart under a stone, did not have trees resting on other trees. The drawing had lengths. It had places for the earth to go and places for people to stand. A small neat space beside the road, where the gate would be, and another at the south approach. It did not have a hollow trunk, or the slope between the yard and the woods, or the distance a good piece of timber became when six people had to carry it home.

Wakana looked at the hanging end.

"Not today."

"We could bring ropes," said Byakuya.

"Tomorrow. With enough people."

Byakuya nodded. He did not say that in his village they would have done it with three. He took the pencil from behind his ear and made a mark on the back of the drawing.

Sekhmet opened one eye.

"There's one farther left."

They went left.

That one was not large. It lay flat. The bark had split along one side, but the heart of it rang when Wakana knocked it with the haft of his knife. He looked at Sekhmet, on the cart.

She had opened both eyes now. She was looking past him.

At an old tree still standing, enormous and dark, with branches high above the others.

"Not that one," she said.

"We weren't going to take it," said Wakana.

"Good."

Her eyes closed.

They took the small one.



By noon they had three sound lengths, a pile of shorter pieces, and a cart that no longer had room for Sekhmet.

Minerva moved her to the spare blanket under a tree. Gently, with the same fierce concentration she gave to lowering anyone who might be hurt. Sekhmet complained only when Minerva tried to put the blanket over her shoulders.

"Summer."

"You'll get cold on the ground."

"Then put it under me."

Minerva put it under her.

The cooper was shaping a peg with his small knife. He had brought a handful of blanks from the yard. They would not replace the iron straps on the gate. They would hold the temporary braces, and there were more braces than anyone had remembered when the drawing was on the table.

"That many?" Byakuya asked him.

"More."

Byakuya looked at the blanks, then at the length of timber nearest him.

"I've got a chisel."

"I've got a knife."

They sat down beside each other and made two different kinds of peg, and argued amiably about which would split first.

Minerva watched their hands. Then the men at the other trunk, getting ready to lift. She stood up.

"Wait," she said.

They had already lifted.

It was only a short movement. The timber rolled against the prop beneath it, and a hand was still where the space had been. A sharp sound. Someone swore. The trunk went down again, and the man pulled his hand against his chest.

Minerva was beside him before anyone else moved.

She took his wrist, looked at the fingers, and went very still.

"Open it. Slowly."

"I can't—"

"Then don't. Show me what you can."

Her own hands were bandaged. He looked at them. At her fists, which had stopped being fists in time to hold him.

Mikasa had turned at the cry. She looked once, saw Minerva there, and turned back toward the trees. Stark stayed at his end of the dip.

"Take him in," Wakana said.

Minerva did.

She walked beside the man, holding his forearm so that he did not keep trying to use the hand. Back up the timber route, across the white line, past the new stakes, to the clinic. She did not hit the injury. She did not make anything shine.

Maomao looked up from a bowl.

"What happened?"

"A prop rolled."

"Hand on the wrong side?"

The man nodded.

"Sit."

Minerva stayed standing until Maomao looked at her too.

"You may sit as well. I haven't abolished chairs."



It was badly bruised. Maomao checked each finger, and the skin, and the places that hurt when she pressed where he did not expect her to press. He made a great many sounds and apologized for half of them.

"Those aren't the problem," she told him.

Minerva held a clean cloth in the bowl of cool water. She wrung it with one hand because the other had begun to ache, and Maomao noticed, and took it from her and did that part herself.

"Both of you," Maomao said. "Hands on the table."

Minerva put hers down reluctantly.

Maomao checked the old joints without a word. Then she put the cloth back in Minerva's good hand.

"Hold it there. Don't press."

For a while the three of them sat in the afternoon light, with a bowl and a wet cloth and a hand that was swelling despite everything they were doing for it.

Minerva's jaw tightened.

"It still hurts."

"Yes," said Maomao. "It was crushed."

"I know."

"And he won't be carrying anything tomorrow. Or the day after. We'll look again before we let him."

The man began to object. Minerva looked at him.

He stopped.

Maomao allowed herself a very small expression, and went to get the bandages.



The timber came home before dusk.

Three sound lengths. The shorter pieces. Not the hollow tree. Not the suspended one. Not the old tree with its high dark crown.

It took the rest of the afternoon. At the yard, Wakana had them lay each piece on blocks, clear of the damp ground. Byakuya wrote the lengths beside them in chalk. The cooper's pegs went in a covered box, where they could not be kicked into the earth and lost.

Senku came down from the workroom and stood over the haul.

He looked at it for a long time.

"That's one day," Byakuya said.

"I can see that."

"Don't look like that at it. We had a good day."

Senku looked at the three lengths again. Then he took the chalk and began to work out which part of the first gate they could actually brace with them.

Minerva came out of the clinic with a small basket of clean wraps for the next morning's party. Sekhmet was back on the cart, on her blanket, watching the yard through one half-open eye.

"Did you bring the bad one?" she asked.

"No," said Wakana.

"Good."

From the house, Momo called dinner.

Byakuya put down his chisel. The cooper pocketed his knife. Senku stayed over the chalk for another moment, until his father picked up the other end of the drawing and folded it toward him.

"It'll still be short after soup."

Senku took the drawing, and they went in.
