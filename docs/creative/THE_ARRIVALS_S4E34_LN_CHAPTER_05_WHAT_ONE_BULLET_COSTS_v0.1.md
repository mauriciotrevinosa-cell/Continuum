# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 26
## Chapter 11 — What One Bullet Costs

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 26
**Reading order:** 11
**Continuity state:** Day 236, morning and evening | a missing hinge pin raises a practical question about Mai's Construction | her daily bullet remains a real bodily expense, not a free hardware supply | ordinary iron is recovered instead | Yuka helps without inventing an obligation between them | Mai and Mau's cost-counting kinship continues in an ordinary repair; no protected family history is disclosed

*When: Day 236. The work cart has acquired a cupboard for the small tools. Its door will not stay on, and the person who last had the hinge pin is certain that he put it somewhere safe.*

The pin was somewhere safe.

Wakana had heard this from three people, each of whom named a different safe place. He had searched all three. Now he was looking at the hinge, holding the cupboard door up with his shoulder, while Yuka kept a bowl under it to catch anything else that fell out.

Nothing else fell out. That was almost more annoying.

Mai sat on the yard bench a little way away, with a cup between her palms. She had come out because it was quiet there at this hour. It had remained quiet for eleven minutes.

"Could you make one?" Yuka asked her.

Mai looked at the hinge.

"A pin."

"Just that little bit. If it isn't—" Yuka stopped. "If it's something you can do."

Wakana turned his head.

"I can get iron."

"I know," said Yuka quickly. "I wasn't saying you couldn't. I only thought—"

Mai put down the cup. Slowly. Her right hand shook a little as she took it away.

Yuka saw.

The bowl in her own hands became very still.

"Oh," she said.

"This morning," said Mai. "The usual one."

Yuka looked from the hand to Mai's face. There were a great many things she had heard about this woman before meeting her, and very few of them had involved sitting on a bench with a cup she had to put down carefully.

"Then not now," said Yuka.

"Not for that, anyway."

Mai nodded toward the cupboard. Wakana was still holding the door with his shoulder. He had become very interested in not interrupting.

"I'll find iron," he said.

"You said."

"Could somebody hold this?"

Yuka looked down at the bowl, then at the door, then set the bowl on the ground and put both hands where he showed her. The hinge held. The door did not fall.

Wakana stepped away with considerable care.



They found the missing pin in the grass.

Not immediately. First Wakana went to the workshop and brought back a piece of old iron he could cut down. Then he discovered that the hole had been damaged, and fetched another tool, and Byakuya came through carrying something much larger and stopped because three people staring at a cupboard was an irresistible event.

He asked whether anyone had looked in the grass.

Yuka said yes. Wakana said twice. Mai said she had watched both of them do it.

Byakuya put down what he was carrying and looked too.

For some time there were four people not finding a pin.

Then the white dog, who had been lying beside the bench through all of this, stood up and put its nose directly into Byakuya's ear.

Byakuya jerked away, put his hand down to steady himself, and found the pin under it.

He held it up.

"Helpful dog."

"She didn't find it," said Yuka.

"She helped."

The white dog sat down beside her and looked entirely satisfied with the arrangement.

Mai picked up her cup in both hands. The tea was cold now. She drank it anyway.



Wakana mended the hole. He put the recovered pin in, then added a small wooden turn-button so that the cupboard would stay shut while the cart moved. Yuka tried it three times, at his request, with increasing force.

On the third attempt he said, "Not that much."

"You said like the road."

"A reasonable road."

"Have you seen the road?"

Mai made a small sound into the cup.

Both looked at her.

"Nothing," she said.

When Wakana went back to the workshop, Yuka brought the bowl to the bench. Empty, except for dirt and a single bent tack that had nothing to do with the cupboard.

"I shouldn't have asked."

Mai looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because you—"

"Can say no?"

Yuka stopped.

"You asked," Mai said. "I answered. That's generally how it works."

Yuka sat on the other end of the bench. The white dog laid its chin on her knee with such force that she had to catch herself against the wood.

"I didn't know it would cost that much."

"Most people don't."

Mai turned the cup between her hands. The tremor was small. It had been larger earlier. She knew what it would be at lunch, and later, and by the time everyone sat down for dinner. None of that made the cup lighter right now.

"Your grandmother knows," she added.

Yuka looked toward the house, where Maki had gone in with Yuta a few minutes earlier.

"She didn't tell me."

"It's not her hand."

Yuka nodded, slowly.

For a while they sat without saying anything. The white dog's ear moved at the sound of the tools from the workshop. A small breeze lifted the hair from Mai's forehead, and she pushed it away with the hand that was not shaking.

Yuka reached for the cup.

Then stopped herself.

"Are you finished?"

Mai looked at the tea. Looked at Yuka.

"Yes."

Yuka took it.

She did not come back with another one. She washed it, set it where Mai could find it, and went to help Momo with lunch, the white dog at her heel.

Mai stayed on the bench for a little longer.



By evening, the cupboard door had stayed on for one full journey of the cart and come off on the second.

The hinge had held. The board had split.

Wakana put the two pieces on the long table after supper and stood looking at them with a concentration so unhappy that nobody made a joke.

Except Mai.

"The pin was excellent."

Wakana looked up.

"Yes."

"A beautiful pin."

"Yes."

"Glad we spent the morning finding it."

Marin, on the other side of the table, covered her mouth. Wakana looked at her, and the corners of his mouth moved despite himself.

Mau was at the far end, turning a wooden peg between his fingers. He had spent part of the afternoon sorting the sound ones from the split ones, in his blindfold, by touch. There was a rejected pile beside his elbow. It had become larger than he wanted it to be.

Mai sat across from him. She took one of the rejects. Her hand was still now.

"What's wrong with it?"

He showed her. A crack along the grain, almost to the point.

"You could make it smaller," she said.

"Wakana said not for a brace."

"Then not for a brace."

She set it down. Mau put it back on the rejected pile, where it would become something else if someone found a use for it. If nobody did, it would burn.

He looked at her hand.

"Better?"

"The same as yesterday."

He understood the answer. Better by evening was not the same as cheaper.

"The cupboard?" she asked him.

"I didn't make it."

"I noticed. There's still a house."

Mau laughed. It came out before he could decide whether he was allowed, and Mai looked down at the peg with the expression of someone who had not intended to be funny and would prefer not to have it reported.

He took the next piece from the basket. Rolled it beneath his thumb.

At the other end of the table Wakana had started drawing a door with the grain running the other way. Marin leaned over his shoulder, her hand resting between his shoulder blades. She said something too quiet for Mai to hear, and Wakana shook his head, and then nodded.

Mai picked up another peg.

"This one's good."

"That was the other pile."

She put it back.

"Then your system needs labels."

Mau pushed the empty scrap of paper toward her. She took the pencil, and wrote *BAD* on it, and put it beside him rather than beside the pile.

He looked at the label.

"Very helpful."

"You're welcome."

She stayed until the basket was empty. When she left, she took the rejected wood to the kindling box without asking whether anyone had decided it was useless.
