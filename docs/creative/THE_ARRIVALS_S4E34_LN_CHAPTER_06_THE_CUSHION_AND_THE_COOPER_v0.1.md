# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 26
## Chapter 12 — The Cushion and the Cooper

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 26
**Reading order:** 12
**Continuity state:** Days 238–239 | the cooper uses his own kept coin to call his daughter; the holder alone hears the question | Arrival House receives her through the ordinary register | Umaru finds the long-kept fort application; one real visit and cushion | father and daughter leave together on an arranged guarded route, without a promise never to return | the native washing-court remnant, register girl and Lise also relocate ordinarily | the bean-girl and her mother remain the last refuge family

*When: Day 238, from the morning's petition to the next day's guarded departure. The cooper has been making arrangements with Hollis, not packing in secret. The one refuge family in the north-road cottage is still here.*

The cooper put the coin on the table beside his knife.

Fern had seen him turn it over in his hand many times. At meals, at the yard, once while he waited for a hoop to cool. He always put it away before she could ask. She had stopped asking a long time ago.

Now he left it where it was.

"I've got a place," he said.

Fern put down her pen.

"Hollis found work along his route. Not a promise of work. A person who needs barrels. There's room above the shop." He looked at the knife rather than the coin. "I can pay for a little of the journey. Work for the rest. We've agreed."

"And your daughter?"

"That's why."

He had not said it bitterly. That made it harder to answer. He looked past Fern toward the yard, where there was a stack of timber and the sound of something being hammered. People were putting a great deal of themselves into that sound.

"I know you're building it," he said.

"We are."

"I hope it works."

Fern nodded.

He rubbed one thumb across the side of his knife. It was the part he touched when he was measuring something without a rule.

"But I've had months to get used to every noise. She hasn't. She'd wake up here, and the first thing I'd tell her is what to do when the bell rings." He shook his head. "I don't want that to be the first thing."

Fern looked at the coin.

"Jinshi knows?"

"I told him yesterday. About the room. About her."

"Then I'll put the date on the List."

She opened it. The cooper watched her write. When she had finished, he took back the knife first. Then the coin.



He used it alone, in the place where he kept his tools.

He sat on the low stool with the morning light coming through the door. A barrel stood beside his knee, unfinished. A hoop rested against the wall. He turned the coin in his hand once, the way he had at dinner on so many evenings when somebody else's loved person was coming.

Then he said his daughter's name.

Nobody else heard it. Nobody needed to.

The coin warmed. He closed his fingers. Somewhere that was not the doorway, a voice asked the question he had been told it would ask, with her name in it.

"Yes," he said.

It became warmer still.

Then there was nothing in his hand, and a pink place across the palm, and he sat looking at it for a while before he stood up.



She came in the afternoon.

There was no procession waiting outside Arrival House. Jinshi had learned a great deal about the difference between making a person welcome and making them face everyone at once. The register girl had clean water ready. The room was quiet. The cooper was on the step, standing up every time someone moved inside.

When the inner door opened he did not get up at once.

He had got up so many times already that for a moment he did not trust it.

Then he heard her.

"Dad?"

He went in.

The register girl looked away. Jinshi stepped aside. Neither left the door closed between him and his daughter while there was anything to write.

She was about eleven. She had a hand raised as if she had been reaching for something a moment before. She saw him, and the hand stayed there, and her face changed very quickly from bewilderment to anger to something too large for either.

"Where were you?"

He knelt because it was the fastest way to get his face level with hers.

"Here."

"Where's here?"

"I'll tell you."

"You were gone."

"I know."

She hit his shoulder with one small closed fist. Not hard. He did not move away. Then her hand opened and caught his shirt, and she was against him, crying so fiercely that he put his own face in her hair and could not have answered another question if anyone had asked.

Jinshi went out onto the step. The register girl gave them the room.

They waited with the book shut.



Umaru found the application in LATER.

It was under several things she was not going to think about this afternoon. She lifted them carefully, kept their order, and slid out a sheet with a small mark where the cooper had pressed too hard on the pencil.

She had asked for it a long time ago. He had filled it in because she had stood over him until he did. Then she had kept it, because somebody ought to keep things like that.

"She's here," she said to Suika.

Suika looked up from the cushion she was mending.

"Then we need this one."

"That one's NEXT."

"I know."

They looked at each other. Then Suika tied off the thread and held out the cushion.



The daughter did not want to leave her father's hand when they reached the house.

Umaru did not tell her she had to.

She stood at the arch of the fort in her sash, with the application held in both hands and Milim behind her, and explained the important part first.

"There's a cushion."

The girl looked past her. At the curtain. At the heap of cushions and people and things that belonged to a room even when the room did not have proper walls.

"For me?"

"Your dad filled in the form."

The girl looked up at him.

He looked at the form in Umaru's hands as if he had forgotten that part of his life had continued to exist.

"She was very insistent," he said.

"It's a good form," said Umaru.

Milim leaned forward.

"You can bring him in."

Umaru turned her head.

Milim looked at her with enormous innocence.

"One visit. She's holding him."

Umaru considered the hands. The daughter was gripping two of her father's fingers. He had not tried to pull them away.

"Temporary visitor," said Umaru, and moved aside.

The cooper had made things for the fort. Pegs, small repairs, a better fastening where somebody had pulled a loop loose. He had not sat in it like this, on a cushion too low for his knees, with his daughter pressed against his side and a small serious girl explaining that the toll was waived because it was her first day.

The daughter listened to all of it.

Then she asked, "Who are you keeping out?"

Umaru opened her mouth.

Closed it.

"People who won't ask first," she said eventually.

The girl thought about that.

"All right."

Suika brought bread. Ori brought a cup that was not too full. The white dog put its head through the curtain, and the daughter pulled back against her father. Yuka stopped the dog at once.

"She doesn't have to come in."

"Is she yours?"

"Yes."

"She's big."

"Yes," Yuka agreed.

The girl watched her. After a while she held out the back of her hand, but not all the way. The dog waited. Her nose moved. Nothing else.

She looked at Yuka.

"Can she stay there?"

"Sure."

So the dog stayed in the doorway, and the girl stayed on her cushion, and nobody needed to settle anything more than that.



At supper she sat beside her father at the long table.

He told her what they were going to do the next morning. A cart. Hollis. A place farther along the ordinary road. Work in the shop and a room above it. He had explained some of it in Arrival House, but he explained it again, because she asked again, and he understood that she would need to.

"You're coming too?"

"Yes."

"The whole way?"

"The whole way."

She ate another mouthful of soup.

Across the room the bean-girl was helping Aira carry the empty bowls. Her mother sat near the kitchen door, watching her. Their cottage on the north road had not been packed. Nobody asked them to change that because somebody else was leaving.

Lise had a small bundle beside her own chair. She was going with the last of the washing-court women as far as their arranged stop along the route. She touched the cup Rem had put in front of her.

"There's washing there too," she said.

Rem nodded, and poured a little more tea.

The register girl would go with them in the morning. She had stayed late to finish the daughter's line in the book and to put the papers in order for Jinshi. The other lodging helpers had made their own arrangements with the departing group; their keys and unfinished tasks were going to Jinshi and the house rota. She had work to go to, and somebody expecting her. That was what she said when Fern asked. Fern wrote down the journey, not a return through a door.

Hollis's hired road partners were still here, under the terms Fern had written for them. She kept those pages separate from the departing household's arrangements.



The next morning the cooper checked every strap on the cart himself.

Hollis let him. He stood with his hat in his hands and waited while the man tightened something that did not need tightening, then loosened it again, afraid of breaking it.

There were guards. There was an agreed first stretch, and a place to meet the next people who knew the road. None of that made the road harmless. Nobody told the daughter it did.

She stood beside her father with her cushion against her chest.

Umaru had given it to her at the step.

"You can take it."

"Doesn't it belong here?"

"It was for you."

The girl had looked at her father, and he had nodded, and now she was holding it with both arms as if the cart might leave without it if she put it down.

The cooper shook Wakana's hand. Then Byakuya's. He gave Fern the last small tally of pegs in the box, because they had been arguing over the number and he had counted them before dawn.

Mau and Rimuru were by the open way. He went to both of them.

"Thank you," he said.

Mau nodded. Rimuru looked toward the cart, at the girl with her cushion.

"Write to us. Through Hollis."

"I will."

He looked back toward the yard. At the workshop door. At the house, where somebody had opened a window and a quarrel about the breakfast pot was already going on.

"I'll finish that wheel when I'm next up," he said to Wakana.

"I've marked it."

"I saw. Wrong side."

Wakana shook his head. The cooper smiled, briefly, then took his daughter's hand and helped her onto the cart.

At the watched opening she turned to see the fort one last time. It was behind the wall of the house, of course. She could not see it from here. Umaru stood on the step instead, her sash crooked, and lifted her hand.

The daughter lifted one back, above the cushion.

Then the cart went on, with the guards walking beside it, along the road the cooper had chosen.
