# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 26
## Chapter 13 — Someone Who Speaks

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 26
**Reading order:** 13
**Continuity state:** Day 241 | the north gate is a timber frame and one hung leaf, not a completed defence | two horned strangers approach beyond the lime | claimed injured mother at the bend; Himmel recognises danger and maintains distance | the bean-girl hears the word from her cottage | Frieren identifies a familiar source-world enemy without equating it with Diablo or the fear-born devil | no crossing, invitation or assault yet

*When: Day 241, late afternoon. The first north-gate leaf is hung and still does not shut properly. The south earthwork has gaps. One refuge mother and one daughter remain in the cottages; the cooper and his daughter have already gone. The paid road guards are still working their agreed routes.*

Stark was underneath the gate when somebody said hello.

Not underneath a fallen gate. Underneath a hung one, which Wakana had assured him was different, with his shoulder pressed against the lower rail while two people above him argued about a wooden pin. His axe was leaning against the inside post. He had been told to hold the leaf level. He had held it level for eleven minutes.

“If you could lift it a little,” said Wakana.

“You said level.”

“It needs to be level slightly higher.”

Stark closed his eyes.

“Hello,” said the person on the road again.

Himmel put down the mallet.

The road beyond the gate was ordinary summer dust. A strip of lime crossed it past the new posts, a few paces outside the timber frame. Beyond that was the beginning of the north slope, then the low bend where the road went behind a bank of grass. Two people were standing between the line and the bend.

The nearer one had a pale coat, worn at the cuffs, and a pair of short curved horns above his ears. His hands were empty. The other was shorter, in a dark cloak, standing far enough behind him that the first one's shoulder concealed part of the face.

They had not crossed the lime.

Himmel's expression changed so little that Wakana did not notice.

Stark noticed. He stopped complaining.

“Wakana,” said Himmel. “Take the wedge out before he moves.”

“The weight will—”

“I'll hold it.”

Himmel set his shoulder where Stark's had been. Wakana crouched and knocked the wedge free. Stark came out, ducking under the rail, and took his axe off the post.

The stranger watched the exchange with interest.

“We're looking for a healer,” he said.

Brenna came round the inside of the frame with a coil of rope over one arm. She stopped when she saw Stark holding his axe. The scar above her left eye pulled when she frowned.

“Stay where you are,” she said to the road.

“We are.”

He said it agreeably. As if they had asked him to wait while someone finished setting a table.

“What happened?” said Brenna.

Himmel looked at her.

She saw the look. Her hand moved from the rope to the crossbow at her hip, without taking it out.

“Our mother can't walk,” said the stranger. “She's at the bend. We heard you help people.”

The second figure turned its head toward the trees.

Nothing moved there. A fly went round the edge of the new gate leaf and landed on the warm timber.

Wakana was still crouched beside the hinge.

“I can get—”

“Fern,” said Himmel. “Get Fern.”

He did not look away from the road.

Wakana stood up. He looked at the horns, then at Himmel, and opened his mouth. Himmel shook his head once. Wakana went inside, walking quickly until the gate was behind him, and then running.

Brenna set the rope down.

“How far at the bend?” she asked.

“Where you can't see from here.”

“I know where the bend is.”

The stranger smiled.

“Then you'll know where to come.”

Stark moved a pace sideways. Clear of the hanging leaf, clear of Himmel's shoulder. The axe was down by his leg, not lifted. His fingers were very tight on it.

“Bring her where we can see her,” said Himmel.

“She can't walk.”

“There are two of you.”

The stranger glanced behind him, as if the second figure had only just become relevant.

“She's heavy.”

For a moment the only sound was the mallet Wakana had dropped, rolling very slowly down a loose board inside the frame. It reached the earth and stopped.

Brenna heard footsteps on the street behind her. She did not turn. She put her free hand out behind her, palm back.

“No closer than the post.”

The footsteps stopped.

Wren. Her bow was strung. She had been checking the fletching at the water barrel, and the rope going down had been enough to bring her. She looked once at Brenna's face and then out at the road.

No one on their side called the two visitors monsters.

No one offered soup.

Stark was thinking about the soup anyway. About the pot Momo had carried to this same line, and the people who had been afraid to touch it. About Cobb standing with a bowl in his hands. About the first step across.

He did not move his axe.

“Mother,” said the shorter figure.

It was the first word it had spoken.

Its voice was thin and clear. It looked toward the cottages as it said it, not toward the bend.

In the last occupied cottage, a shutter opened.

Brenna saw Himmel see it.

The bean-girl was standing on something inside, high enough for her face to reach the window. Her mother appeared behind her almost at once and drew her back. A small hand remained on the sill for a second, then disappeared.

The woman did not open the door.

“You have children,” said the nearer stranger.

“You said someone needs help,” said Brenna.

“Yes.”

“Then look at us.”

He looked back at her, obligingly.

Himmel eased the gate leaf down onto the waiting block. He took his shoulder away slowly, making certain it would stay. Only then did he step forward to stand beside Stark.

Not beyond the lime. There was still room between them and it.

“Where did you come from?” he asked.

The stranger named no place.

“Down the road.”

“Before the road.”

“Somewhere else.”

Wren's thumb moved once along the wood of her bow.

It was an answer many people here could have given. Brenna had heard versions of it from frightened travellers who did not know which names would get them killed. She could not make a verdict out of that alone.

She could make one out of Himmel's face.

He was not confused. He was waiting for something he already understood to confirm itself.

“Will you help?” said the stranger.

“Nobody is coming down the road with you,” said Himmel.

The smile did not leave the stranger's face.

“You haven't seen her.”

“That's true.”

“She might die.”

Stark's jaw tightened.

Himmel heard the breath he took. He did not put a hand on him. He only shifted enough that Stark could see him without taking his eyes entirely off the road.

“Stay with me,” he said, very quietly.

“I am.”

“I know.”

The stranger tilted his head at the exchange. Then he looked past them again, toward the cottage window.

“Mother,” said the shorter one.

This time it sounded almost exactly like a question.

Fern arrived at a walk.

She had run most of the way, but at the water barrel she had stopped running. Her staff was in her hand. Frieren was beside her, in the hoodie she had put on for the late shade, with her own staff resting against her shoulder.

Brenna felt something in Himmel's stance loosen.

Only a little. Only the part that had been waiting for them.

Frieren looked at the two on the road.

The nearer stranger looked at her. At the ears. At the staff. At her small figure beside Fern.

His smile changed.

“An elf,” he said.

Frieren said nothing.

“We asked for a healer,” he said. “Our mother—”

“I heard.”

Her voice was so ordinary that Brenna looked at her.

Frieren had spoken in that voice about cracked cups. About a badly copied spell. About the place Mau left a book so she could not open the closet properly. There was no anger in it now. No surprise.

She moved to Himmel's other side. Fern stopped half a pace behind her, with a clear view past Stark's shoulder.

The stranger's eyes went to Fern.

Then back to Frieren.

“There's very little of you,” he said.

“Enough to stay here,” said Frieren.

The shorter figure was no longer looking at the cottages. It was looking at her staff.

At the house end of the street, a hammer started up again. Someone had not heard. Three blows, then a pause, then another. Summer work, on the other side of a conversation nobody could hear clearly from there.

Frieren glanced at the hanging gate leaf.

“Don't try to shut that in a hurry,” she said to Himmel. “It'll fall.”

“I know.”

“Move the workers back.”

Brenna turned her head just enough to speak to Wren.

“The barrel. Everyone behind it.”

Wren went. Without lowering her bow.

The stranger took a step toward the bend.

Not toward the line.

“We'll wait,” he said. “Until you come.”

“You can leave,” said Frieren.

He smiled at her once more. Then he turned and walked up the road, and the smaller one followed. Their boots made very little sound in the dust.

At the bend the shorter figure looked back. Toward the cottage. It lifted one empty hand.

The mother closed the shutter.

Frieren stayed where she was until both figures were out of sight.

Then she turned to Brenna.

“Call Anko,” she said. “Tell her exactly what they said.”

“What are they?”

Frieren looked toward the bend again.

“Demons,” she said. “From my world.”

Stark sat down on the hinge block before anyone could ask him to move it.

Fern looked at him.

“I'm not holding that gate again,” he said.

She did not tell him to get up.

She stood beside the block with her staff in both hands, watching the empty road while his fingers stopped shaking.
