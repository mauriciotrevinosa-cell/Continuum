# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 26
## Chapter 14 — A Word for Mother

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 26
**Reading order:** 14
**Continuity state:** Evening of Day 241 | Frieren, Fern and Himmel explain the specific threat witnessed outside the north gate | Diablo's source-world kind is distinguished from these predators and from the fear-born devil | claimed third patient remains unverified; no healer dispatched blindly | Aira stays with the last cottage; a visible-road encounter is arranged for daylight with workers withdrawn | Mau does not investigate through his eyes or memories; no new protection rule

*When: The evening of Day 241. The two figures have withdrawn beyond the north bend. No one has gone after them. The north gate leaf rests on its block, and the night watch has moved far enough inside the marked boundary to see both the road and the last cottage.*

Anko asked for the words before she asked for the horns.

She sat at the kitchen end with her notebook open and a bowl of Momo's stew cooling beside it. Brenna stood on the other side of the table. Her rope was still round one wrist. She had forgotten it was there until Momo touched it, and then she unwound it and put it on a chair.

“Exactly,” said Anko.

Brenna repeated what she could. Hello. Healer. Our mother can't walk. At the bend. Where you can't see from here. She repeated the smaller one's word twice, because it had said it twice.

“And when it said that,” said Anko, “it was looking at the cottage.”

“Both times.”

Anko wrote it down.

“Could be checking for someone who'd understand.”

“Could be,” said Brenna.

Frieren was on the bench beside Fern. She had her bowl between her hands and had not lifted the spoon. Her staff was against the wall within reach.

“It was checking which word worked,” she said.

Momo stopped at the table with the ladle.

“Worked how?”

“The shutter opened.”

For a moment no one said anything.

Then Momo set the ladle down, very carefully, on the cloth beside the pot.

“You know them,” said Anko.

“I know their kind.”

“These two?”

“No.”

Anko nodded and put a short line under what she had written. She did not turn *their kind* into *everything about them*.

Himmel stood near the window, where he could see the street. Stark was eating opposite Fern, much faster than usual, as if he wanted the plate empty before somebody asked him a question.

“They're predators,” said Frieren. “In my world, they learned to speak to us. It makes hunting easier.”

“Even the small one?” said Momo.

“Its size doesn't tell you its age.”

“Or whether it's helpless,” said Fern.

Stark put his spoon down.

“I know that,” he said.

“I wasn't correcting you.”

He looked at her. She was looking at his hands. There was a splinter in the side of his thumb from the lower rail. He tucked the thumb beneath the table.

“Would you have gone?” said Anko.

Stark looked back at his plate.

“If Himmel hadn't been there,” he said. “Maybe. I don't know.”

“Then I'm glad he was.”

She wrote nothing beside Stark's name.

At the counter, Diablo was drying cups.

He had been there through the account, placing each one on the shelf in a neat row. He put the last cup down and turned.

“An unfortunate name to share,” he said.

Brenna looked at him.

Then at Frieren.

“I wasn't—”

“I know,” said Diablo.

There was no offence in his voice. That made Brenna feel worse for a moment, until he took Momo's ladle and wiped the handle where stew had run down it, and she remembered how often he had done that.

“He's different,” said Frieren.

Diablo's eyebrows rose slightly.

“You've been very inconvenient about it,” she added.

Momo made a noise that might have been a laugh if the room had been less quiet.

“I shall treasure the assessment.”

“Don't.”

Fern picked up her spoon.

The small movement let other people move too. Brenna sat. Anko tasted her stew. Himmel came away from the window to take the cup Momo had left for him.

“Not that thing in the woods either,” said Anko.

“No,” said Frieren.

“It talks.”

“Talking isn't what makes them the same.”

Anko looked at the page. The voices at the line, the words from the towns. Then today's polite request.

She turned to a clean place.

“Separate page,” she said, and wrote the heading.



The bean-girl's mother came in with Aira after the first bowls were cleared.

The child had gone to Momo's kitchen stool with Vamola, who was showing her how to roll the sleeves of an apron without losing the ribbon. There were three people between the stool and the door. The mother counted them without meaning to.

“She wanted to go,” she said.

Aira took the rope off the empty chair and sat beside her.

“She heard it ask for its mother. She said we should get her.”

Momo's hands stopped on the apron tie.

“I held her back,” said the woman quickly. “I didn't open the door.”

“I know,” said Aira.

“But I thought about it too.”

No one answered at once.

The mother looked at Frieren. Not at the staff. At her face.

“What if there is someone at the bend?”

“We haven't seen anyone,” said Frieren.

“That isn't the same.”

“No.”

The woman looked surprised by the answer.

Himmel set his cup down.

“We can keep looking for a person without following those two,” he said. “Both things at once.”

“From here?”

“From places we can see each other. In daylight. Not a healer walking round a blind corner.”

Anko rubbed the side of her nose with the end of her pen.

“No claim that the lime will sort it for us,” she said. “We don't know that.”

“I wasn't going to try,” said Frieren.

“I mean for everyone else.”

Frieren looked past her at the people who had gathered near the kitchen door, then at the child with the rolled-up sleeves.

“Don't tell her every person asking for help is lying,” she said.

The mother's face changed.

“What do I tell her?”

Frieren took a moment over it.

“To get you.”

“That's all?”

“For now.”

The mother put her hands over each other on the table. She had been washing dishes. The skin round her knuckles was still pale from the water.

“I can do that,” she said.



They laid the north-road plan out on the cleared end.

Not the whole settlement plan. Just the strip Wakana had copied for the gate hinges, with the road and the bank and the nearest cottages marked round it. Brenna turned it the right way for where she would stand. Anko set the salt pot on the bend so it would stop curling up.

“They can stand here,” said Himmel, touching the open road beyond the lime. “If they come back. We ask them to show us the third person. No one follows a voice into the trees.”

“Himmel and Stark at the front,” said Fern. “Not alone. Frieren and I behind. Mikasa on the bank side.”

“I know the bank,” said Brenna. “I can keep Wren where she sees past you.”

“Workers back at the barrel,” said Anko. “Not on the unfinished walk.”

Wakana looked at the sketch.

“I'd like everyone off that walk anyway.”

“Then you get your wish,” said Anko.

At the other end of the table, Mau had been repairing a torn handle on a supply bag. He had listened without coming down to the map. Now he put the needle through from the wrong side and caught it on his fingertip.

“Damn.”

Frieren turned.

He held up the bag, then his hand, as if establishing which of them had won.

“I'm not volunteering,” he said. “I'm losing to this.”

She looked at the crooked stitches.

“You're using too much thread.”

“It looked stronger.”

“It's going to be a knot.”

“It already is.”

He looked toward Anko.

“Do you need the workers moved before breakfast?”

“Before dawn,” she said. “You can tell the ones in the house.”

“Okay.”

He put the bag down. Nobody asked him to read through anything with the eyes he now kept filtered. Nobody asked him to identify a life in the grass. The map already had people who knew the road, and people who knew this enemy, and one badly weighted salt pot.

Frieren went back to it.

“If they attack,” said Fern, “we stop them there. We don't chase.”

Himmel nodded.

“And if they leave?” said Brenna.

“We keep the watch,” said Anko. “And the workers don't hurry back out because breakfast got boring.”

Brenna smiled without much humour.

“I'll tell them that part.”



Afterwards, Fern found Stark at the water barrel.

He was checking the axe edge in the last of the light. He had already checked it inside. There was nothing wrong with it.

She took his free hand.

“What?” he said.

“The splinter.”

“It isn't bad.”

“Hold still.”

He did. She pulled it out with the small tweezers she had brought from Maomao, and wiped his thumb, and kept holding his hand while the sting went away.

The cottage shutter was closed across the road.

“It knew that would make me want to go,” he said.

Fern looked at the window.

“You didn't.”

“Because someone else knew first.”

She turned his hand over to check that no wood remained.

“Then tomorrow,” she said, “stand where I can see you.”

He nodded.

Inside the gate frame, a pin worked loose and dropped into the dust. They both looked down at it.

“We should tell Wakana,” said Stark.

“You should.”

“Why me?”

“You're still holding the axe. He'll take you seriously.”

Stark looked at the pin again. Then at her.

“You're laughing at me.”

“A little,” said Fern.

She did not let go of his hand until they had gone back inside.
