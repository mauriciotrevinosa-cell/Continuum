# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 26
## Chapter 16 — The Cup She Kept

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 26
**Reading order:** 16
**Continuity state:** Evening of Day 242 | the last refuge mother tells Aira that she and her daughter choose to leave | her Day 219 decision to stay remains sincere; the new threat exploits her child's impulse to help | no accusation, exile or promise of safety elsewhere | Hollis arranges the family's first booked night at the guarded southern-ford lodging used by the road crew, with onward housing still to choose | Aira and Mikasa agree to the known first leg only; Brenna and Dunmore take the onward cart | one mother, one child

*When: The evening of Day 242. The north-road encounter is over. The cottage has not been damaged. Aira has told its mother exactly what Himmel found beneath the cloth, and has come back after supper with the child's apron, washed and folded.*

The cup was on the bed.

Most of the other things were on the table. Clothes folded in two piles. A blanket rolled badly and tied with the cord that had once held up the loose end of the shutter. A little sack of beans. Three wooden spoons. The cup sat apart from them, blue round the lip, with a chip on the side where a thumb went.

Aira stopped in the doorway.

The bean-girl's mother was sitting on the edge of the bed with a shirt in her lap. She looked up.

“I haven't told her yet.”

Aira looked at the things again.

Then she set the folded apron down on the chair.

“She's in the kitchen,” she said. “Momo said she's helping with tomorrow.”

“She'll eat half of tomorrow.”

“Vamola's watching her.”

The mother smiled, briefly.

“Then they'll eat it together.”

Aira sat on the other end of the bed. The mattress dipped toward her. The cup slid a little, and the mother caught it with one hand without looking.

For a while they listened to the ordinary noises outside. A barrow being pushed back to the timber yard. Somebody calling for the other end of a rope. A hammer, slower than yesterday. The village was still there. The cottage was still there. There had not even been a broken window.

“I said we'd stay,” said the mother.

“I know.”

“I meant it.”

“I know that too.”

The woman looked at her.

Aira was sitting with her knees together and her hands flat on them. The old blade was not in her lap. She had left it by the door, where it would not frighten the child if she came back early. Aira had been doing that for days now, taking it away before sitting down to hear about something small.

“You stood right here,” said the woman. “With that thing outside. And then you went at the titan with a sword too short to reach it. I haven't forgotten.”

“You don't have to tell me.”

“I do.”

Her hand tightened round the cup.

“Because I don't want you to think this morning made all that into nothing.”

Aira looked at the pale mark on her own palm where the sword hilt pressed when she held it too long.

“It didn't,” she said.

The woman bent her head.

“When the big thing came, I could tell her where to run. When the little titan ran, Eren took her hand. You were on the step. I could see the danger. It was there, and we were here, and everyone knew which way to stand.”

She put the cup down between them.

“Yesterday she heard somebody ask for a mother. She tried to climb off the box before I could get to her. She said she knew where the healers were. She was going to show them.”

Aira did not look up.

“And I said no. I said stay with me. But I thought about it too. About someone lying down the road while we shut a window.”

“There wasn't anyone.”

“I know. You told me.”

Outside, the barrow squeaked to a stop.

The woman rubbed her forehead with both hands.

“I don't know how to teach her the difference fast enough.”

“Frieren said to come and get you.”

“And I can teach her that. I will.” She looked at the packed table. “I just don't want every afternoon to be the next time she has to get it right.”

Aira breathed in slowly.

There were things she could say. *I'll be here.* She would be, as often as she could. *Nobody gets past me.* She had said that already, at this door, and meant it with everything she had. She could say it again and make the woman watch her trying to turn it into enough.

Instead she asked, “Where do you want to go?”

The mother's shoulders moved. Not relief, exactly. Something giving way beneath it.

“Somewhere I can be a woman who needs work,” she said. “For a while. Not the last family that stayed.”



Hollis was mending a strap at Arrival House when they found him.

He put the strap down as soon as he saw the cup wrapped in a shirt under the woman's arm.

“Tomorrow?” he asked.

“If it can be done,” she said. “If it can't, we wait.”

He looked at Aira, then back at the woman.

“There's a room at the ford. Where my people stop. I've paid for beds there tomorrow, whether I fill them or not.” He rubbed his hand over his jaw. “You can have that first night. After that we ask. A kitchen that needs hands. A room someone can spare. I won't tell you I've found a house when I haven't.”

“I'm not asking you to.”

“The road isn't safe because you walk away from this one.”

“I know.”

He nodded, slowly. She had said it in a way that did not invite reassurance.

Brenna had come in behind them. She stayed by the open door, listening. Dunmore was outside with the cart, and Hollis called him in.

They spoke over the small table in the intake hall. No great map. Hollis drew the known turnings on the back of a spoiled packing list and put his thumb where they would stop.

“First leg to the ford,” said Mikasa, when Aira went to get her. “We walk you there. We don't promise the next three days in one sentence.”

“Brenna and I have the cart from there,” said Dunmore. “Daylight. Together. Not sent up a lane on their own.”

“And if the road's wrong?” said the mother.

“We turn back,” said Mikasa.

The woman looked at Aira.

“You can?”

“Yes.”

She did not ask who had the right to let her. Nobody at the table made her ask.

Jinshi wrote the first stop on a scrap for her to keep. He asked whether she wanted the cottage shut or left ready for the night in case they returned. She looked at the words for a long time before answering.

“Ready,” she said. “Until you hear.”

Jinshi nodded.

“No one will put your things out.”

“There won't be many.”

“Even so.”



The bean-girl did not cry when her mother told her.

That made it worse for a little while.

She sat on the bed beside the cup and listened, with her knees under the hem of her nightshirt. She looked at the table, then at the rolled blanket, then at Aira standing by the door.

“Tomorrow?”

“In the morning,” said her mother.

“Before porridge?”

“After some bread.”

“Momo's bread?”

“Yes.”

She considered that.

“Is Aira coming?”

“To the ford,” said Aira.

“Then you go home?”

Aira sat down on the floor in front of her, so she would not have to look up.

“Then I come back here.”

The child put a finger in the chipped place on the cup.

“Can we come back?”

Her mother looked at Aira, almost helplessly.

“You can ask us to walk the road with you,” said Aira. “When there's a way.”

“You won't have to fight the tall one again.”

Aira swallowed.

“No,” she said. “Not that one.”

“Your sword worked.”

“It did.”

The child was quiet for a long time.

Then she looked up at her mother.

“Are we going because I opened the window?”

“No.”

The word came so quickly that the child flinched. Her mother put the packed shirt down and knelt beside Aira.

“No,” she said again, more softly. “You didn't make us go anywhere. You did what I said. You stayed with me.”

“But I wanted to get its mother.”

“I know.”

“We should help.”

The woman's face crumpled. She caught it before it became something the child would have to fix.

“We can still help people,” she said. “You get me first. Remember?”

The girl nodded.

“You get me first,” said her mother. “And then I do the next part.”

The child leaned forward off the mattress, and her mother put both arms round her, and for a while there was no room for anything else between them.

Aira stayed on the floor.



Momo came down later with the bread wrapped for morning and a little pot with a cloth tied over it.

“For the first stop,” she said.

“I can't pay you.”

Momo looked annoyed.

“You can give Hollis the pot when you're done.”

“She means thank you,” said Aira.

“I know what she means. She can still give him the pot.”

The mother laughed. It caught in the middle and came out wet. Momo put the bread on the table and pretended not to hear the difference.

The child had fallen asleep before she arrived, sideways across the bed where the cup had been. Her mother picked the cup up from the floor and turned it over in her hands.

“I should leave this,” she said. “The handle will break on the cart.”

“Wrap it,” said Momo.

“It's a bad cup.”

Momo looked at the blue lip. At the chip. At the place the woman had been holding it through the whole evening.

“I've got worse.”

She took the spare cloth from round the bread, folded it into the cup, then wrapped the rest round the outside. She did it briskly, as if packaging an argument so it could not start again.

“There.”

The mother put it in the middle of the clothes.

Aira stayed until the lamp was nearly out, holding the blanket away from the child's face while the woman packed the last things round her. When there was nothing left to do, she went to the door and picked up the old blade.

“I won't knock early,” she said. “I'll wait here.”

“I know,” said the mother.

Outside, Aira sat down on the step. The stone was still warm from the day.

After a little while Momo came out and sat beside her, with her skirt tucked under her knees. She did not ask Aira to come back to the house. She took out the heel of bread she had saved in her apron and broke it in two.

Aira took the smaller piece.

Momo took it back and gave her the larger one.

They ate without talking while the light under the cottage door went out.
