# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 26
## Chapter 17 — One Family

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 26
**Reading order:** 17
**Continuity state:** Day 243 | the last refuge mother and her daughter leave voluntarily by the south road, one family of two | Aira and Mikasa walk the known half-day first leg with the guarded cart | Brenna and Dunmore receive responsibility for the ford lodging and onward arrangements; Hollis remains at the settlement | Aira sees them settled at the first booked stop, then returns with Mikasa by evening | the child may return when an escorted route is available; no unconditional safety or immediate-return promise | Ring Four's remaining refuge household is gone; core residents and paid route relationships remain

*When: Day 243, morning to evening. Hollis's guarded cart has a place for one woman and one child. Aira and Mikasa are taking the familiar first leg to the southern ford and returning the same day; the onward road belongs to Brenna and Dunmore. The cottage will be shut, not reassigned.*

The bean-girl came out carrying the sack of beans.

It was too large for her. It hung against her knees, and she had to walk round it, and after four steps she stopped and put it down with a face of great seriousness.

“It's heavy.”

“I said I would take it,” said her mother.

“I'm helping.”

Aira crouched beside the sack.

“We could do it together.”

The girl considered her qualifications.

“You have to take that side.”

Aira took that side. They carried the beans as far as the cart at the south end, very slowly, with the girl's mother behind them carrying three things that would have fitted much more easily in two hands if her daughter had allowed it.

The road through the settlement was awake.

Rem was bringing back a tray from Arrival House. She stepped aside, saw the sack between them, and looked at the mother. The mother nodded. Rem said nothing to stop them. She took the rolled blanket off the woman's elbow and walked with her instead.

At the corner by the house, the Fastest Child was waiting with something folded in both hands.

She gave it to the bean-girl without speaking.

It was a small scrap of cloth with a door drawn on it in charcoal. The drawing had six hinges and no handle. Above it, in letters Umaru had plainly helped with, someone had written *STILL ALLOWED*.

The bean-girl looked at it.

“It's got too many hinges.”

“It's a strong door.”

“Where do you open it?”

The Fastest Child looked alarmed.

Ori, behind her, bent down and drew a handle with the last bit of charcoal.

“There.”

“Now it's got a hole,” said the Fastest Child.

“The handle is on it. It isn't a hole.”

The two children inspected the repair. The bean-girl nodded and put the cloth in the little pouch at her waist, away from the sack.

Then she put her arms round the other girl so suddenly that they both nearly fell over the beans.

Ori caught the sack. Aira caught the girls.

For a moment there were too many elbows. Then they untangled themselves, and the Fastest Child stepped back with her face very stiff, and the bean-girl took her side of the sack again.

“We have to go,” she said. “It's heavy.”



The cart was mostly ordinary things.

Empty crates going back. Tools that needed mending somewhere they had a proper forge. A bundle of packing straw. Enough room beside the driver's board for the woman and child, and their blanket and clothes, and the cup wrapped in the middle.

Dunmore lifted the bean sack in. The girl watched him do it in one hand with deep dissatisfaction.

“We did the road part,” she told him.

“I saw.”

He put it exactly where she pointed.

Brenna checked the wheel pins. Mikasa stood with Eren beside the new south posts, her scarf loose in the warm air. He was fastening the strap on her small travel bag. She had already fastened it. He undid it and did it again.

“It was shut,” she said.

“I know.”

She waited.

He finished, and put the bag back into her hand.

“Before dark?”

“That's the walk.”

“If it's clear.”

“If it isn't, we don't make it clear by hurrying.”

He nodded. He knew the route. He knew why she was going. Knowing had not stopped his fingers making the knot twice.

She touched the side of his face, briefly, where the others would not have to pretend they hadn't seen.

“Eat,” she said.

“You too.”

“I packed it.”

Hollis handed Dunmore the lodging paper. He had made the writing larger than usual and gone over the name at the bottom twice.

“He knows the hand,” he said to the mother. “If he looks as if he doesn't, tell him I'm the one who paid for the west wheel last autumn.”

“You said it was booked.”

“It is. He's just a man who likes being reminded that other people owe him. Reminding him works both ways.”

She smiled. Tired, and very small, but a smile.

“Thank you.”

Hollis touched the brim of his hat and stepped back. He was not taking this run. There were still crates in Arrival House that needed counting, and a road agreement he had helped make, and people to hear from after the cart reached its stop.

Jinshi had the departure page with him. He asked the woman whether the bag under the seat was hers, and whether she had everything, and did not ask once more if she was certain.

She looked north, up the street.

The cottage was out of sight from here.

“Yes,” she said.

The bean-girl leaned over the side toward Aira.

“Can I get down at the water?”

“When we say it's all right.”

“There were little fish.”

“We'll look.”

Dunmore clicked to the mule.

The cart moved through the gap between the new posts. There was no finished gate to swing behind it yet. Brenna walked beside the wheel. Mikasa took the outside edge of the road. Aira walked where the girl could reach her shoulder if she leaned down.

After a little while, the child did.



They stopped at the broad, shallow part of the ford a little after midday.

The last rise took longer with the cart than on foot. Dunmore let the mule rest in the shade before the descent, and Brenna checked the crossing from the bank while Mikasa watched the road behind them. The girl had fallen asleep with her head against her mother's ribs. Aira had spent the last half-hour holding the sack away from her dangling foot.

“You can wake her,” said the woman.

“She's sleeping.”

The woman looked at Aira for a moment. Then she put her head back against the crate and closed her own eyes.

Nobody mistook the rest for a holiday. Brenna came back, said the crossing was clear, and kept looking upstream while they ate the bread Momo had wrapped.

The bean-girl woke in time to object to having missed the top of the hill.

“You can see it on the way back,” said Aira.

Then she stopped.

The woman looked down at her bread.

Aira took a breath.

“When there's a way,” she said. “If you come back this road.”

The girl was already looking toward the water. She had heard enough to accept, or enough to leave the rest until later. She took Aira's hand and went to the bank.

There were fish. Very small ones, holding almost still over the stones until a shadow touched them. The girl crouched, careful not to put her knees in, and watched them turn.

“Do they go home?” she asked.

“I don't know,” said Aira.

“Maybe this is home.”

“Maybe.”

The girl put her hand in the water. The fish vanished. She laughed, surprised, then looked up to see whether Aira had seen.

Aira had.



The lodging yard was a short way beyond the crossing, where the known road widened enough to turn a cart.

Mikasa went in with Dunmore first. Brenna waited by the mule. Aira stayed with the woman and child until Mikasa came back to the open door and nodded.

The room was small. A bed with space beside it for the rolled blanket. A shutter that closed. A shelf on which the mother put her wrapped cup before she put down anything else.

No one called it their new home.

It was tonight. Tonight had been arranged. The next place still had to be found, and Brenna and Dunmore knew that, and would stay for the agreed stop rather than deliver them to a road and leave.

The mother counted the coins in her purse onto the bed while Dunmore spoke to the man outside. Her own money, for what came after. Aira looked away so she would not have to count it while being watched.

The child inspected the shutter.

“It sticks.”

“Don't pull the peg out,” said her mother.

“I wasn't.”

She had been. She took her hand off it.

Then she turned to Aira.

“Are you going now?”

Aira knelt. The girl came straight into her arms, without the hesitation the adults were all trying to hide from one another.

“I have to walk back before it gets dark.”

“With Mikasa.”

“Yes.”

“So you aren't alone.”

Aira pressed her face against the child's hair for a second.

“Yes.”

The girl held on until her mother touched her shoulder. Then she let go, and immediately remembered something, and went to the little pouch at her waist to check that the folded door was still there.

Aira stood up.

The mother took her hand.

She did not say *you saved her* again. Aira was grateful for that, and then ashamed of being grateful, and then too tired to separate the two things.

“I heard you,” said the woman. “At my door. Both times.”

Aira nodded.

“If there's a message,” she said, “send it with Hollis's people. Or give it to the man here. Tell him it's for the house.”

“I will.”

“It doesn't have to say you've found somewhere perfect.”

The woman squeezed her hand.

“All right.”

Outside, Mikasa had the bags ready for the walk home. Lighter now. The things that had been carried for someone else had stayed on the other side of the door.

Aira looked back once as they crossed the yard.

The child was at the window, but her mother was beside her, and the shutter was open on an ordinary afternoon, and both of them lifted a hand.



They reached the settlement while there was still colour in the sky.

They had walked steadily. Stopped for water. Waited on the rise while Mikasa checked a movement in the grass that turned out to be a loose branch dragging on another. Neither of them had called it nothing before they knew.

At the south posts, Eren was lifting a bucket out of the way of a barrow.

He put it down when he saw them.

Mikasa went to him. She took the bucket out of the road before he could forget it there.

Aira looked north along the street.

No one was running down it with a bean sack. The cottage lamp would not be lit tonight.

Momo came out of the house before she could decide where to go.

“There's food,” she said.

“I ate.”

“At midday. It isn't midday.”

Aira let herself be taken inside.

At the long table, Fern had the return written down already. *Aira. Mikasa. Back before dark.* Above it, the first line of the day said *Departures: one family. Two people. First stop: the ford.*

She did not draw a line through the cottage on the settlement plan.

Momo put a bowl in front of Aira. Rem moved the bread within reach. Vamola asked whether the fish were still there, and Aira looked up sharply, then realised the girl had told them about the fish before they went.

“They are,” she said.

She picked up the spoon.

Her first mouthful was too hot. She ate it anyway, and Momo, watching from the kitchen door, brought her a second piece of bread.
