# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 26
## Chapter 26 — The Shelf Is Still in the Way

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 26
**Reading order:** 26
**Continuity state:** Day 255 evening | the same east room, shelf in the floor, cars and Ori's stone on the sill | Mau/Frieren choose an ordinary evening together | new grimoire page in the already-owned book, no new loot or power unlock | future time remains wanted and uncertain | Rimuru returns to his established sleeping loft; Ori uses the connecting door | no formal family-day payoff or longevity guarantee

*When: The evening of Day 255. The north walk has been checked after a shower. The south threshold boards have been taken up, inspected, and put back. Nobody needs Mau for either job.*

Frieren had put a marker in her book.

This was suspicious.

Mau stood in the doorway of the east room with two cups and looked at her. She was sitting on the bed in the orange-and-black hoodie, with her bare feet tucked under the blanket and the book shut beside her. The light from the east window had gone blue. Somebody downstairs was trying to persuade somebody else that a second helping did not count as a first helping merely because it was on another plate.

"What?" said Frieren.

"Nothing."

"That's your face for a question."

He brought the cups over. The shelf was still in the way. He went round it on the same side he always used, making the same small turn of his shoulders, and set her cup where she could reach it.

"You stopped reading."

"I can stop reading."

"I know."

"I've done it before."

"I've been here for some of those."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she lifted the edge of the blanket.

He took off his slippers and sat beside her.

There was still work downstairs. There would have been work if he had gone looking for it. He could hear Wakana's careful step on the corridor and the little clink of glass from a tray somebody was carrying to the clinic. For a while, out of habit, he tried to decide whether either sound meant he should get up.

Frieren put her foot against his ankle.

"It's a tray," she said.

"I know."

"They can carry it."

He settled back against the pillow.

The book rested between them, closed. Its cover was worn where her thumb lay while she read. There were small scraps of paper sticking out at different places. She had written on one of them. He could see a line of her very old, very neat script, which he could not read at this angle.

"What's that one?"

She turned the book over before he could lean closer.

"An important page."

"About mimics?"

"They're all important pages."

"Right."

The corner of her mouth moved. He saw it and smiled, and she looked annoyed that he had seen it. She had looked exactly like that on the day he first learned how much pleasure she could take in having an answer ready for him.

He took her hand off the book. Gently. Giving her time to decide whether to leave it there.

She left it.

"What did you do today?" he asked.

"I walked the north watch with Fern. I told Ram it wasn't her day. I took my book back from Gojo."

"Was he reading it?"

"He was holding it while he talked."

"That sounds like reading for him."

"He was holding it upside down."

Mau laughed. She waited until he had finished, then said, with great dignity, "I also found a useful page."

"The important one?"

"They're all—"

"I know."

She sighed and opened the book on their knees.

The page had a diagram, several notes, and a very small picture of something with too many teeth. It was not a new spell she could use to finish the wall or save the roads. It was a careful explanation of a detail somebody had thought worth writing down, a long time ago, and Frieren began describing it with the pleasure of somebody who had been waiting all afternoon for this particular question.

Mau listened.

He did not need the eyes to take it apart. He looked at her finger on the paper, and the turn of her mouth when she disagreed with the note in the margin. At the way a little piece of hair escaped the fall over her shoulder and rested against the hoodie.

She stopped halfway through a sentence.

"Are you listening?"

"Yes."

"Then why are you looking at me?"

"You're explaining it."

"The diagram is there."

"It's not explaining it."

Her ears went pink. Slowly. From the tips inward.

"You do that on purpose now."

"Sometimes."

She pressed her foot more firmly against his ankle and went back to the diagram. He kept holding her hand, which made turning the page difficult until she decided the page did not need turning yet.



There was a knock from the connecting door.

Ori stood in the opening with two folded shirts and a ribbon over her wrist. Her hair was untidy on one side, as if she had leaned against something while she worked and forgotten to straighten up afterward.

"Which pile?"

Mau looked at the shirts.

"Mine?"

"I know they're yours. Where do you want them?"

He pointed to the place beside the closet. Ori went in, went round the shelf with the same turn of her shoulders, put the shirts down, and stood looking at the obstruction.

"You still haven't moved it."

"We're discussing it," said Mau.

"You said that before."

"It's a very complicated shelf," said Frieren.

Ori looked from one of them to the other.

"You're both doing the face."

"What face?" said Mau.

"The one where you're not going to be useful."

She went back to her door. Then she stopped, and held up the ribbon.

"This one is spare. I'm leaving it here. Milim says the other one isn't getting shorter. It is."

Frieren held out her free hand. Ori put the ribbon in it. They both looked at its length with the same solemn attention.

"I'll check it before she sleeps," said Ori.

"Ask her to come down first," said Frieren. "Don't climb after her."

"I know."

She did know. She said it with the ordinary impatience of someone who had done a job many times and still had a mother who wanted the job done safely.

At the threshold she turned back.

"Are you coming down for tea later?"

Mau glanced at Frieren. She glanced at him.

"You can bring yours here," he said. "If you want."

"I was going to ask that."

"Then yes."

Ori smiled and went through. She left the door a hand's width open.



Afterward they sat without speaking for a while.

The room grew darker. Frieren moved the book off their knees before the light was too poor to read, and Mau reached for the lamp. She let him. Nobody needed to call a doctor because he lit an ordinary lamp with an ordinary flame.

The two little cars stood on the sill, the red-and-white one and the red one, with Ori's stone between them. Somebody had washed the cloth underneath when the window was cleaned. The stone rested in its familiar little hollow in the fold.

"I wanted this evening," said Frieren.

He looked at her.

"Before you came upstairs. I kept thinking, I'll finish this page, and he'll bring the cups, and we won't go anywhere."

"We can do that."

"We're doing it."

"I know."

She looked down at their hands.

"I used to miss things while I was waiting for time to pass," she said. "Days. A few years. I didn't think there was anything I had to do with them."

He waited.

"Now I get annoyed when somebody takes an evening."

"Gojo?"

"Anyone."

Her voice made him smile, but he did not turn it into a joke.

"You can tell me you want one," he said.

"I'm telling you."

He moved closer and put his forehead against hers. For a moment there was only the small shared space between their breathing, and her fingers turning inside his until they fitted properly.

"You can have this one," he said.

She stayed there.

She did not ask him to promise every evening that could ever exist. He did not offer them as if he had found a way to keep that promise. He kissed her, slowly, once, and when they drew apart she pulled the blanket over his knees too.

There was a soft knock at the corridor door. Rimuru had finished downstairs. He waited for Mau to answer, came in, and went round the shelf to the closet ladder. They heard the steps, then the loft hatch. He was careful, though he knew they were awake.

"I'm going to sleep," he called down softly.

"Okay," said Mau.

"If somebody asks, I am not in the office."

"We'll say you're asleep," said Frieren.

"That would be great."

There was the sound of a pillow being moved. Then quiet.

At the connecting door, Ori arrived with her cup. She sat on the rug beside the bed, put the cup carefully out of reach of her own feet, and leaned her head against the blanket over Mau's knee.

Frieren opened the book at the marked page.

"You can tell her too," said Mau.

Ori looked up at the diagram. "Is that the teeth one?"

"It's an important page," said Frieren.

Mau put his hand over his mouth.

Frieren looked at him.

"Both of you can leave."

Neither of them moved.
