# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 26
## Chapter 29 — Tomorrow's Work

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S4 v0.1
**Volume:** 26
**Reading order:** 29
**Continuity state:** Day 261 | ordinary library and workshop work continues after the gates become usable | Mau tells Echidna about the earlier arrival-state door; she remembers Nokotan's deer-shaped departure | resemblance is a question, not a demonstrated mechanism or a new route | no return experiment, memory access, divine contact or new power | Ori and the others retain their own plans; tomorrow's tasks remain ordinary

*When: Day 261, afternoon. The repaired south threshold has survived its first rain. Two boards are still waiting to be planed, and a library book is overdue.*

The book under Mau's arm was not his.

Frieren had asked him to return it because she was carrying three others, which seemed reasonable until he realised the three others were going in the opposite direction.

"You could return one and take two," he had said.

She had looked at him with a disappointment too old for the argument.

Now he was in the library with a book he had not read and could not explain.

Echidna held out her hand without looking up.

"How far did she get?"

"There's a piece of string in it."

"There are three pieces of string in it."

He opened the cover. There were.

One marked a drawing of a root, one a page of cramped measurements, and the third appeared to be holding a loose gathering in place. He shut it carefully.

"I'll ask."

"Don't. She'll decide she needs it back."

Echidna put it on the little pile to her left. The pile on her right contained books that needed mending. Mau had put his hand on one before noticing that she had already threaded a needle.

"That one's mine," she said.

He withdrew his hand.

She pushed a strip of clean paper toward him instead.

"Fold those. Exactly in half."

He sat down.

Outside the open window, somebody was trying to explain to Milim that a board could be too long even when she could carry it. It sounded like Wakana. Milim's answer was lost under the scrape of a chair from the next room.

Mau lined up the corners of the paper. Echidna drew the needle through the damaged gathering, pulled the thread tight, and held the book up to see whether it would open properly.

It would not.

She said something short under her breath and began loosening the last stitch.

He decided to look at his paper.

For a while they worked without talking. Through the open doorway, Kita's voice carried faintly from downstairs, repeating a single line without singing it, trying the weight of a word. Somebody answered with three soft notes. Then they did it again.

"That's been going on for twenty minutes," said Echidna.

"Does it bother you?"

"I would like them to get it right."

"You could shut the window."

"Then I wouldn't know."

He smiled and pressed another crease flat with his thumbnail.

The book she was mending had a dark mark near the edge. He watched her move her thumb clear of it before taking the next stitch. The movement was small and careful, and for some reason it brought back a different line. A narrow white edge standing upright where there should only have been water.

He put down the paper.

"There was something I meant to tell you."

Echidna glanced at him. The needle stopped.

"Before all the things that kept happening," he said. "I don't think I ever told you properly. About a door."

She set the needle in its folded scrap of cloth.

He told her what he could remember of the lake. The light drawing itself into a frame. Rimuru on the shore. Ciel finding pieces of things that had not been doors before they were put together. He separated what she had said from what he had actually seen, stopping once to correct himself.

"She thought it was trying to get back to the first version of me she could observe. That isn't the same as knowing where it went."

"No," Echidna said.

"It looked like the clearing. The morning Frieren found me. There were even the flowers."

Her eyes stayed on him.

"And you wanted to go."

"My body did."

He rubbed the crease in the paper he had already folded.

"I did too, for a second. It looked very quiet. Then she called me."

Echidna did not say that quiet things could be dangerous. He was grateful. He had been there.

"You turned away?"

"Yes."

"And no one crossed it."

"No."

She sat back. For a moment she was looking past him, toward the window and the little stretch of sky visible above the work shed.

"Nokotan," she said.

Mau looked up.

"The hole she left. The shape of it. A door that wasn't built into a wall."

He remembered the absurd outline. How quickly it had ceased to be a joke once there was someone missing from the other side of it.

"Do you think it's the same?"

"I think I remember another opening we couldn't explain."

He waited.

"That's all," she said. "I wasn't at your lake. I didn't see your door. Remembering a resemblance doesn't tell me that either of them can take us where we want."

The sound from downstairs stopped. Kita said, "Yes, that," with such relief that both of them looked toward the doorway.

"I don't want to try it," Mau said.

Echidna looked back at him.

"I'm telling you because I kept thinking I should. Not because I want somebody to help me open it."

"Good distinction."

"You look disappointed."

"I can be curious and still hear a sentence."

He laughed a little.

She pulled the book toward her again, but did not pick up the needle.

"Has it happened again?"

"Not like that."

"Tell someone if it does. Before you go looking for the quiet part."

"I know."

He said it without irritation. It was not a new instruction. There were people in this house who had been made to wait until after, and he had promised not to do that to them again.

Echidna tapped the stack of folded paper.

"These aren't all the same size."

He looked at the last three. She was right.

"You gave me different-sized pieces."

"I asked you to fold them, not confess to them."

He pulled the uneven ones out and began again.



Ori found him at the bench outside the work shed.

He was holding the end of a board while Wakana checked a line along it. Milim had gone elsewhere. The board, freed from the possibility of being carried to the wrong side of the yard, was behaving well.

"I'm not coming back with you," Ori told him.

He looked up.

"Okay."

"Umaru and I are doing something."

"Secret?"

"No. You can see it when it's done."

"That sounds secret."

She gave him the look she had been practising on people who asked unhelpful questions. It had become alarmingly good.

"Do you need anything?" he asked.

"The string in your pocket."

He took it out. It was not the string from Frieren's book; he had checked before leaving. Ori wound it round two fingers, leaned down to kiss his hair, and went off toward the fort without explaining herself further.

Wakana waited until she was out of earshot.

"That was your measuring string."

Mau looked at his empty hand.

Wakana produced another from beside the plane.

"I thought it might happen."

They finished the line before supper.



Frieren was at the window when he reached their room. She had one of the three books open on her knee and the silver compass beside it. There was no reason for the compass to be there except that she liked having it within reach.

"Did you give it back?"

"Yes."

"Did she say anything?"

"She asked how far you got."

Frieren looked at the book in her lap, then at the two beside her.

"I'll go tomorrow."

He sat on the edge of the bed and told her about Echidna. The whole conversation this time. Frieren listened without moving the page under her hand.

When he had finished, she asked, "Did it hurt to talk about?"

"A little."

She set the book down.

"Come here."

He moved into the space she made. Her hand settled behind his neck, warm through his hair. Outside, the yard was filling with the small interruptions that meant supper: someone calling for a bowl, someone insisting they had already carried one, a dog barking once and being answered by a person who should have known better.

Mau closed his eyes. For a moment the air against his cheek felt unexpectedly cold.

"What?" Frieren asked.

"The window. I think."

She looked at it, then back at him.

He was not sure. So he told her that too.

They stayed where they were until the next call came from downstairs.
