# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 27
## Chapter 11 — Don't Come In

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1
**Volume:** 27
**Reading order:** 11
**Continuity state:** Mau knocks; Frieren sets a boundary; they speak through their closed bedroom door

*When: Day 262, late evening.*

The corridor was quieter than the room below it had any right to allow.

Mau passed the doors he knew by the things left outside them. A pair of shoes turned away from one another. A cloth hung over a rail to dry. A small scrape in the floor where something heavy had been dragged and nobody had yet decided to sand it out.

The east room had light under its door.

He stood there too long before knocking.

"Frieren?"

Nothing moved for a moment.

Then, "Don't come in."

His hand was already turning toward the latch. He put it flat against his leg instead.

"All right."

He sat with his back to the wall beside the frame. From here he could see the edge of the door to Ori's room. Closed. No light at the bottom. He did not know if she was sleeping, and did not ask Ciel to find out.

"Maki said you'd gone to bed."

"I wanted her to go."

"I thought so."

"Then why did you come?"

He looked down at his hands. He could still see the pale places where he had gripped Frieren's fingers outside.

"Because I'm downstairs trying to do all the things I might not have another chance to do. And mostly I'm thinking about this door."

Inside the room, a floorboard gave under a familiar weight.

He waited.

"Is it the door in the yard," he asked, "or what I said at the table?"

Her answer came from lower down than before.

"Both."

She had sat on the floor on the other side.

He turned a little, bringing his shoulder against the wood.

"I didn't mean to make it worse."

"I know."

He heard her take a breath that did not quite work. He could picture the way she would press her lips together, annoyed with the sound, and wanted to touch her face so badly that his fingers hurt.

He kept them on his knees.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"For what?"

"Asking."

There were several things she might have meant. He let her choose the one she wanted to say.

"For longer. For something I don't know if you can do."

"Frieren."

"I heard you. At the table. You talk as if you can find a way if you try hard enough. As if failing would be a choice."

He looked down the corridor. A lamp had been left low near the stairs, enough to keep people from stumbling. Somebody had done that without being asked.

"I don't know if I can," he said.

"Then why promise it?"

"Because I want it."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the one I have. Not a way to make it happen. A reason I'd look."

On the other side of the door, something brushed the wood. He thought it was her sleeve.

"You shouldn't have to do that because I asked you."

"I don't."

She was silent.

"You don't have to decide that you don't want it anymore," he said. "Just so I won't think I owe it to you."

"I do want it."

The words were so small that he leaned nearer before he could help it.

"That's the part I can't—" She stopped. "I still want you here."

"I want to be here."

"And tomorrow you go."

He had no good answer to make those two things fit.

"Yes," he said.

The corridor stayed quiet. From below came a brief burst of laughter and then somebody shushing it too hard. He did not think either of them recognized the voice.

"I don't know why you chose me," Frieren said.

He turned toward the door.

"I read books. I pick up spells nobody needs. I sleep when I shouldn't. I've already lived so much of it. You should get to—"

"Do you want an answer?"

She stopped.

"Yes," she said, after a while.

He moved until his back was against the door, matching the place where he thought she was sitting.

"All right."

He started with mornings.

Not the pretty ones, with light across her hair and everybody else still asleep. The ones where she refused to open her eyes, put a hand over his mouth when he said breakfast, and somehow acquired three quarters of the blanket while denying that she was awake enough to know where it was.

"Those are difficult mornings," Frieren said through the door.

"For me."

"For both of us."

He smiled. She could not see it; he hoped she heard it.

"I like the face you make when you've found a spell you want to explain. Before you've even checked if I'm listening."

"You should be listening."

"I usually am. Sometimes I'm trying to work out whether you're asking me to fetch something."

"That would be part of listening."

"And the smile when you have a plan. Especially a terrible one."

"My plans are good."

"You tried to justify the shelf by saying I was young enough to learn to walk around it."

For a moment she made no sound. Then he heard the small, offended breath that meant he had quoted her accurately.

"You did learn."

"That's true."

He turned his head against the wood.

"You keep being curious. After everything you've seen. Somebody tells you a spell makes a flower the wrong colour and you're interested. You're not embarrassed that it isn't useful. You want to know how somebody thought of it."

"Flowers are useful."

"I know. You gave me some."

The silence changed.

"You didn't know what to do with me," he said. "Neither did I. You made the flowers anyway."

His hand found a small groove in the door. He followed it down to the place where it vanished into the frame.

"Do you remember the day everybody agreed not to speak to me?"

"You were horrible."

"I was."

"You didn't want to be."

"I was still horrible."

Another quiet breath. He could almost feel her beginning to lean against him on the other side.

"What you told me afterward," he said. "About the part of you that didn't want to go back to living without me."

He let her have time with the memory before he continued.

"I don't know a better way to say it than you did. But it goes both ways. I don't want to build a life and then discover I've left you outside it so you won't think you asked too much."

"I wouldn't ask you to do that."

"I know. So don't ask it of yourself."

She was crying quietly. He could hear the effort to make it quieter still.

"You're my world, Frieren. That's probably a stupid way to say it in a house this full of people. I love them too. But wherever I am, whatever I'm doing, I want there to be a way back to you."

"You don't know there is one tomorrow."

"No."

He would not trade the whole conversation for an easy lie at the end of it.

"But wanting you isn't something you made me do. I'd like a long time to keep proving that. Not one big thing so you believe me forever. Mornings. Your books. You falling asleep halfway through explaining something and being furious when I don't know the ending."

She made a small sound that might have hurt her throat.

"I would finish explaining."

"Eventually."

"Before breakfast."

He laughed softly. She did too, and for a moment the door was only a door he happened to be sitting against.

"That sounds rather like a proposal," she said.

The laugh caught a little in his chest.

"I haven't finished dealing with the last wedding yet."

She knew which one he meant. He was relieved she did not ask him to describe it.

"You would have to ask properly," she said.

"Good. Because the corridor floor is a terrible venue."

"And I would want breakfast."

For a while they sat with that small, impossible ordinary future between them.

Then her hand touched the door. He could hear a ring of wood under her fingertips.

"I believe you."

His eyes closed.

"I still don't know what to do," she said.

"You don't have to do anything right now."

"Don't come in. Please."

He kept his hand away from the latch.

"All right."

"If I see you, I'll try to stop feeling the rest of it. I don't want to do that. I want to know what I mean when I see you tomorrow."

"Then I'll see you tomorrow."

"Before."

"Before."

He waited a little longer, because leaving the door immediately felt like another kind of hurry. When he stood, his knees cracked loudly enough that Frieren told him to be careful.

"Always good advice," he said.

"You never take it."

"I'm leaving the door closed."

She did not answer, but when he put his palm against the wood, something touched it from the other side.
