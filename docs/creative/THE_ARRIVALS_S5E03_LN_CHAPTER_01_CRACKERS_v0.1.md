# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 27
## Chapter 11 — Crackers

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (expanded 2026-10-17 for pacing)
**Volume:** 27
**Reading order:** 11
**Continuity state:** Mai offers company without taking over; Frieren invites the crackers into the east room

*When: Day 262, late evening. The common room has decided, without anybody saying so, that it is not going to bed. Yuta and Rimuru are at the long table with Mau's plate between them. The door in the shed is still open, and the rope Wakana strung in front of it is still the only thing anyone has thought to put there.*

Mau came down the main stair slower than he had gone up it.

He did not mean to. His legs had made the decision somewhere around the turn, where the lamp had been left low, and he let them. The house was loud below him in the particular way of a house trying to be quiet. Cups set down carefully. A chair dragged half a foot and then lifted the rest of the way. Somebody laughing, and then somebody else saying *shh* so hard that it was louder than the laugh.

He knew all the voices. He could have named every one of them with his eyes shut. That was the trouble with tonight. Everything he could name, he kept trying to memorize.

Mai was sitting on the third step from the bottom.

He nearly walked past her. She sat the way she always sat when she did not want to be part of a room but did not want to leave it either: knees up, back against the wall, one hand loose on the step beside her, where her fingers could find the edge of the wood. Most evenings nobody noticed her until she wanted them to. Mau had stopped being surprised by it a long time ago. She was like Anko in that. There when it mattered, and somewhere else the rest of the time, and never once sorry about either.

She moved one foot into his path, and he stopped.

She looked up at him.

"You look awful."

"Thank you."

"I mean it as information." She tilted her head toward the upper floor. "Did she let you in?"

"No."

Mai looked toward the turn in the stairs. He could not tell what she had heard from down here. The walls in this house were clay and straw, and Frieren had once told him he had never in his life talked quietly. Mai did not pretend to know the rest. She did not pretend not to know any of it, either. She just waited, which was a thing she was better at than almost anyone in the house.

"We talked," he said. "She asked for a little more time."

"And you gave it to her."

"I'm standing down here."

"That could mean several things."

He sat down beside her, because his legs had been suggesting it for a while. The step was narrow. Their shoulders were not quite touching. From here he could see a slice of the common room through the banister: the end of the long table, the cold hearth, Rimuru's elbow, the back of Yuta's head. Somebody had put a blanket over the back of Mau's chair at some point in the evening. He did not know who. He found he did not want to know. He wanted it to be the house.

For a minute they listened.

Ryo was objecting to somebody putting away her plate while there was still a crumb on it. Kita was explaining, with tremendous patience, that a crumb was not a meal. Ryo was explaining back that it was a principle. Bocchi was saying nothing at all, which in Bocchi meant she was listening to everyone and would remember all of it. Ryo sounded almost normal until she had to repeat herself, and then her voice did a small thing on the second try that she covered by being even ruder about the crumb.

Mau put his elbows on his knees.

"Everybody's being very normal," he said.

"Yes."

"It's terrible."

"It's how they're saying it." Mai did not look at him. "If they stopped being normal, you'd have to look after them. They know that. So they're being normal at you."

He thought about that for a while.

"That's very kind of them."

"It's very annoying of them," Mai said. "Both things are true."

On the other side of the banister, Rimuru said something about tea, and Yuta said something back about Rimuru not having been asked, and the long table made the sound it made when it was pretending a joke had not been funny.

Mai turned the edge of the step under her fingers.

"Do you want me to ask her?" she said.

He looked at her.

"Company," she clarified. "Not whether she's ready to forgive you for whatever she hasn't told me. I'm not going up there to carry messages. You can do your own talking. You've proved that tonight." She paused. "But she's on her own in that room, and you're down here with half the house, and that's the wrong way round."

"Maki's been up already."

"Maki went up as Maki. That's different." She considered. "Maki went up and asked. If I go up, I'll bring the others. And nobody will ask anything."

He knew who she meant. It was the strangest table in the house, and it had been happening every seventh evening since the winter: crackers, and tea, and Seiko complaining, and Turbo Granny complaining louder, and Maki and Mai and Frieren sitting at the end of the long table saying almost nothing to each other for an hour and getting up afterward looking as though they had been somewhere restful. Mau had never once been invited. He had never asked to be. Some things were better because he was not in them.

"Yes," he said. "If she'd like that."

Mai stood up and brushed off her trousers.

"That sounds like crackers."

"It usually does."

She went to find her sister.

He stayed on the step after she had gone. Through the banister he watched her cross the common room without seeming to hurry. She stopped at the long table. Maki looked up. Something passed between the two of them that did not need words; they had grown up in a house where the words were dangerous, and they had kept the habit of not needing them. Maki put down her cup and stood. Mai went on into the kitchen, toward the shelf where the tin lived, and Mau heard Momo say, "Take the good ones, then," in a voice that had obviously been waiting all evening for something useful to do.

Then Mai came back with the tin under her arm and went to the corner where Seiko sat on her high seat with her fan in her lap.

Seiko looked at the tin. Then at the stair. Then, across the whole room, at Mau.

She did not say anything to him. She got down from her seat, which took a little time, and refused Okarun's arm, which took a little more, and picked up her own cup.

Turbo Granny's head appeared over the edge of Seiko's pocket.

"Where are we going?"

"Upstairs."

"I was comfortable."

"You were complaining."

"I was comfortable complaining."

They went past him on the stair, one at a time. Maki touched his shoulder as she passed, lightly, the way she would have touched a doorframe. Mai did not touch him at all. Seiko stopped on the step above his and looked down at him with her sharp old eyes.

"Eat something," she said. "You'll need it in the morning. Whatever happens."

"I will."

"You won't. But I've said it."

She went on up. He heard her stop at the turn to breathe and pretend she had stopped to look at the lamp.

* * *

Frieren had moved off the floor by the time they knocked.

Not far. She was sitting on the edge of the bed again, holding the cup Maki had left, and the water had gone warm against her hands. She had been holding it for so long that she had stopped noticing she was holding it. The room was the room. The reading chair. The shelf in the middle of the floor. The cars on the windowsill with Ori's grey stone between them. His side of the bed, with his book face down on the pillow, open to the place he had stopped.

She had not touched the book. She had looked at it a great many times.

She had sat on the floor with her back to the door for as long as he had sat on the other side of it, and then for a while after he went downstairs, and then she had got up because the floor was cold and because she had begun to think that if she stayed there any longer she would open the door and run down the stairs after him and say none of the things she needed to understand first.

So she had moved to the bed. Two steps. It had felt like a great distance.

The knock was not his.

She knew his knock. This one was three small taps, evenly spaced, like someone who had been taught to knock in a house where knocking mattered. Then a fourth, impatient, lower down, which could only have been a cat's paw.

"I didn't ask for a meeting," Frieren said through the door.

"Then don't conduct one," said Seiko. "We're bringing food."

Frieren looked at the door for a long moment.

Then she put the cup down and got up and opened it.

Maki was carrying a plate. Mai had the tin. Seiko had brought her own cup, an act of confidence that might have annoyed Frieren on a different evening.

Turbo Granny looked out of Seiko's pocket.

"I was comfortable," she announced, to the room in general.

"You were complaining," said Seiko.

"I was comfortable complaining."

Nobody asked to come in. They simply stood there in the corridor with their crackers and their cups and their cat, looking at her, until Frieren understood that they would stand there for as long as it took and would go away again if she shut the door, and would not mind.

She stepped back.

They waited while she moved the grimoire off the reading chair and put it on the bed. Seiko would have tripped over the shelf if Frieren had not caught her elbow.

"Still there, then," said Seiko.

"It belongs there."

Maki looked at the narrow space beside it and made no comment. Mai did.

"That is the centre of the floor."

"The room has several centres."

"Wonderful," said Mai. "We've found a new branch of geometry."

Frieren sat on the bed. Seiko took the reading chair, because she was the oldest and because she had already decided it was hers. Maki sat on the floor with her back against the bed frame, close enough that Frieren could have touched her hair. Mai took the windowsill, moving the cars along very carefully with one finger to make room, and set the tin on her knees and opened it.

The smell of crackers came into the room. Ordinary, salty, a little burnt at the edges where Seiko insisted they should be.

For a little while nobody asked her anything, which was why she could bear them there.

Seiko inspected a cracker as if it had disappointed her personally. Turbo Granny demanded a piece, received one, and complained about its size. Mai ate hers looking out of the window at the yard, where the shed stood dark against the last of the sky, with the rope in front of it and the wrong morning glowing faintly through the gap. Maki had left enough space between them that Frieren could put her hand down on the blanket without touching anybody.

She found herself moving it closer.

Maki did not look round. But after a moment she leaned back a little, so that her shoulder rested against the edge of the mattress, beside Frieren's knee.

It was a very small thing. Frieren had been alive for more than a thousand years and had learned that the small things were usually the ones that held.

"I know he means it," she said.

Maki did not ask what.

"I know he wants to stay."

Mai put the lid under the tin. "You don't sound very convinced."

"I am convinced. That's the problem."

She looked down at her hands.

"The last time he went somewhere I couldn't follow," she said, "I sat outside a white wall for five hours and forty-one minutes. I counted them. Everybody counted them. And when he came out, he had lived through more than any of us could count." She stopped. "I keep thinking about the wall. That's all. It's very stupid. It's a door in a shed. It isn't the same."

"It isn't stupid," said Seiko. "It's arithmetic. You've done it once. Your hands remember the answer."

Frieren looked at her.

Seiko bit her cracker. "Anyone my age has sat in a corridor and counted something. Tiles. Breaths. Footsteps that weren't the right ones." She shrugged. "Nobody asks you to. Your hands want something to do with the waiting."

Turbo Granny, in her pocket, said nothing at all, which was so unusual that Mai looked round.

Frieren picked up the cup again. The water moved when her fingers tightened.

"He can want something until it hurts him," she said. "That doesn't tell him how to stop."

"He came back downstairs when you asked him to," Maki said.

Frieren looked at her.

"He didn't like it," said Maki. "He did it."

That was an inconveniently small thing to put beside the door in the yard. Frieren could not dismiss it for being small.

Seiko pulled the chair a little nearer the bed. Its legs scraped. The shelf was in the way. She went round it.

"What did he tell you?"

Frieren did not repeat all of it. Some of it belonged to the wood between them. She told them about the mornings, the ones where she would not open her eyes and he said *breakfast* and she put her hand over his mouth. She told them about the shelf, and how he had quoted her own words back to her about learning to walk round it. She told them that he had said he wanted to look for a way, rather than already knowing one.

When she got to the books, she had to stop.

Maki reached up without looking and passed her the cloth from beside the cup.

Mai looked out of the east window for a while.

"That's an awful lot of nonsense to remember," she said at last, "just to make someone feel better."

Frieren laughed once into the cloth, unwillingly.

"They're not nonsense."

"He seems to agree," said Mai.

Outside the window, the last pale strip of sky over the hills had gone, and the only light left in the yard was the morning that did not belong to it, coming through the door in the shed.
