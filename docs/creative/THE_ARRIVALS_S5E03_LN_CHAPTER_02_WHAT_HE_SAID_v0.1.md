# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 27
## Chapter 12 — What He Said

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (expanded 2026-10-17 for pacing)
**Volume:** 27
**Reading order:** 12
**Continuity state:** The night conversation continues; belief and fear coexist; Frieren realizes Mau has never lied to her (the only thing he has kept was Fern's own request, which she guesses concerns Stark); nobody resolves Frieren's feelings for her

*When: Day 262 into the morning of Day 263. The crackers table has never met upstairs before. Nobody mentions it.*

The second plate was mostly broken pieces.

Maki apologized for it when she came back. Momo had said that if they wanted anything less broken, they could begin baking it themselves, preferably without occupying her doorway. Then Momo had put three whole ones on top of the broken ones anyway, without comment, and turned back to the stove before Maki could thank her.

"Fair," Mai said, and took the largest piece, which was one of the whole ones.

Frieren had stopped holding the cup. That seemed to have happened without her agreeing to put it down. It sat on the floor by Maki's knee, with a small skin of dust on the water where a moth had brushed it.

The lamp had burned down a finger's width. Mai got up and turned the wick without being asked and sat back down on the sill. Below the window, the yard was very dark except for the shed, and the rope, and the light in the door that was a different hour from everything around it. From up here it looked almost pretty. Frieren disliked that it looked pretty.

"You don't have to be cheerful when you see him," Seiko said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

Frieren pulled a thread of the blanket straight between her fingers. "I don't want the last thing I give him to be more fear."

The room was silent long enough for her to dislike what she had said.

"It doesn't have to be the last thing," Maki answered.

"You don't know that."

"No. Neither do you."

Seiko reached for another broken cracker. "You can be frightened and still tell him what you came to tell him."

"Which is?"

"I'd hate to invent it for you. You've been very rude about our geometry."

Frieren looked up. Mai's mouth moved at one corner.

It was irritating to be allowed to decide while so many people were waiting for her to decide.

She said that aloud.

"Yes," Maki said.

Nothing after it. No way to improve the feeling. Frieren rested her head against the wall and looked toward the shelf.

There were five flowers in the place they had made for them. She could distinguish the fifth without going over. Keeping them had never been a question. When something was about to be lost, she had always known she wanted it. She had wanted Himmel's smile after it was gone. She had wanted the bronze statues and the little useless spells. Wanting things after they were gone was the only kind of wanting she had been good at for a very long time.

The difficult thing was wanting him while he was still here, and not making a cage out of her hands.

"If he changes his mind," she said. "About longer. If he finds out what it would cost him."

"Then he'll have to tell you," said Mai.

"He might not."

Maki turned her head and looked at her.

"He might," said Maki.

And Frieren stopped.

Not because of the words. Because of the way Maki had said them. Plainly, as if it were a fact about the weather. As if there were nothing to argue.

She sat very still on the edge of the bed and thought about it. Properly, the way she thought about a page she had read too quickly the first time.

He had never lied to her.

She went back through all of it, looking for the place where he had. The old inn, at the very beginning, when he had known almost nothing and had said so. The lake, after they had both been so sure the other did not want them. The chocolate. The festival. The Trial, when he had come out of the white place and sat at the long table a week later and said the number aloud in front of everyone, when he could have carried it alone and nobody would ever have known. The bad season, when he had not been able to say anything at all, and had not pretended.

Tonight, through the door. *I don't know if I can.* He could have said yes. It would have been easier for both of them. He had said he didn't know.

He did not lie to her. He did not even lie to her kindly, which was the kind of lie most people thought did not count.

There was one thing. She found it at the very end of her searching and turned it over, carefully, to be sure.

One evening in the spring, he had gone quiet in the middle of a sentence. Fern had been across the table. Fern had looked at him, very briefly, the way Fern looked at the ledger when a number was wrong. And he had changed the subject, badly, the way he did everything that was not honest, and Frieren had noticed, and had not asked.

She had not needed to ask. She had raised Fern. She knew what Fern's face did when it wanted something kept, and she knew there was exactly one subject in the whole house that made Fern ask anyone for privacy.

Stark.

So that was the one thing. Not a lie. Fern's secret, which Fern had asked him to keep, and which he had kept, and had kept so badly that Frieren had known it was there the whole time. It was not even his.

She almost laughed. She put her hand over her mouth instead.

"What?" said Mai.

"Nothing." Frieren took her hand away. "He's a terrible liar."

"Everyone knows that," said Seiko. "The fort knows that. The fort has a chart."

"No. I mean—" Frieren stopped and tried again. "He doesn't. Lie. To me. He doesn't know how. When he can't tell me something, it shows on his whole face, and he goes red, and he changes the subject so badly that a child would notice." She looked at the door. "So when he says he wants to stay. When he says he wants longer. When he says I didn't make him." Her voice did something. "He isn't saying it to make me feel better. He doesn't have a way of saying things to make me feel better. He only has the true way."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Well," said Mai. "That's inconvenient for you."

"Very," said Frieren.

Maki was looking at her with an expression Frieren could not quite read, and then could.

"You're still afraid," said Maki.

"Yes."

"That's allowed. Believing him doesn't fix that part."

"I know." Frieren looked down at her hands. "I wanted it to."

Downstairs something thumped, then somebody laughed at the attempt to hush it. There were people spending a night awake with him. Yuta. Rimuru. Others coming and going. She could not hear Mau's voice distinctly enough to make out a word. She found herself listening for it anyway, the way she had listened for his breathing in the bad season, counting it.

"I want him to know he can," she said. "Change his mind. Stop. If it ever starts to hurt him. I want him to know he's allowed to tell me."

"That sounds like something to tell him," said Maki.

She nodded.

It did not make her want him for less time. She did not think anything could.

* * *

Later, Seiko told Turbo Granny to stop dropping crumbs into her pocket.

"Your pocket is badly designed."

"It's designed for things that don't eat in it."

"Then carry me somewhere better."

Mai looked at the reading chair. Maki looked at the shelf blocking the easiest route to it. Neither of them volunteered a solution that would survive Frieren hearing it.

Frieren began to laugh, stopped because it hurt, and found Seiko watching her without making a triumph of it.

"He offered to move it once," she said.

"The shelf?" Mai sounded impressed.

"The chair."

"Of course."

"He said I could have a better view of the window."

"And did you?" Maki asked.

Frieren looked toward the glass. She had wanted to read beside him, not improve a view, and had been unable to explain why his helpful proposal annoyed her so much. The memory was ridiculous. It made her miss him while he was still downstairs.

"I told him it belonged here."

"Efficient," said Mai.

Maki picked up a broken piece from the plate and turned it before eating. Frieren watched her for a moment.

"Do you always know what to say to Yuta?"

Maki stared at her. Mai laughed before her sister could answer.

"No," Maki said. "I sometimes know what I should have said afterward."

The plainness of it helped more than Frieren wanted to admit.

Seiko asked whether that could be written on the wall for general use. Turbo Granny said most of the people downstairs would need a larger wall. They argued about which wall, and whether the fort would charge a fee to display it, and whether Umaru would accept crackers as payment, and Frieren listened until she could join in without using the argument to explain anything about herself.

They talked about other things for a while. Seiko complained about the chair. Mai said it had been expensive enough in arguments to be comfortable. Frieren said she would tell Mau they had insulted it, and Maki asked which of the two of them would take that worse.

They laughed. Frieren laughed with them, then cried again without warning and was furious with her face.

Nobody told her that meant they had made no progress.

Maki moved the plate out of the way so that she would not knock it over, and went on eating.

At some point Fern knocked softly with fresh water. She set the jug inside the door and looked at Frieren rather than counting the empty cups. Frieren looked back at her, at the small serious face she had watched grow up, and thought about the one thing Mau had not told her, and did not say anything about it. It was Fern's. It would stay Fern's.

"I will come for you before he goes," said Fern.

"I'll come down."

Fern did not argue about which of them had said it first.

"Then I will tell you when he's waiting outside."

After she left, Frieren lay back against her own pillow and put an arm over her eyes. They stayed. She heard Maki and Mai speak quietly about the plate, Seiko move a foot away from the shelf, the tin being closed and then opened again because Turbo Granny had not finished.

She slept in small pieces without meaning to. Waking was always the same: the weight of tomorrow arriving before she remembered whether tomorrow had arrived yet.

Once she heard the connecting door move. Ori looked in, saw the company and the open tin, and came to the bed without asking why everybody was still awake.

Frieren lifted her arm. Ori lay against her for a while, careful of the others' legs. She had a piece of red ribbon wound round her finger.

"Milim's asleep," she said.

"Good."

"She wanted to stay awake. She said she'd wake up instantly if I told her to."

"Will she?"

"Not if I ask nicely."

Frieren touched the hair at Ori's temple. Ori closed her eyes, then opened them again as if she had done something wrong.

"You can sleep," Frieren said.

"You too."

"He told you first," said Frieren, after a while. Very quietly, into Ori's hair. "Today. At the table. Before anything."

"I know." Ori turned the ribbon round her finger. "He promised. At the tidepools. And then he broke it. And then he promised again." She was quiet. "He kept it this time."

"Yes."

"I still don't like it."

"No," said Frieren. "Neither do I."

They stayed like that until Ori grew heavy, and Mai got down off the windowsill and offered to help her back through her own door. Ori went without arguing, which meant she was more tired than she would admit. At the door she stopped and looked back at Frieren on the bed.

"You'll come down," she said. It was not a question.

"I'll come down."

The window was no longer black when Frieren looked at it next.

She sat up too quickly. Maki put a hand out, then let her find her own balance.

"How long?"

"Less than an hour," said Maki. "Fern will tell us."

Frieren looked toward the closed door. He had spoken to her through it for all that time, and had left it closed because she asked. He had never lied to her. Not once. Not even kindly.

She put her feet on the floor.

"I know what I want to tell him," she said.
