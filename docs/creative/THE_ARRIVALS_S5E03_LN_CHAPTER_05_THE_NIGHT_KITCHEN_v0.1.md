# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 27
## Chapter 14 — The Night Kitchen

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (breathing chapter, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 27
**Reading order:** 14
**Continuity state:** Breathing chapter. The small hours of the twelve-hour night from the kitchen: Momo bakes because she cannot do anything else; Okarun keeps her company; Kaneki comes in from the night shift; the pear bread Mau eats at dawn is made here; no new decision

*When: The small hours of Day 263, between three and four. Upstairs, the crackers table is still awake in the east room. In the common room, Mau is asleep on the bench under a blanket that is not his.*

Momo could not sleep, so she made bread.

That was the whole of the reasoning. She had lain in bed for two hours with her eyes open, listening to the house not sleeping around her, and at some point she had understood that if she lay there one more minute she would go downstairs and stand in the yard and stare at the shed, and that would help nobody. So she had got up and put her apron on over her nightdress and gone down to the kitchen instead, where there were things to do with her hands.

The stove was banked low. She opened it and fed it. The kitchen was dark except for the stove's mouth and one lamp she lit and turned down, and very quiet, and it smelled the way it always smelled at this hour, of yesterday's bread and the cold stone of the floor and the herbs Maomao hung from the beam above the long table.

She got out the big bowl.

She did not decide what to make. Her hands decided. Flour. Salt. The starter from the crock on the windowsill. Water from the jug. And then, because her hands had already gone to the basket in the cold cupboard before she could stop them, pears.

Pear bread. Mau's favourite. He had never said so. He did not need to. He always took the second slice when he thought nobody was counting, and Momo was always counting.

She stood with a pear in each hand and looked at them for a while.

Then she started cutting.

* * *

Okarun found her at half past three.

He came down the back stair in his socks, because the front stair creaked on the fourth step and he did not want to wake Mau on the bench, and stopped in the kitchen doorway, and looked at her.

"You're baking," he said.

"Yes."

"It's three in the morning."

"Half past."

"Momo."

She did not look up. She was kneading. She kneaded the way she did everything that mattered, with her whole body, leaning into the dough as if it had said something rude to her.

"You can go back to bed," she said.

"I wasn't in bed."

She looked up then. He was in yesterday's clothes. There was a crease down one side of his face from lying on the floor of his room with his head on his arm.

"Turbo Granny wouldn't stop talking," he said. "So I went and sat on the stairs. I've been on the stairs."

"For how long?"

"A while." He came in and sat down on the bench at the long table, across from her, the way he sat when she was cooking and he was not allowed to help. "She stopped talking about an hour ago. She's asleep in my pocket. That's how I knew it was bad. She never sleeps when something's happening."

Momo went back to the dough.

For a while neither of them said anything. The stove ticked. The dough made the soft sound dough makes when it is nearly right.

"It's pear," said Okarun.

"Yes."

"He likes the pear one."

"I know he does."

Okarun was quiet.

"He's asleep on the bench," he said. "Somebody put a blanket on him. Kaneki's coat's on top of that. I don't know when Kaneki came in." He paused. "I didn't wake him up."

"Good."

"He looks really young when he's asleep."

Momo's hands stopped.

She stood very still over the bowl with her floury hands in the dough. Then she started again, harder.

"Don't," she said.

"Sorry."

"It's fine. Just don't."

Okarun did not say anything else for a while. Then he got up, without asking, and went to the cupboard and got down the loaf tins and started greasing them, the way she had shown him once, with a scrap of butter on a cloth. He was bad at it. He used too much butter. She did not tell him so.

* * *

Kaneki came in from the night shift at ten to four.

They heard the outer door, very quietly, and his boots on the stones of the yard, and then nothing for a long time. Momo knew where he had stopped without having to look. He had stopped at the rope. He was standing in front of the shed in the dark, looking at the wrong morning through the back wall, the way everybody who went past it did.

When he came into the kitchen, there was sawdust on his sleeves and charcoal under his nails and the smell of the kilns on him. He looked at the two of them, and the dough, and the greased tins, and Okarun's floury face.

"I heard," he said. "At the timber yard. Anko came down." He did not say what Anko had told him. He did not need to. "I thought about coming back. And then I thought there'd be nothing for me to do here. So I finished the shift."

"There's nothing for anyone to do here," said Momo.

"You're doing something."

She looked at the dough.

"It's bread," she said. "It doesn't count."

Kaneki looked at her for a moment. He reached to take off his coat, found only shirtsleeves, and seemed to remember where his coat was. Something passed across his face that might have been embarrassment.

"I put it on him," he said. "On the bench. On my way in. He was cold."

"We saw," said Okarun.

Kaneki sat down at the long table beside Okarun. He did not ask for anything. Momo put a cup of tea in front of him anyway, from the pot that had been keeping warm on the back of the stove since midnight and had stewed. He drank it without a word, which meant he was more tired than he would ever say.

"Rem and I walked for three days," he said, after a while. To the cup. "Looking for him. In the bad season. Before any of this." He turned the cup. "Neither of us called it trust. She told him that, I think. In her room. In the rain." He paused. "We didn't know what we were walking toward. We didn't know if he'd be alive at the end of it. We just walked."

Momo kept kneading.

"That was worse," said Kaneki. "Not knowing where he was." He looked at the window, at the dark yard, at the faint wrong glow from the shed. "Tonight we know where he is. He's on the bench, with my coat on." He drank the last of the tea. "That's something."

Okarun looked at him.

"Is it?" he said.

"It's what I've got," said Kaneki.

* * *

The bread went into the oven at a quarter past four.

Four loaves. Momo had made enough for the whole long table, without meaning to, because her hands had kept going. She closed the oven door and stood back from it and wiped her hands on her apron, and then she did not know what to do with her hands, and she stood in the middle of the kitchen not knowing.

Okarun got up.

He did not say anything. He came round the long table and stood in front of her. And then, very awkwardly, because he was Okarun and he was always awkward about this, he put his arms round her.

Momo stood stiffly for a moment, with her floury hands held out to the sides so she would not get flour on him.

Then she put them on his back anyway, flour and all, and held on.

She did not cry. She had decided at two in the morning, lying in bed, that she would not cry until it was over, one way or the other, and she was a woman who kept what she decided. But she held on to Okarun in the middle of the kitchen for a long time, with her face against his shoulder, while the bread began to smell like bread and Kaneki looked very hard at the bottom of his empty cup.

"He'll eat it," said Okarun into her hair. "In the morning. He'll take the second slice when he thinks nobody's counting."

"I'll be counting," said Momo.

"I know you will."

In his pocket, Turbo Granny woke up, sniffed, and said, very quietly, for once in her life without shouting:

"Pear."

Then she went back to sleep.

At ten to five, Momo took the loaves out and put one on a plate on the long table where the common room could see it, and covered it with a cloth, and went to stand at the kitchen door with flour on her hands.

She did not go any further than that. She stood there, and waited, the way everyone was waiting, for the morning to come and the man on the bench to wake up and take the second slice.
