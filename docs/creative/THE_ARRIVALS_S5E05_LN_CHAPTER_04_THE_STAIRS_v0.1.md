# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 27
## Chapter 28 — The Stairs

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (breathing chapter, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 27
**Reading order:** 28
**Continuity state:** Breathing chapter. The evening of Day 264: Frieren keeps climbing the stairs for every small thing, as Mau once walked to fill the jug for her (S3 V14, "Two Hands Full"); the roles are reversed with comedy; no cure, no power change

*When: Day 264, evening. The fever is lower but has not gone. Shoko has written instructions on a slate by the door. Frieren has read them several times and disagreed with two.*

He heard her on the stairs for the sixth time just after the lamp was lit.

He knew it was her. Everybody in the house had a sound on those stairs. Rimuru took them two at a time and skipped the creaking fourth step. Fern took every one, evenly, as if counting them. Milim did not take them at all. Frieren took them slowly, with a small pause at the turn, where the lamp hung, every single time, as if the turn needed to be considered before she was willing to go round it.

Pause. The turn. The corridor. Their door.

She came in with a cup.

One cup. Of water. She set it down on the side table beside the other three cups, in the place where it would make a fourth, and stood back and looked at the arrangement with the serious face she used for grimoires.

Mau looked at the four cups.

"Frieren."

"Yes."

"There were already three cups."

"Yes. That one is for after."

"After what?"

"After you drink the others." She pointed. "Left is water. Middle is the one Maomao made, which tastes like a boot. Right is the one Shoko made, which also tastes like a boot but a different boot. And that one is water for after you drink the boots."

Mau looked at her for a long time.

"You went downstairs for a cup of water," he said. "To go with the water."

"It's a different water," said Frieren. "It has a different job."

He put his face in the pillow so she would not see it.

* * *

She had been going up and down the stairs all day.

He had lost count somewhere around noon. A cloth. A different cloth, because the first one was too warm. The book he had asked for. The other book, because she had decided he had asked for the wrong one. A blanket. The same blanket again, after she had taken it down to air because Maomao had said something about fresh air that Frieren had interpreted as applying to blankets. A bowl of soup. The bowl's spoon, which she had forgotten. Salt, for the soup, which he had not asked for and she had decided he would want.

Every time, he heard her on the stairs. Pause at the turn. Corridor. Door.

She could have stayed. Fern had offered to fetch things. Rimuru had offered, from the loft, and then actually come down twice and done it. Shoko had told her in so many words that the patient did not need to be checked on every quarter of an hour and that she was allowed to sit down.

Frieren had nodded, and sat down, and stayed sitting for perhaps four minutes, and then got up and gone downstairs for something.

He had not understood it at first. Then, at about the sixth trip, he had.

* * *

"You must have a spell for this," he said, when she had sat down on the edge of the bed with her back against the wall and her knees up. "For fetching things. You have a spell for everything."

"I have one."

"Then why are you walking?"

Frieren did not answer at once.

She looked at the four cups on the side table. Then at the door. Then at him, lying on his back with the cloth on his forehead and the fever still sitting under his skin like a coal that would not go out.

"You walked," she said.

He did not say anything.

"When I had the cold," said Frieren. "In the Muslin. You kept doing the thing. The leak. Where you'd be at the well and then you'd be in the doorway with the jug, and you didn't know you'd done it." She was not looking at him now. She was looking at her knees. "And then you stopped. On purpose. You started walking. Every time. You'd go all the way down and all the way back up. I could hear you on the stairs."

"I remember."

"You said you wanted to know when you were doing it. If you stopped noticing the small ones, you wouldn't notice the big ones."

"I remember," said Mau again. More quietly.

Frieren turned her head and looked at him.

"I don't have a leak," she said. "I could use the spell and you'd never know. It would be faster. It would be more sensible. I could sit here the whole time." She paused. "But then you wouldn't hear me coming."

Mau did not say anything for a long moment.

"And you'd know I hadn't gone anywhere," said Frieren. "Every time. Because you'd hear me go, and you'd hear me come back. Down, and up. The turn. The door." She looked away again. "I didn't get to hear you come back. Yesterday. You came back out of the sky. I want to hear you hear me come back. As many times as it takes."

The lamp ticked.

Mau took the cloth off his forehead. It was warm. He held it in his hand and looked at the ceiling, at the crack in the corner that Rimuru said was decorative, and felt something in his chest that had nothing to do with the fever.

"Then walk," he said.

Frieren looked at him.

"I like hearing you on the stairs," said Mau.

She looked at him for a long time. Her ears had gone faintly pink at the tips.

"That's my line," she said.

"I know. I'm borrowing it."

"You have to give it back."

"When I'm well."

"When you're well," Frieren agreed. She took the cloth out of his hand, and got up, and went to the basin and wet it again, and wrung it out, and came back and put it on his forehead, cool. "Then I'll take it back, and you'll have to walk again, and I'll be the one listening."

"That seems fair."

"It is fair. I've thought about it."

* * *

Later, she read to him.

She sat in the reading chair, which meant going round the shelf, which meant he heard her mutter something about the shelf under her breath, which was the most ordinary sound he had heard in two days and made him want to laugh. She opened the book to the place he had lost and then had to go back three pages to explain why the place mattered.

"Hold it lower," he said.

She looked up.

"The book," said Mau. "You're holding it too high. I can't see the pictures."

"There aren't any pictures."

"I can't see the diagrams."

"There aren't any diagrams either. It's a book about the history of a water-purification spell."

"I can't see your face, then. Hold it lower."

Frieren looked at him over the top of the book for a long moment.

Then she lowered it. Exactly two finger-widths.

"Better?"

"No. You're standing in the wrong place."

"I'm sitting."

"You're sitting in the wrong place."

Frieren stared at him. He watched her remember. The Muslin. Her in the bed, coughing. Him in the chair, holding the book, being told over and over that he was holding it wrong, standing in the wrong place, letting the light fall badly on the page.

Her mouth moved at one corner.

"You're enjoying this," she said.

"I'm ill," said Mau. "It doesn't count."

"It counts."

"You said it didn't count. When you were ill from concern. Two days ago. With the shelf."

Frieren opened her mouth. Then closed it.

She got up out of the chair, which meant going round the shelf again, and came and sat on the edge of the bed beside him, with the book on her knees, where he could see her face and the page and the lamp all at once.

"There," she said. "The right place."

"The right place," Mau agreed.

She read. He listened. Somewhere around the third explanation of why the water-purification spell had been considered controversial in its own century, his eyes closed.

"You're not listening," said Frieren.

"I'm listening. Very hard. With my eyes shut."

She did not stop reading. She lowered her voice, and kept going, and turned the pages very quietly so the paper would not crackle. When she was sure he was asleep, she put the book down on the side table beside the four cups, and lay down beside him on top of the blanket, and put her hand flat on his chest to feel it go up and down.

Then, very quietly, so as not to wake him, she got up, and went downstairs, and came back up again. Pause at the turn. The corridor. The door.

For no reason. With nothing in her hands.

Just so that if he was dreaming, he would hear her coming.
