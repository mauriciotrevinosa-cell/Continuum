# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 27
## Chapter 55 — The Candle Book

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (breathing chapter, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 27
**Reading order:** 55
**Continuity state:** Breathing chapter. Day 271: Frieren reads the first of the three new grimoires (keeping a candle lit in a draught) in the Witch Hat room; the Witch Hat girls and Qifrey compare its drawn diagram with glyph work without merging the two magic systems; Coco's mother watches; no new technique is learned or unlocked

*When: Day 271, afternoon. Mau is resting by Shoko's order and has been told that reading counts as rest only if someone else does the reading. The three books are in the record, "for study and access", in Fern's hand.*

Frieren had meant to read the candle book alone.

That had been the plan. A quiet afternoon in the reading chair with the first of the three books open on her knee, and Mau asleep on the bed, and nobody needing anything. She had read the first page four times that morning and had begun to understand what the author meant by *the edge of the flame is a conversation*. She wanted to keep understanding it without anyone watching her do it.

Then Coco had knocked on the east-room door and asked, very politely, whether it was true that one of the books had a drawing of a candle with the wind barred off it.

And Frieren, who had raised a child, had heard what was underneath the politeness.

So now she was in the Witch Hat room.

* * *

The room was crowded in the way it always was when all of them were in it. The beds round the walls. Agott's alcove with its curtain half drawn. The table under the window where Coco drew. The shelf at the end of the row with the small glass bottle that had been full of ink since the tree began to give. Tetia on her bed hugging her knees. Richeh awake, which was rare, sitting very straight against the wall with her eyes on the book. Qifrey by the door, the way he always stood by doors.

And Coco's mother on the end of Coco's bed, with her sewing in her lap, not sewing.

Frieren sat at the table under the window with the book open in front of her. Coco sat across from her. Agott had come out of her alcove and was standing behind Coco's chair with her arms folded, pretending she had only come out to see whether anyone was going to make a mess.

"There," said Frieren. She turned the book round so they could see.

The drawing was in the margin of the third page. Small. Careful. A candle, with a flame. And round the flame, on one side, a series of short curved lines, like the lines a child draws to show wind. And across those lines, three straight strokes. Barring them.

Coco leaned so close her nose nearly touched the page.

"Oh," she said.

Agott leaned too, despite herself.

"That's not a glyph," said Agott. "It's a drawing of what the spell does. It's a picture. For the reader."

"Yes," said Frieren.

"Glyphs aren't pictures," said Agott. "Glyphs are the spell. You draw the glyph and the glyph does the thing. You don't draw a picture of a candle and expect it to stay lit."

"No," said Frieren. "This isn't a glyph. In my world, nobody draws spells. You hold them. Here." She touched her own temple. "The drawing is so you can see the shape of what you're trying to hold. The words around it tell you how." She ran her finger along the lines of script beside the drawing. "The spell is the words and the holding. Not the picture."

Coco was still staring at the drawing.

"But the shape's right," she said slowly. "Look. The wind comes from this side. And the bars are here. Not all the way round. Only on the side the wind comes from." She looked up. "If you barred it all the way round, the flame wouldn't get any air. It'd go out anyway. So whoever drew this knew that. They only barred one side."

Frieren looked at her.

It had taken Frieren four readings that morning to understand that the spell was one-sided. That it did not wall the flame off. That it turned the draught aside, only where the draught came from, and let the rest of the air in. The author had spent two pages explaining it.

Coco had seen it in the drawing in about ten seconds.

"Yes," said Frieren. "That's exactly right."

Coco went pink.

Behind her, Agott said, "Hm," in the voice she used when she was impressed and would rather die than admit it.

* * *

Frieren read the spell aloud.

Not to cast it. She was very careful to say that first, and Qifrey, by the door, nodded once. She read it the way you read a recipe to someone who wants to know what is in a dish, slowly, explaining as she went. *The edge of the flame is a conversation.* The flame wants air. The draught wants to take the flame with it. The spell does not stop the draught. It talks to it. It says, very politely, *go round*.

"Go round," said Tetia, from her bed. She had stopped hugging her knees. "That's a nice spell."

"It's a very small spell," said Frieren.

"Small spells are nice," said Tetia. "Big spells are scary."

Nobody argued with that.

Richeh, against the wall, said, very quietly, "Can it be drawn?"

The room went still.

Frieren looked at her. Richeh did not say much. When she did, it was usually because she had been thinking about something for a very long time.

"In ink," said Richeh. "As a glyph. Not this spell. A spell that does what this one does. Turns a draught aside. On one side only." She looked at the drawing. "Our glyphs can wall things. We know how to wall things. We've never made one that only walls one side and talks to the rest."

Qifrey had come away from the door.

He stood behind Frieren's chair and looked down at the drawing for a long time. Frieren could feel him thinking, the way you can feel heat off a stove.

"I don't know," said Qifrey at last. "It's a different kind of magic. Hers comes from inside. Ours comes from the ink." He paused. "But the shape is a shape. And Coco is right. Whoever drew this understood the shape." He looked at Richeh. "It might be worth trying. On paper. With very little ink. Very carefully."

"Not today," said Frieren.

Everyone looked at her.

"It isn't mine to say," she said. "It's yours. But I'd like to understand this one first, my way, before anybody tries to turn it into another way." She closed her hand gently over the margin. "I've only had it two days. The person who wrote it spent much longer than that."

Qifrey looked at her for a moment. Then he nodded.

"Not today," he agreed.

Richeh did not argue. But she took a scrap of paper from her lap and wrote something on it, very small, and folded it, and put it in her pocket. And Frieren, who had carried a great many folded scraps of paper with things on them she was not ready to do yet, did not ask what it said.

* * *

Coco's mother had not said anything the whole time.

She sat on the end of Coco's bed with her sewing in her lap and watched. Not the book. Her daughter. Coco leaning over the drawing with her nose nearly touching it. Coco explaining the one-sided bars. Coco's inky fingers, hovering above the page, not quite touching it, the way you hold your hand over something too precious to smudge.

When Frieren closed the book, Coco's mother spoke.

"In our village," she said, "there was a woman who could keep a lamp lit in any wind. Everybody said it was a trick. She had a way of holding her hand." She cupped her own hand, slightly, on one side, as if round a flame. "Only on one side. Never all the way round."

Coco turned round on her chair and stared at her mother.

"You never told me that."

"You never asked about lamps," said her mother. "You were always asking about witches." She looked at the book in Frieren's hands. "I thought it was just a trick. A way of holding your hand." She paused. "Maybe it was. Maybe everything is, if you know the shape."

Frieren looked at her for a long moment.

Then she opened the book again, to the drawing, and turned it round so it faced Coco's mother instead.

"Show me," she said. "How she held her hand."

Coco's mother hesitated. She looked at her daughter. Coco nodded, very fast.

So Coco's mother put down her sewing, and got up off the bed, which still took her a moment, and came to the table under the window. And lifted one hand. And cupped it, slightly, on one side only, round an imaginary flame, the way a woman in a village by a stream had done a long time ago in another world.

Frieren watched her hand.

Agott watched her hand. Tetia came off her bed to watch. Richeh leaned forward against the wall. Qifrey stood very still. And Coco sat with her inky hands in her lap and looked at her mother's hand cupped round nothing, and her face did something soft and astonished, the face of somebody learning that the person they love most had a whole life of small knowings they had never thought to ask about.

"Like that," said Coco's mother. "I think. It was a long time ago."

"It was years ago," said Coco. "For you it was a moment."

Her mother smiled at her.

"Then I remember it very well," she said.

Frieren wrote something in the margin of her own notebook, very small, beside her copy of the drawing. *One side. The hand, cupped. A village woman's trick.* And under it, smaller:

*Ask Coco's mother more about lamps.*

When she went back to the east room an hour later, Mau was awake.

"How was it?" he said.

Frieren put the candle book down on the shelf beside the five flowers. She thought about it.

"I went to teach," she said. "And I learned two things. One from a child and one from her mother." She sat down on the edge of the bed. "It was a very good afternoon."

"Better than reading alone?"

Frieren considered this honestly.

"Don't tell anyone," she said.
