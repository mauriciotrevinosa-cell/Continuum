# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 27
## Chapter 57 — The Bench

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (breathing chapter, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 27
**Reading order:** 57
**Continuity state:** Breathing chapter. Day 273, the weekly gate change: the ground under the west support has dried, and Wakana fastens the seat of the inside-path bench from the family day (Day 267); Ori updates her map; Mau is not allowed to help and mostly does not

*When: Day 273, morning. The gate changed at dawn; Anko has already pinned the new week's report to the wall. It has not rained for six days.*

Ori found out about the bench from Marin, who found out from Wakana, who had not meant to tell anyone.

"He went out at first light," said Marin, at breakfast, buttering bread with great concentration. "With his tools. And a plank. He said he was checking the ground under the west support." She put the knife down. "He's been checking the ground under the west support every morning for six days."

Ori stopped eating.

Across the table, Mau stopped eating too.

"The bench," said Ori.

"I didn't say that," said Marin.

"You said west support."

"I said west support," Marin agreed. "I didn't say which west support. There are a lot of supports in the world. Some of them face west." She picked up her bread. "I'm just a woman eating breakfast."

Ori looked at Mau.

Mau looked at Frieren, who was reading, and did not look up, and said, "Go," without turning a page.

* * *

The bench was on the inside path, past the place where the work stopped and started with the weather, at the first turn. The place where Ori had drawn a bench on her map before they reached it, because she remembered one being there. And where there had only been two supports and a plank leaning against them.

Twice. They had met it twice.

On Ori's map, which lived on the inside of her connecting door, there were two small angry faces beside it.

Now there was Wakana.

He was kneeling in front of the west support with his sleeves rolled up and a mallet in one hand, and the plank was not leaning against anything any more. It was lying across both supports, level, with a line of new pegs along one edge where he had fastened it. He was tapping the last peg in. Very carefully. Listening to the sound it made, the way he listened to everything he built.

He heard them coming and looked up and went slightly red.

"It isn't finished," he said.

"It looks finished," said Ori.

"The seat isn't sanded." He put the mallet down. "And the east end sits a hair's breadth higher than the west end. I can feel it. I need to take a shaving off the east support before anybody sits on it." He looked at the bench with an expression of deep, private disappointment. "I was going to have it done before anyone saw."

"I'm not anyone," said Ori. "I'm the person with the map."

Wakana considered this.

"That's fair," he said.

Mau had stopped a few paces back. He had his hands in his pockets. Ori could see him looking at the east end of the seat, and at the mallet on the grass, and at the plane in Wakana's tool roll. She could see him beginning to think about helping.

She turned round.

"No jobs," she said.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're looking at the plane."

"I'm allowed to look at a plane."

"Not like that."

Wakana looked from one of them to the other, and something at the corner of his mouth moved.

"He can hold the end," he said. "If you like. While I take the shaving. It's only holding."

Ori looked at Wakana. Then at Mau, who had gone very still, the way he went when somebody offered him something he wanted and he was trying not to want it in front of them.

She thought about it seriously. It was her map. It was her rule.

"Only holding," she said at last. "And I'm watching."

* * *

So Mau held the east end of the bench while Wakana planed the support underneath it.

It took a long time. Wakana did not hurry anything. He took a shaving off, so thin that the light came through it, and set the plank back down, and put his hand flat on the seat, and closed his eyes. Then he took it off again and took another shaving, thinner. And set it down. And put his hand on it. And closed his eyes.

Ori sat on the grass and watched.

She had her map. Not the real one; that stayed on the door. A copy, on the back of a used tally, that she had been carrying in her pocket since the family day, in case. She had the pencil behind her ear, the way Senku kept his.

"Why do you close your eyes?" she said, on the fourth shaving.

Wakana did not open them.

"Because my eyes lie," he said. "My hands don't. I can see it's level. I can't feel that it's level yet. So I keep going until my hands agree with my eyes." He took his hand off the seat. "Most things are like that. You think something's finished because it looks finished. And then somebody sits down on it."

Mau, holding the east end, did not say anything. But Ori saw his face.

"Marin says you've been checking the ground for six days," she said.

Wakana went red again.

"The ground was wet," he said. "Under the west support. If I'd fastened it while the ground was wet, the support would have sunk when it dried. And then it would have been crooked. And somebody would have sat on it, and it would have tipped, and they'd have thought it was a bad bench." He put the plane down. "I didn't want it to be a bad bench. It's had a difficult week. It deserves to be a good one."

Ori stared at him.

"You saw the map," she said.

"I saw the map." Wakana looked at the ground. "The faces. On the door. When I came up to fix the catch on your door." He paused. "It looked very angry. The second face especially."

"It was angrier the second time," said Ori.

"I could tell," said Wakana. "That's why I wanted to get it right."

* * *

The fifth shaving was the last one.

Wakana set the plank down, and put his hand flat on the seat, and closed his eyes, and kept them closed for a long time. Then he opened them. And he did not say anything. He just took his hand away, and looked at Mau, and nodded.

Mau let go of the east end.

The bench sat on its two supports, level, in the morning sun, with a little curl of pale wood shavings on the grass underneath it.

"It's done," said Wakana. Quietly. As if he were telling the bench.

Ori got up off the grass.

She went to the bench. She stood in front of it. She put her hand on the seat, flat, the way Wakana had, and closed her eyes.

It was warm from the sun. It was smooth. It did not move.

She opened her eyes and sat down on it.

It held.

Mau sat down on one side of her. Wakana, after a moment, as if he were not sure he was allowed, sat down on the other. The three of them sat on the bench on the inside path, at the first turn, in the sun, and did not say anything.

Then Ori took the pencil from behind her ear, and took the copy of the map out of her pocket, and found the bench on it, with its two angry faces.

She did not cross them out. She drew a third face beside them.

It was smiling.

"There," she said.

Wakana leaned over to look. He looked for a long time.

"Can I have that?" he said. "The copy. When you're done with it."

Ori looked at him in surprise.

"Why?"

Wakana looked at the map. Then at the bench under them. Then at his own hands, which had pegged and planed and checked the ground under the west support for six mornings in a row so that it would be a good bench.

"Because nobody's ever drawn a face for something I made," he said. "Not one that smiled."

Ori looked at him for a long moment.

Then she folded the copy carefully along its crease, and held it out.

Wakana took it. He put it in the breast pocket of his shirt and buttoned the pocket.

That evening, on the inside of Ori's connecting door, the real map got a third face too. Mau saw her draw it. He did not say anything. He went downstairs and found Wakana in the workshop and stood in the doorway until Wakana looked up.

"Thank you," said Mau. "For waiting until the ground was dry."

Wakana looked at him. Then went back to the joint he was cutting, and did not say anything for a while.

"Somebody has to," he said at last. "Most people don't."
