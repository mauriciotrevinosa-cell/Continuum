# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 28
## Chapter 21 — Yuka's Watch

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (breathing chapter, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 28
**Reading order:** 21
**Continuity state:** Breathing chapter. Red Gate night four: Yuka and Mau share a watch after the pond; she thanks him for the strike without making it heroic; they talk about her grandparents' "plans" and about missing home in small ways; the points stay unspent

*When: Red Gate night four (Day 280 outside). The pond is behind them. Mau's knuckles are bruised. The window with five unspent points is closed.*

Yuka had the first watch with Mau, because Frieren had looked at the two of them after supper and said, "You two," in a voice that did not invite discussion.

So they sat at the mouth of the shelter with the fire between them and the sleeping rolls behind, and watched the dark.

For a while, neither of them said anything.

Yuka had her knees up and her scarf pulled over her nose. Mau had his bruised hand resting on his knee, palm up, the way Shoko would have told him to keep it if Shoko had been there. The fire made small noises. Somewhere behind them, Milim was snoring, very softly, in a way she would deny in the morning.

"You hit it," said Yuka, at last.

Mau looked at her.

"The thing. At the pond. The one that came up the bank." She did not look at him. She was looking at the fire. "It was going for my leg. And you hit it."

"Milim finished it."

"I know Milim finished it. I was there." Yuka pulled the scarf down off her nose. "You hit it first. With your hand. And now your hand's purple."

"It's not that purple."

"It's very purple, Oppa."

Mau looked down at his hand. It was, he had to admit, quite purple.

"Ciel helped," he said. "One strike. Then I closed it. I didn't do anything big."

"I know you didn't do anything big," said Yuka. "That's what I'm trying to say." She was quiet for a moment. "You could have. You could've done something huge. Everyone knows you could. And you just—hit it. Once. Small. And then let Milim do the rest." She looked at him at last. "That's the bit I wanted to say thank you for. Not the hitting. The small."

Mau did not say anything for a long moment.

"Thank you," he said. "For noticing the small."

Yuka went pink above the scarf and pulled it back up over her nose.

* * *

"Do you think they're doing their plans?" said Yuka, a while later.

Mau looked at her. "Who?"

"Grandpa and Grandma." She said it into the scarf. "Yuta and Maki. They said they had *plans*. While we were gone. And then Grandma said they *hadn't* made any plans, and that was the plan." She made a face. "Which is gross."

Mau tried very hard not to laugh.

"It's not funny," said Yuka.

"It's a little funny."

"It's *gross*." She kicked at the edge of the fire with her boot. "They're my grandparents. They're supposed to be old. They're supposed to sit in chairs and tell me I'm doing everything wrong." She paused. "Instead they're barely older than me and they look at each other at breakfast and I have to leave the room."

"You came all this way," said Mau. "Through a coin. To be with them."

"I know."

"And now you're in a frozen forest instead."

"I know." Yuka was quiet for a moment. "I wanted them to have a day. Without me. Without anyone. They never get one. Grandpa's always teaching someone, or fixing someone, or watching the walls for you. And Grandma's always training, or on the gate list, or being Grandma." She looked at the fire. "So when Frieren said she was making a list, I thought, if I go, they can have a day. A real one. Just them."

Mau looked at her for a long time.

"That's very kind," he said.

"Don't tell them."

"I won't."

"I mean it. If you tell them, Grandma will make a face, and Grandpa will say something nice, and I'll have to leave the room again."

"I won't tell them," said Mau. "I promise."

Yuka nodded, satisfied.

* * *

The stars moved. The fire burned low. Mau fed it a piece of wood from Stark's pile, and Milim, half asleep behind them, said "wrong one" without waking up, and Yuka had to put both hands over her mouth to keep from laughing.

"Do you miss it?" said Mau, when she had stopped. "Your world."

Yuka thought about it.

"Small things," she said. "Not big things. The big things are here. Grandpa and Grandma are here. My dog is here." She glanced down at her own shadow, where the white dog slept. "But small things. There was a shop near our house that made a kind of sweet bread. With red bean inside. I used to get one every day after training." She was quiet. "Momo can't make it. I asked. She tried twice. It wasn't the same."

"You could describe it to her again."

"I did. Twice." Yuka pulled her knees up tighter. "It's not the bread. Not really. It's that I can't explain it right. Whatever I say, it comes out wrong. And then I think, if I can't explain a bread, how am I going to explain anything else? About where I'm from. About what it was like." She stopped. "It's like the thing you said. At the meeting. About the Trial. That some things you can't tell anyone, even if you want to."

Mau did not say anything.

"Sorry," said Yuka. "That's not the same. Your thing is huge. Mine is a bread."

"It's the same shape," said Mau.

Yuka looked at him.

"Wanting someone to understand a thing," he said. "And not being able to get it across. And feeling like that means they never will." He looked at the fire. "Size doesn't matter much. It's the same shape."

Yuka was quiet for a long time.

"When we get home," she said at last, "will you come to the kitchen with me? When I try to explain it to Momo a third time?"

"Yes."

"You won't help. You'll just stand there."

"I'll just stand there," Mau agreed. "And look very serious about the bread."

Yuka laughed, a short surprised sound into the scarf.

"Okay," she said. "Deal."

* * *

When their watch was over, Mau woke Fern, and Yuka crawled back to her sleeping roll beside Milim.

But before she lay down, she stopped, and turned round, and looked at Mau sitting there in the firelight with his purple hand on his knee.

"Oppa."

"Mm."

"The points. The five. You still haven't spent them?"

"Not yet."

"Good," said Yuka. "Don't spend them on anything stupid."

"I'll try."

"Don't spend them on *flying* either. You already fly. Badly. Two fingers."

"A metre, now."

"Badly," said Yuka firmly, and lay down, and pulled her blanket over her head.

Mau sat a little longer, looking at the place where she had been.

Then he went to his own roll, and Frieren lifted the blanket without opening her eyes, and he lay down beside her, and his hand throbbed, and he did not mind at all.
