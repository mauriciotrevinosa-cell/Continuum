# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 23 — Not A Kitchen Problem

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 23
**Continuity state:** Momo uses the shared meal coverage for an outing with Okarun, Vamola and Jiji; their game and affection continue without resetting Okarun's memory or assigning her another service task

*When: Day 291, late afternoon.*

Momo had one shoe on when Jiji asked whether they should bring a pot.

She looked up at him.

"Why?"

"For the river."

"We're not cooking the river."

"I meant carrying water."

"There'll be water at the river."

Okarun made a sound behind his hand. Jiji looked at him with profound disappointment.

"I'm thinking ahead."

"We have a bottle," Momo said. She finished with the second shoe and stood. "We have something to eat. Nobody needs me back to tell them where the bowls are. We're going."

Vamola appeared beside the door, already eating part of the something. Momo took the bag from her, inspected the remaining bread, and decided it would survive the journey.

They stayed on the path inside the protected boundary, where the river widened around a shallow bend. Okarun carried the bottle. Jiji carried a folded bundle of paper he would not explain. Vamola followed a bright insect until it chose a direction none of them were going, then came back.

At the bend, Momo found the flat patch of grass she wanted and sat down before anybody could ask her to organize something else.

Jiji opened the bundle.

Boats.

Five folded paper boats, one so high at the prow that Momo suspected it would fall over standing still.

"I made you one," he told Vamola.

She accepted it with both hands.

"What does it do?"

Jiji glanced at the river.

"In theory, an impressive distance."

Okarun took the smallest boat. One corner had been folded beneath the hull instead of inside it. He turned it over and looked at Jiji.

"Was this on purpose?"

"An experimental design."

"Which means no," Momo said.

She held out her hand. Okarun passed it over, then crouched beside her while she opened the crooked corner far enough to put it right.

His knee touched hers. Neither moved it.

"There."

He studied the change. "That makes more sense."

"Don't tell him. He's invested in the experiment."

"I can hear you," Jiji said.

Vamola had put her boat in the water already. It was floating upside down.

"Is that good?"

All three of them went to the edge.

The current carried it toward a clump of grass. Jiji caught it with a long twig, turned it over, and showed her where the open part needed to be.

She watched carefully. Tried again.

This time it moved away upright.

Her face changed with such immediate delight that Momo forgot the laugh she had been preparing.

They chose a starting place upstream of the bend. A line of stones, a count, no hands after release. The winner had to pass the pale root on the opposite bank without help.

"No powers," Momo added when Jiji gave her a look.

"I didn't say anything."

"You're loud when you don't say anything."

Okarun held her boat while she found her footing. She took it back and waited until the others had crouched beside them.

Jiji counted down.

His towering prow entered the water magnificently, tipped, and filled.

Vamola's boat turned in a slow circle. Okarun's found the current first. Momo's caught against a stone beside her shoe.

She stared at it.

"No hands," Okarun reminded her very softly.

She turned toward him.

He was looking at the river with an innocence he had not earned.

"You're enjoying yourself."

"A little."

Vamola cheered when her boat escaped its circle. It passed the root behind Okarun's and caught in the bank where they could retrieve it. Jiji was already designing a convincing explanation for the sinking of his own.

Momo picked hers out of the water after the race was over. One end had soaked through.

Okarun held out a dry one.

"We have enough for another."

She took it. "I'm winning this one."

He smiled without answering.

They raced until the paper grew soft. Then they retrieved the boats and spread what could be saved on a flat stone. Jiji sat down to eat and discovered that Vamola had been regarding the bread as a separate event rather than a future shared resource.

There was enough. Barely.

Momo leaned back on her hands and let them argue about how to divide the last piece. Okarun sat beside her, the damp little boat balanced on his knee.

"We could make better ones," he said.

"Don't let Jiji supervise."

"I heard that too."

Momo reached over and took Okarun's free hand.

He looked down at their hands, then at her. The small surprised warmth in his face made her want to tease him. She kept it for later and watched Vamola explain to Jiji why the best boat deserved the largest portion.

They returned with the wet paper bundled for the rubbish, the bottle empty, and nothing useful to report about dinner.

Momo thought that was an excellent result.
