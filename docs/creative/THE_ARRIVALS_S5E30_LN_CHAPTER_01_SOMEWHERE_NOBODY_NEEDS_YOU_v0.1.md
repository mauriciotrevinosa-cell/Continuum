# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 25 — Somewhere Nobody Needs You

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 25
**Continuity state:** R16. Gojo asks Shoko properly, after Mau's earlier advice ("ask her what she likes"); she chooses; the afternoon belongs to them; Mau's help ended before the date began; Shoko's cigarette stays unlit; no confession contest, no power demonstration

*When: Day 292, late morning into afternoon. The tower frame waits on the dry ground; nobody is going in today. Shoko has finished her morning checks.*

Gojo asked her in the clinic doorway, which was the one place Mau had specifically told him not to.

"Not where she works," Mau had said on the roof. "She can't say no properly where she works. She'll say yes so you'll leave."

Gojo had agreed this was very wise. Then, the next morning, he had stood outside the clinic for twenty minutes pretending to examine the drainage, and when Shoko came out with a basin of water to empty, he had said, before he could think of a better place:

"Would you like to go somewhere? This afternoon. With me."

Shoko looked at him over the basin.

He had his blindfold on. He had put it on that morning because it seemed more serious than the glasses and less serious than his bare eyes, and he had spent longer deciding that than he had spent deciding most things in his life.

"Where?" said Shoko.

"I don't know."

She waited.

"I mean," said Gojo, "I have a place. I have several places. Mau said the river bend farther along the inside path. Yuta said somewhere with shade. Rimuru said anywhere I wasn't talking." He paused. "But Mau also said I should ask you what you'd like. Before I improved the geography."

"He said that?"

"He said several things. That was the useful one."

Shoko emptied the basin into the drain. She took a long time about it.

"What do I like," she said, not quite a question.

"That's what I'm asking."

She set the basin down on the step and looked at him properly. Not the doctor's look, the one that counted things. The other one. He had not seen it very often in this world.

"Somewhere nobody needs me," said Shoko.

Gojo did not say anything.

"For an afternoon," she said. "Not a whole day. I'm not unreasonable. Just an afternoon where nobody comes running in with a cut, or a fever, or a boy who fell out of the sky. Where if someone gets hurt, it's somebody else's turn." She picked up the basin again. "Can you find that?"

Gojo thought about it.

"Yes," he said.

"Then yes," said Shoko, and went back inside.

* * *

He went to find Maomao first.

She was in the stillroom behind the clinic, sorting dried leaves into jars by a system only she understood. She did not look up when he came in.

"I need a favour," said Gojo.

"No."

"You haven't heard it."

"It involves Shoko," said Maomao. "You have the face." She put a lid on a jar. "What?"

"An afternoon. Nobody needs her. If someone gets hurt, it's your turn." He paused. "Or Kusuri's. Or both. I don't care who. Just not hers."

Maomao looked up at last.

She studied him for a long moment, the way she studied a mushroom she had not seen before and suspected of being interesting.

"That's not a favour," she said. "That's the job." She went back to her jars. "I'll tell Kusuri. If anyone comes looking for Shoko, I'll tell them she's dead."

"Please don't tell them she's dead."

"I'll tell them she's busy," said Maomao. "Which is worse. Nobody argues with busy." She paused. "Go away now. I'm working."

He went.

He went to Fern next, because Fern would know if anything was scheduled that needed a doctor, and Fern looked at the rota for a long moment and then crossed something out and wrote something else in its place, and said, without looking up, "Done."

Then he went to Rem, because the kitchen sent people to the clinic with burns more often than anywhere else, and Rem listened, and nodded, and said she would make sure nobody touched the big pot without a cloth that afternoon, and then said, very quietly, "Good."

He did not go to Mau. Mau had already helped. Gojo found, a little to his surprise, that he wanted to do the rest himself.

* * *

They walked out along the inside path at about two.

Not far. Past the workshop, where Wakana looked up from something and then very carefully looked down again. Past the bench Ori had drawn three faces for, which was now a perfectly good bench. Past the place where the path forked toward the river. Along the bank, under the trees, where it was cooler, to the bend Mau had told him about, where the river widened and slowed and the water went clear over pale stones.

Nobody else was there.

Gojo had brought a blanket. He had thought about bringing more: food, a book, the little cards Yuka had taught him, something to show he had planned. In the end he had brought a blanket and two cups and a bottle of water from the cold jar, because Senku's insert was working again and he had wanted one thing to be cold.

Shoko looked at the blanket.

"That's it?"

"That's it," said Gojo. "You said somewhere nobody needs you. I didn't want to bring anything that might need you."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she sat down on the blanket.

He sat down too. Not close. A careful distance. The distance he would have left on the roof at school, a very long time ago, when there were three of them and the third one was always the one in the middle.

He poured the cold water. She took the cup.

For a long time they did not say anything.

The river made its small sounds. A fish rose, once, and made a ring on the clear water. Somewhere upstream a bird was trying the same call over and over and giving up halfway, the one Mau said had forgotten the words. The light came down through the leaves in moving pieces and lay on the blanket and on Shoko's hands.

She did not take the cigarette out from behind her ear.

Gojo noticed that. He did not say anything about it.

"I can't do this," he said at last.

Shoko looked at him.

"Nothing," said Gojo. "Sitting. Not being needed." He turned the cup in his hands. "I don't know how. I've never once in my life been somewhere nobody needed me. Somebody always did. Even when I was locked in a box, the whole world needed me out of it." He looked at the river. "I keep waiting for someone to come running."

"Nobody's coming," said Shoko.

"I know. Maomao's going to tell them you're busy."

"Then sit," said Shoko. "Badly, if you have to. I'll sit well enough for both of us."

He laughed. It came out short and surprised.

He tried. He sat, and looked at the river, and did not say anything. It was very difficult. After about a minute he took his blindfold off, because it seemed silly to wear it by a river, and then he could see everything: every leaf, every stone under the water, every small fish. That made it harder, because there was so much to look at. But it also made it easier, because none of it needed anything from him.

Shoko watched him do it.

"You look less tired without that," she said.

"I'm always tired. You just can't see it."

"I'm a doctor. I can see it."

He looked at her.

"You came here dead," said Shoko quietly. "I came here alive. I still don't know which of us had the harder trip." She looked at the river. "I used to think you'd never have to be tired. That you were the one person in the world who'd never need fixing." She paused. "I was very wrong about that. For a long time."

Gojo did not say anything.

"I'm glad I get to see it now," said Shoko. "The tired. It means you're letting someone look."

* * *

At some point she lay back on the blanket and closed her eyes.

Gojo sat beside her and watched the river and did not move. He thought she might be asleep. Her breathing slowed. A leaf came down and landed on her sleeve and she did not brush it away.

He looked at her for a long time.

He had looked at a great many things in his life. He had seen through walls and around corners and into the structure of every technique ever turned against him. He had looked at the whole world, all at once, every day, and been very bored by most of it.

He was not bored now.

"You're staring," said Shoko, without opening her eyes.

"I'm looking at the river."

"The river's on the other side."

He looked at the river.

She smiled. He could see it, because he could see everything. She did not open her eyes.

"I used to know what you'd say," said Gojo, after a while. "Before. At school. I always knew. You'd say something dry and I'd say something stupid and he'd—" He stopped. "I always knew."

"And now?"

"Now I don't." He looked down at her. "Now I have to ask."

Shoko opened her eyes.

She looked up at him, lying on the blanket in the moving light, for a long moment. Then she held out her hand. Not reaching. Just open, palm up, on the blanket between them.

"Then ask," she said.

He looked at her hand.

"Can I?" he said.

"That's a good question," said Shoko. "Yes."

He put his hand in hers.

They stayed like that until the light went gold and the bird upstream gave up on its call entirely. Nobody came running. Nobody needed either of them. The cold water in the bottle went warm, and neither of them noticed.

When they walked back, at dusk, they did not hold hands. They walked side by side along the inside path, a little closer than they had walked out, and past the workshop, where Wakana looked up and then very carefully looked down again.

At the clinic door, Maomao was waiting with her arms folded.

"Nobody needed you," she said to Shoko. "Somebody cut their thumb. I fixed it. It was boring." She looked at Gojo. "You can do this again. Next week. I'll tell them she's busy."

Shoko looked at Gojo.

Gojo looked at Shoko.

"Next week," said Shoko.

"Next week," said Gojo.

He went to find Mau, afterward, because he thought he ought to say something. He found him on the bench under the ink tree with Frieren, reading.

Mau looked up.

Gojo opened his mouth. Then closed it.

"Good?" said Mau.

"I'm not telling you anything," said Gojo. "You've done enough."

He walked away. But Mau, watching him go, saw that he had not put the blindfold back on.
