# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 26 — First Light

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 26
**Continuity state:** R34. The telescope Byakuya and Senku began grinding in S4 V25 finally sees first light; Suika and Umaru invite Mau; the first stars of this sky get names, as promised under the ink tree ("Then we name them"); no astronomical claim beyond what the eye and lens show

*When: Day 293, night. Clear and moonless. The tower frame waits; nobody is going in until the day after tomorrow.*

Suika came to find him after supper with Umaru behind her, and the two of them had the expression of people who had agreed on a plan and were now taking turns being brave enough to say it.

"Oppa," said Suika. Then stopped.

Umaru nudged her.

"Senku finished it," said Suika, very fast. "The telescope. The lens. It's done. He finished it this afternoon and he hasn't told anyone because he says it isn't *calibrated*, but it's *done*, and Byakuya says the sky's clear tonight and there's no moon, and—" She took a breath. "And we want you to come. To the first look. Me and Umaru. We're inviting you."

Mau looked at her. Then at Umaru.

"You're inviting me," he said.

"It was Suika's idea," said Umaru. "I just made it official." She held up a small card. It said, in her square Ministry hand, *INVITATION (FIRST LIGHT). ADMIT ONE. PLUS ELF.*

"Plus elf," said Mau.

"She'll want to come," said Umaru. "Elves like old things. Stars are very old."

Frieren, who had been reading at the other end of the bench and appeared not to be listening, turned a page.

"Very," she agreed.

* * *

They had set it up on the flat part of the roof, at the east end, where Rimuru and Yuta and Mau sat on roof nights. Not because it was the best place for stars, Senku said, but because it was the only place on the whole settlement where the light from the house did not reach.

The telescope stood on three legs that Byakuya had made from timber offcuts and Wakana had planed smooth. The tube was rolled copper, beaten thin, joined with a seam that had been soldered and re-soldered until Senku stopped finding fault with it. At one end, the big lens: the one they had been grinding since midsummer, by hand, in the lab, a little every day. At the other, a small eyepiece made from one of the fort's spare glasses, which Suika had donated without being asked.

Senku was crouched beside it with a lantern turned down so low it was nearly out, adjusting a screw.

"It's not calibrated," he said, without looking up.

"We know," said Suika.

"The focus is approximate. The mount wobbles. The seam lets in light at one angle. The lens has a flaw near the edge that I can see and you can't, and if you look at anything bright it'll make a ring round it." He turned the screw a hair's breadth. "It's ten billion percent not ready."

"Then we'll look at something dim," said Byakuya.

He was sitting on the roof with his back against the chimney stack and his arms folded and his big face turned up at the sky, and he looked happier than Mau had ever seen him.

Senku looked at his father.

"You want to go first," he said. Not a question.

"No," said Byakuya. "I want *her* to go first."

He pointed at Suika.

Suika went very still.

"Me?"

"You told me the first tale," said Byakuya. "Under the tree. With the bird in it." He smiled at her. "That makes you the first one to see what's past the tree."

Suika looked at Senku.

Senku looked at her for a long moment. Then he stood up and stepped back from the eyepiece and held out his hand toward it, the way you hold a door open for someone.

"Glasses off," he said. "You'll see better without them. The lens does the work."

Suika took off her glasses.

She gave them to Umaru to hold, and Umaru held them very carefully in both hands, the way she held the Ministry slate. And Suika put her eye to the little fort-glass eyepiece, and was quiet.

She was quiet for a long time.

"Suika?" said Umaru.

"Shh," said Suika. "Suika is looking."

Nobody said anything. The roof was very dark. The stars were very many. Somewhere below, in the house, a door closed, and someone laughed, and the sound came up faint and small.

"There's more," said Suika at last. Her voice was very soft. "Than you can see. With eyes. There are ones behind the ones. Little ones. Hundreds. Where there's just dark when you look up, there's—" She stopped. "There's more."

Byakuya let out a long breath.

"Yeah," he said. "There always is."

* * *

They took turns.

Umaru went next, and announced that the stars were *adequate* in a voice that wobbled. Then Mau. Then Frieren, who looked for a very long time and did not say anything at all, and when she straightened up her eyes were bright in the faint light.

"In my world," she said, "I saw the stars from a great many places. Mountaintops. Deserts. The middle of the sea." She looked up at the sky with her own eyes. "I never once saw them through glass. Someone always said it would spoil them." She paused. "It doesn't spoil them."

Byakuya went last.

He knelt at the eyepiece, a big man folding himself down to look through a small glass, and he was quiet for longer than any of them. Senku stood beside him with the lantern. Mau saw his hand, the one not holding the lantern, close and open and close again.

When Byakuya sat back on his heels, his face was wet.

"I don't know a single one," he said. "I told Suika that. Under the tree." He wiped his face with the back of his hand, unashamed. "Forty years I looked at the sky in the village. I knew every star. I could navigate by them. And I don't know one of these." He looked at his son. "We said we'd name them."

"We said we'd name them," Senku agreed.

"So name one."

Senku looked at the sky for a long moment.

Then he crouched at the eyepiece, and moved the tube a little, very carefully, and looked, and moved it again. And stepped back.

"That one," he said. "Look. Low, to the east. Three in a row, close, and a fourth a little apart. Like a line that hasn't decided whether to finish."

Byakuya looked.

"What do you call it?"

Senku did not answer at once. He stood there with his hands in his pockets and his hair sticking up in the dark.

"The Hundred and First," he said.

Byakuya did not say anything. He did not need to. Suika put her hand over her mouth.

"Because it isn't finished," said Senku. To the sky, not to anyone. "Like the story. We're still in it."

* * *

After that, everyone named one.

Byakuya named a bright one high overhead *Lillian*, and did not explain, and nobody asked. Suika found a small faint one beside a bigger one and named it *the Bird*, because the bird was always there, she said, even if somebody added it later. Umaru named a red one *the Ministry*, and wrote it down on the back of the invitation card, and then, after thinking, added a second name for a dimmer star beside it: *Taihei (later)*. She did not explain that either. Mau saw it, and did not say anything, and his chest went tight.

Frieren took a long time.

She looked through the glass, and away, and through it again. And at last she pointed at a cluster of six small stars, close together, so faint that you could only see them properly through the lens.

"Those," she said. "Six."

"What are they called?" said Suika.

Frieren looked at them for a long moment.

"The Party," she said.

She did not say which party. Mau thought of the six of them in a frozen forest, and of four people a very long time ago, and he understood that both of them were in it.

Then they all looked at him.

"Your turn, Oppa," said Suika.

Mau went to the eyepiece.

He looked for a long time. He did not know what he was looking for. Stars, and stars behind stars, and dark, and more stars. He did not know the names of any of them in any world. He had come into this one with no memory of his own sky at all.

And then he saw one.

Not bright. Not dim. Just a small, steady star, a little apart from the others, low over the place where the hills were. It was not doing anything. It was just there.

He stepped back.

"That one," he said. "On its own. Over the hills."

Byakuya looked. "What do you call it?"

Mau thought about the clearing, and the grass, and waking with no name and nothing behind him. He thought about a voice saying *Still here* at the reset hour, every night, for longer than he could count.

"Still Here," he said.

Frieren did not say anything. But in the dark, her hand found his, and her cold foot found his ankle.

"One," she said, very quietly. So only he could hear.

"Still here," said Mau.

* * *

They stayed on the roof until the cold came down and Umaru began to shiver and pretend she was not.

Senku packed the eyepiece away in a cloth. Byakuya folded the legs. Suika put her glasses back on and blinked, and the sky went back to being ordinary, and she looked a little sad about it.

"We can look again," said Senku. Gruffly. "Tomorrow. If it's clear. I'll fix the seam."

"And name more?"

"There are a lot left," said Senku. "Ten billion, roughly."

"Then we'll need a lot of tomorrows," said Byakuya.

Senku looked at his father. Then, very slightly, so slightly that only Suika saw it in the dark, he smiled.

Umaru held up the invitation card. On the back, in her square hand, there were seven names now, written in the order they had been chosen.

"This is the official record," she said. "The Ministry will keep it."

Nobody argued.
