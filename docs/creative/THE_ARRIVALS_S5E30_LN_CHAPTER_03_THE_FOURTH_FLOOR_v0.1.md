# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 27 — The Fourth Floor

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 27
**Continuity state:** Third tower visit: Mau (owner) with Frieren, Fern and Stark; cleared floors persist and must be walked; floor 4 is a flooded underground passage with a lamp objective; Frieren plans inside Mau's gate at his invitation; no transfer checkpoint yet; level and points rise only in Mau's branch; no extraction

*When: Day 295, morning. Two hours outside agreed with Anko. Rimuru at home. Yuka has been told, firmly, that this is not her turn, and has accepted it with conditions.*

Frieren read the names on Anko's board three times before she would step up to the frame.

"Four," she said.

"Four," said Mau.

"The same four as the stars."

"Three of the same," said Fern. "And Mau."

Frieren looked at her.

"He's in the stars," said Fern. "I counted. Six. You and me and Stark and Mau and Yuka and Milim." She folded her arms. "The other party is somewhere else."

Frieren did not answer that. But something at the corner of her mouth moved, and she stepped up beside Mau at the dark frame.

Stark checked the axe one more time. He had checked it four times since breakfast.

"Afraid?" said Mau.

"Very," said Stark.

"Good," said Mau. "Me too."

Anko turned the big glass. Mau took out the key.

* * *

They walked the first three floors without stopping.

That was a strange thing in itself. The burning street, the sentry post empty now, nothing on it but scorch marks and the place where the net had scraped his hand. Down through the gutted building to the market, where the chain marks were still in the paving and Fern stopped to look at them and asked how heavy the chain had been, and Mau said *very*, and she said *that isn't a measurement*. Across the bridge over the dark, past the place where the sword had lain, with the lamps still lit in their strips. Up the broad stair.

Nobody attacked them. The cleared floors stayed cleared. Mau had known that, from the notice, from the second visit. It was still strange to walk through a place where things had tried to kill him and have it be quiet, as if the building remembered what had happened and had decided to leave them alone.

Frieren looked at everything.

She looked at the burning buildings the way she looked at a ruin in a book, with her head a little on one side, reading the shapes. She looked at the sign that hung from its bent support, with its marks that were almost words. She stopped at the dead traffic signal and looked up at it for a long time.

"What is this?" she said.

"I don't know," said Mau. "It was here."

"It's a light," said Stark. "Three lights. It's got three holes for lights."

"For what?"

Nobody knew.

Frieren looked at it a moment longer. Then she took a small notebook out of her coat and drew it, quickly, three circles one above the other in a box on a pole, and wrote *?* beside it, and put the notebook away.

"I'll ask Senku," she said.

* * *

The stair on the third floor went down, not up, after the first landing.

The pale four was painted on the wall at the turn. Below it, the stair went on into the dark, and the air that came up from it was cold and wet and smelled of stone and standing water.

Mau stopped at the top. The notice came up.

*Floor 4 of 100.*

*Objective: light the platform.*

The map below it showed a long rectangle, mostly dark, with a line running down one side and small marks at intervals along it.

"Platform," said Fern. "For what?"

"I don't know," said Mau. "I don't know what any of this is for."

Frieren looked down the stair into the dark.

"Then we find out by looking," she said. "Not by guessing." She turned to Mau. "It's your gate. You choose."

Mau looked at her.

"I'd like you to plan it," he said. "Inside. If you want. You're better at it."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment.

"I'm better at it," she agreed. Not boasting. Just true. "But it's still your gate. If you say stop, we stop."

"If I say stop, we stop," said Mau. "And if you say stop."

"Then we'll be stopping a great deal," said Fern.

Stark laughed, very quietly, and then looked embarrassed about it.

* * *

The platform was a long hall underground.

Water covered the floor. Not deep. Ankle height at the stair, rising toward one side, where the floor dropped away into a channel full of black water. Along the dry side, a row of tall lamps on poles, all dark. Along the wet side, the channel, and beyond it a wall with a long dark mouth in it, like a tunnel, going off into nothing.

At the far end, very faint, a single lamp was lit. Just one. Blue.

"Light the platform," said Frieren. She was looking at the row of dark lamps. "Those."

"How?" said Stark.

Frieren walked to the nearest lamp, through the shallow water, and looked at it. Up close it was a tall pole of dark metal with a glass housing at the top, and inside the housing, nothing. No wick. No oil. A small socket at the base of the glass, empty.

"It wants something put in it," she said. She looked back at the single blue lamp at the far end. "Like that one has."

Fern went and stood beside her and looked up at the empty socket.

"A crystal," said Fern. "Look at the shape. It's the shape of the ones from the antler thing."

Mau took out the crystal pouch. He had brought it because Anko had told him to bring it, the way she told him to bring paper. He took out one of the small ones.

Frieren held up her hand.

"Wait," she said.

They waited.

She was looking at the channel. At the black water, very still, and the dark mouth of the tunnel beyond it. She stood there for a long time, not moving, the way she had stood at the edge of the frozen forest in the Red Gate, reading something nobody else could read.

"Something's in the water," she said quietly. "Not near. Down the tunnel. Waiting." She looked at the dark lamps, one after another, all down the dry side of the hall. "When we light one, it'll come."

"How do you know?" said Stark.

"Because that's what I'd do," said Frieren, "if I were a thing in the water and someone was lighting my platform." She turned. "So we don't light them one at a time and get eaten one at a time. We light the far one first. Near the blue one. And we walk back toward the stair, lighting as we go, so the thing in the water has to come out into the light to reach us. And the stair is behind us the whole time."

Mau looked at her.

That was the plan. He could see it, the whole shape of it, the way he could see the shape of a lattice when Coco drew it. Light behind them. Stair behind them. The thing in the water coming up into more and more light.

"Yes," he said.

* * *

It went almost exactly as she said.

They waded to the far end, past all the dark lamps, keeping to the dry side, Stark first, then Frieren, then Mau, with Fern at the back watching the water. At the far end, by the single blue lamp, Mau put the first crystal in the first socket.

The lamp lit. Blue. Soft.

Somewhere down the tunnel, the black water moved.

"Next," said Frieren.

They walked back toward the stair, lamp by lamp. Mau put a crystal in each socket. Each one lit. The hall filled slowly with soft blue light, from the far end toward the stair, and the water in the channel began to shine, and somewhere in the tunnel a long, heavy shape was coming.

At the sixth lamp, it came out.

It was long and pale and flat, like a fish that had forgotten it was a fish, with a head that was mostly mouth and a row of plates down its back like the sentries' armour. It came up out of the channel in a long slithering rush, onto the platform, into the water at the dry side, toward them.

Into the light.

It hated the light. Mau could see it. It came forward with its eyes narrowed to slits and its whole long body flinching from the lamps, and that made it slow.

"Stark," said Frieren.

Stark went.

He did not charge. He waited until it lunged, and stepped aside in the shallow water, the way Mau had seen him step aside from the dragon, and brought the axe down where the plates met behind its head.

It screamed. A wet sound, like water going down a drain.

Fern's spell took it in the side before it could turn back to the channel. Frieren's took it in the head. It thrashed once, sending water up over all of them, and then lay still in the shallows, and blue sparks began to run along its pale flat body.

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Stark, standing in the water with the axe dripping, looked down at himself.

"I'm wet," he said. "All of me."

"You're alive," said Fern.

"Wet and alive."

"That's the best kind," said Frieren. She had stepped back, out of the splash, and was perfectly dry. Fern looked at her with deep suspicion.

"You moved," said Fern.

"I saw it coming."

"You could have said."

"I said *Stark*," said Frieren. "That was the important part."

* * *

Mau lit the last three lamps.

The platform was all light now, soft and blue from end to end, shining on the water and the dark tunnel mouth and the pale shape dissolving in the shallows. The notice came up.

*Platform lit. Route to Floor 5 opened.*

Level six. Twenty-five points, still unspent. He showed them, the way he always showed them now. Frieren looked at the number for a long moment.

"You haven't spent any," she said.

"Not yet."

"Umaru says not yet."

"Umaru says not yet," Mau agreed. "And Fern. And Shoko. And Yuka. And you, now."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

Frieren did not deny it.

There was no extraction outline under the pale thing. Mau checked, and did not try to make one appear. There were crystals, large ones, in the shallows where it had died, and Fern counted them into the pouch and frowned at how many lamps they had used to earn them.

"We spent nine," she said. "We got back five. Bigger ones." She held one up to the blue light. "I'll have to ask Anko how the shop counts them."

At the far end of the platform, past the place where the single blue lamp had always burned, a door had opened that had not been there before. Beyond it, a stair, going up. A pale five on the wall.

Frieren looked at it for a long time.

"We have time," she said slowly. "Inside."

"We have time," Mau agreed. "Not enough to walk back from five."

Frieren looked at him.

Then at the stair.

Then she took out her notebook and drew the platform, quickly, the long hall and the channel and the row of lamps, and wrote *4* at the top, and *the far one first* underneath, and put the notebook away.

"Next time," she said.

* * *

They walked home through four floors.

At the bridge, Frieren stopped to look at the lamps again. At the market, Fern stopped to measure the chain marks with her hand and declared them *heavy*. At the burning street, Stark stopped at the traffic signal and looked up at the three empty holes.

"I still don't know what it's for," he said.

"Senku will know," said Frieren.

"Senku will pretend to know."

"That's nearly the same," said Fern.

They came out of the frame at an hour and fifty-one minutes, wet to the knee, all four. Anko wrote it down. Then she looked at Stark, who was wet to the neck, and wrote that down too, in the margin, without being asked.

Yuka was waiting by the screen with her arms folded.

"Well?" she said.

Mau thought about it.

"Frieren lit a whole platform," he said. "From the far end first. So the thing in the water had to come into the light."

Yuka looked at Frieren.

Frieren looked back, perfectly composed, with the notebook in her hand.

"It's a good trick," said Yuka slowly.

"It's a very old trick," said Frieren. "I'll teach it to you. Next time you come."

Yuka's face did something very fast, and then went back to being folded.

"Fine," she said. "Next time."

That night, Frieren showed Senku the drawing of the three circles on a pole.

He knew it at once. She saw it go across his face before he said anything.

"It's a traffic signal," he said. "For roads. Red means stop. The others mean go, or careful." He looked at the drawing a moment longer than he needed to. "In my world, before the stone, there were thousands of them. On every corner. I grew up under them."

Frieren looked at him.

"Then the city in Mau's tower is from a world like yours."

"Or from a world that also had roads and needed to stop carts running into each other," said Senku. He handed the notebook back. "One lamp is one lamp. Don't build a theory on it." He paused. "But write down everything else you see in there. Signs. Shapes. Anything with writing on it. Ten billion percent."

Frieren went to bed very pleased, with a new page in her notebook headed *Things in the tower Senku knows*, and one line on it so far.
