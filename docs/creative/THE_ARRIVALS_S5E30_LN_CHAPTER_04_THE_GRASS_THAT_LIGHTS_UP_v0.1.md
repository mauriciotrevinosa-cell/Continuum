# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 28 — The Grass That Lights Up

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 28
**Continuity state:** R23 + R28. Ori proposes her own small plan and her parents fit around it; she tells them about something she and Milim found while they were busy; Nazuna, awake for her evening window, helps and stays; inside the protected boundary; no powers

*When: Day 296, from late afternoon to full dark. Hot all day. The fort has spent the afternoon in the Summer Annexe under the ink tree.*

Ori came through the connecting door with a plan.

Mau could tell it was a plan because she had written it down. She held the page against her chest with both hands, the way Umaru held the Ministry slate, and stood in the middle of the east room and waited until both of them looked up.

Frieren looked up from her book. Mau looked up from the strap he was mending.

"Tonight," said Ori. "After supper. When it's dark. I want you to come somewhere. Both of you." She took a breath. "It's my plan. Not a fort plan. Mine."

"Where?" said Mau.

"The long grass. By the north field. Inside the lime." She held up the page. It had a drawing on it: the house, very small, and the north field, and a wavy line for the lime, and a lot of small dots inside the line. "Milim and I found something. Three nights ago. When you were—" She stopped. "When you were doing the tower thing. And Frieren was reading with Fern. We went out late because Milim couldn't sleep, and we found it."

Frieren put her book down.

"Found what?"

Ori looked at the dots on her drawing. Then at them.

"It's better if you see," she said.

Mau looked at Frieren. Frieren looked at Mau.

"What time?" said Frieren.

Ori's face did something very fast, the way it did when she had expected an argument and not got one.

"When it's properly dark," she said. "Nazuna says she'll come too. She'll be awake." She paused. "She knows the grass better than anyone. She's out there every night."

* * *

Nazuna woke at dusk, the way she always did, and came down the stairs from the loft over Anko's bed with her hair in her face and her coat half on, and found Ori waiting for her at the bottom with the page.

"Is it tonight?" said Nazuna.

"Tonight."

Nazuna yawned enormously and looked at the page and pointed at one corner of it.

"They're thicker here," she said. "By the ditch. Where the grass is wet. I've been watching them. They come out a bit after full dark and go on until about midnight." She handed the page back. "I'll show you the best place."

"You've been watching them?" said Ori.

"Every night since they started," said Nazuna. "There's nothing else to look at out there in summer. The night shift's mostly me and the owls." She looked at Ori sideways. "I didn't tell anyone. I thought somebody small should find them first."

Ori stared at her.

"You knew we'd find them?"

"I hoped," said Nazuna. "Milim's very bad at sleeping. And you always go with her." She yawned again. "Let me eat something first. I'm not watching anything glow on an empty stomach."

* * *

They went out after supper, when the light had gone out of the sky over the hills.

Ori in front, with the page. Milim beside her, floating a hand's breadth above the path and forgetting not to. Mau and Frieren behind. Nazuna at the back, with her coat buttoned now and a lantern she did not light.

"No lights," said Nazuna, when Mau looked at it. "It scares them. You have to let your eyes get used to the dark."

They walked out past the end of the street, past the place where Mikasa stood every afternoon, along the edge of the north field. The grass was long here, waist-high on Ori, long and pale and dry on top, and there was a smell of warm earth and something sweet. Ahead, the white line of lime ran across the dark like a chalk mark on a slate.

Nazuna stopped them well inside it.

"Here," she said. "By the ditch. Sit down. Don't talk for a bit."

They sat down in the grass. Ori in the middle, with Milim on one side and Mau on the other, and Frieren on Mau's other side. Nazuna sat a little apart, with her knees up, the way she sat on top of Anko's red stake.

It was very dark.

Mau could see the shape of the hills, and the stars above them, and the line of lime, and nothing else. His eyes kept looking for light and finding none. He heard the grass moving, and an owl somewhere, and Milim breathing too loudly because she was trying very hard to be quiet.

Then, in the grass by the ditch, something lit up.

A small green-gold light. On. Off. On.

Then another, a little way off.

Then three more, all at once, rising out of the grass in slow drifting curves.

Then dozens.

Mau stopped breathing.

They came up out of the long grass all along the ditch, small soft lights, green and gold, blinking on and off in slow drifting rhythms, rising and falling, each one on its own path. They hung in the warm dark above the grass like stars that had come down to see what it was like near the ground. Some of them came close. One drifted past Mau's face so near he could have touched it, blinked once, and went on.

Nobody said anything.

Beside him, Frieren had gone completely still.

"Oh," she said. Very softly.

Ori turned her head and looked at her.

"Do you have them?" said Ori. "In your world?"

"Yes," said Frieren. She did not look away from the lights. "Some places. In some summers." She was quiet for a moment. "I haven't seen them in a very long time. A hundred years, maybe more. I always meant to go back to the place where they were. I never did."

Ori looked at her for a long time.

Then she reached out and took Frieren's hand.

* * *

Milim could not stay still for very long.

She lasted about five minutes, which Mau thought was a record, and then she floated up out of the grass, very slowly, into the middle of the lights. They did not seem to mind her. They drifted round her and past her and one landed, for a moment, on her hair, and blinked there, and she went perfectly rigid with delight and did not breathe until it flew away.

"Did you see?" she whispered, coming back down. "Did you see? It was on me."

"I saw," said Mau.

"It chose me."

"It was very lucky."

Milim sat back down beside Ori, glowing almost as much as the bugs.

Nazuna had not moved. She sat apart with her knees up, watching the lights, and then watching the four of them watching the lights. Mau saw her face in the faint green-gold glow. It was soft in a way he had not seen it before.

"You come out here every night," he said quietly. Not to disturb the others.

"Every night," said Nazuna. "On the night shift." She did not look away from the ditch. "In my world, I was awake when everyone else was asleep. Always. I liked it. The city at night is the best city." She paused. "But I never had anything like this. Just neon. And people who'd had too much to drink."

"Do you miss it?"

Nazuna thought about it.

"Sometimes," she said. "The neon. The noise." She watched a light drift up past Ori's head. "But I never had someone small to show something to. I didn't know I wanted that." She shrugged. "Turns out I do."

Mau did not say anything.

After a while, Nazuna got up and came and sat down in the grass next to Milim, without asking, the way people sat down at the long table. And Milim, without asking either, leaned against her.

* * *

They stayed until the lights began to thin, a little before midnight.

Ori had fallen asleep against Frieren's side. Milim was floating, asleep, a hand's breadth above the grass, and Nazuna had tied the end of her own coat-belt loosely round Milim's ankle without being asked, the way Ori did with the ribbon.

Mau carried Ori back. Frieren walked beside him, with Ori's page folded in her hand. Nazuna came behind, towing Milim gently by the belt like a very small, very sleepy kite.

At the north end of the street, Nazuna stopped.

"I'm going back out," she said. "Night shift." She handed Mau the end of the belt. "She'll want to go again. Tomorrow. Or the next night. They'll last a few weeks yet."

"Will you come?" said Frieren.

Nazuna looked at her in surprise.

"If you want," she said.

"We want," said Frieren. "Ori will want. She'll make a plan."

Nazuna looked at the sleeping girl in Mau's arms for a long moment. Then she smiled, very slightly, and turned back toward the dark field.

"Then I'll show her where they're thickest," she said, and went.

* * *

In the east room, Mau put Ori to bed on her side of the connecting door, and Frieren tied the ribbon on Milim's ankle and took Nazuna's belt off. Ori woke for a moment while they were doing it.

"Did you like it?" she said, half asleep. "My plan?"

Mau sat on the edge of her bed.

"It was the best plan anyone's had all summer," he said.

"Better than the tower?"

"Much better than the tower."

Ori smiled into her pillow.

"Frieren held my hand," she said. "I held hers. First."

"I saw," said Mau.

"She hasn't seen them in a hundred years."

"I heard."

Ori was quiet. Her eyes were closing.

"Then we'll go again," she said. "So it's not a hundred years again."

She was asleep before he could answer.

Mau went back through the connecting door. Frieren was sitting on the edge of their bed with Ori's page in her hands, looking at the drawing: the house, very small, and the north field, and the lime, and a lot of small dots.

She looked up when he came in. Her eyes were very bright.

"She wrote it down," said Frieren. "Her own plan. She made us a map."

"She's been making maps all summer," said Mau.

Frieren put the page on the shelf, next to the five flowers. Very carefully. Where she would see it every morning.

"I'm keeping this one," she said.
