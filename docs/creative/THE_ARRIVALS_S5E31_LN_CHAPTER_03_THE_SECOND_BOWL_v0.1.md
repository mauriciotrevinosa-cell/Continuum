# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 31 — The Second Bowl

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 31
**Continuity state:** R24. After lunch Yuta and Mau wash up; Yuta shares something of his own (he has been learning one of Momo's soups to cook for Maki) and gets real interest back; brotherly ease; no medical check, no technique talk

*When: Day 299, after lunch. The kitchen coverage is Yuta and Mau's turn. Momo has gone to the river with Okarun.*

Yuta washed. Mau dried. That was the arrangement, and it had been the arrangement since the first coverage week, and neither of them had ever discussed it.

The kitchen was hot. The windows were open but there was no wind, and the big pots were still giving off the last of the lunch heat, and the bowls came out of the washing water too hot to hold. Mau held them anyway, by the rims, with the cloth, and stacked them on the shelf where Momo kept them, bottom-heavy ones on the left, the chipped one separately on the top, because Okarun always put it on the bottom and Momo always moved it.

"You're doing the chipped one right," said Yuta.

"I've been told."

"Okarun hasn't."

"Okarun's been told," said Mau. "He just doesn't listen."

Yuta smiled at the washing water.

They worked for a while without saying anything. The clink of bowls. The slosh of water. Outside, in the yard, somebody was laughing, and Milim was explaining very loudly why a rule was unfair, and Ori was explaining back, more quietly, why it was not.

Then Yuta said, without looking up, "Can I ask you to taste something?"

Mau looked at him.

"Taste what?"

Yuta did not answer at once. He finished the bowl he was washing and handed it over. Then he dried his hands on his trousers, which Momo would have told him off for, and went to the back of the stove, where a small covered pot was sitting on the cool edge, away from the heat.

Mau had not noticed it. It had been there through all of lunch.

Yuta took the lid off.

Soup. Not much. A bowl's worth, maybe two. It smelled of leek and something green and a little of the good bacon.

"It's Momo's," said Yuta. "The leek one. Or it's trying to be." He looked at it with deep suspicion. "I've been learning it."

Mau put the cloth down.

"Since when?"

"Since the coverage started." Yuta found a clean spoon in the rack. "Momo said if I was going to be in her kitchen every fourth day, I might as well learn something properly. She taught me this one. I've made it four times." He held out the spoon. "This is the fifth."

Mau took the spoon.

He tasted it.

It was good. It was not quite Momo's. Something about it was a little flatter, a little shy, as if the soup were not sure yet that it was allowed to be soup. But it was good. It tasted like somebody had been very careful.

"It's good," said Mau.

Yuta watched his face.

"Really," said Mau. "It's good. It's not Momo's. But it's good."

"What's wrong with it?"

Mau thought about it. He was not a cook. He had burned things in two worlds, probably, though he only remembered one. But he had eaten a great deal of Momo's soup.

"It's quiet," he said. "Momo's is loud. Hers sort of grabs you. This one's—" He searched for it. "Polite. It's waiting for permission."

Yuta stared at him.

Then he laughed. Quietly, but for a long time, with the spoon still in his other hand.

"Momo said that," he said, when he could. "Almost exactly. She said, *you're cooking it like you're apologising to it*." He shook his head. "She said I needed more salt and less worrying."

"Did it help?"

"I put in more salt," said Yuta. "I'm still working on the worrying."

* * *

They finished the washing up with the little pot of soup sitting on the edge of the stove between them.

"Why the soup?" said Mau, stacking the last of the bowls. "Of everything Momo could teach you."

Yuta did not answer at once. He was wiping down the washing board, very carefully, more carefully than it needed.

"Maki," he said.

Mau waited.

"She looks after me," said Yuta. "All the time. She doesn't say so. She just does it. She notices when I'm tired before I do. She takes my cup when I've forgotten it. She makes me eat when I'm thinking about something else. When the gate went red, she stood at the stake and held my hand low, where I didn't have to stop watching to hold it back." He put the cloth down. "She's always looking after me. And I've never once cooked her anything."

Mau did not say anything.

"In my world," said Yuta, "there wasn't time. For cooking. For anything like that. There was always something coming, and somebody hurt, and something to do." He looked at the little pot. "Here, there's time. There's kitchen coverage. There's Momo, who'll teach anyone who stands in her kitchen long enough." He paused. "So I thought, I'll learn one thing. Properly. And one day I'll make it for her. Without telling her. And she'll come in tired from the gate list and there'll be soup."

Mau looked at him.

At Yuta, who could heal a body coming apart, who had held Mau together on a battlefield, who had stood at a fountain for five minutes not knowing what he would catch, standing in a hot kitchen worrying that his leek soup was too polite.

"She'll love it," said Mau.

"It's quiet."

"She'll love that it's quiet," said Mau. "Because it'll taste like you."

Yuta went very still.

Then he turned away, and put the lid back on the pot, very carefully, and did not say anything for a while.

"That's a very soft thing to say," he said at last. His voice was not quite steady.

"I've been around Frieren a long time," said Mau. "It rubs off."

* * *

When the kitchen was done, they sat on the back step in the shade with the little pot between them and two spoons, and ate the rest of it, because Yuta said there was no point keeping the fifth attempt when he was going to make a sixth.

It was better cold. Mau said so. Yuta said that was not a compliment to a soup.

"When are you going to make it for her?" said Mau.

"When it's loud enough."

"How will you know?"

Yuta thought about it, turning the spoon over in his fingers.

"When Momo tastes it and doesn't say anything," he said. "Momo always says something. When she doesn't, it's ready."

Mau nodded slowly. That made sense. That made perfect sense, about Momo.

"Can I taste the sixth?" he said.

Yuta looked at him.

"You'd want to?"

"I'm your brother," said Mau. "I want to taste all of them. Until it's ready. And then I want you to tell me what Maki's face does."

Yuta looked at him for a long moment. Then down at the empty pot.

"Okay," he said. "The sixth. Next coverage day."

"Make it louder."

"I'll try."

"More salt."

"Less worrying," said Yuta, and almost smiled. "I know."

They sat on the step until the shade moved off their feet. Then Yuta took the empty pot inside to wash it, and Mau went to find out why Milim thought the rule was unfair.

On the way past the kitchen window, he heard Yuta humming. Very quietly. Something that was not quite a song.

He did not say anything about it. He just went on smiling the whole way to the yard.
