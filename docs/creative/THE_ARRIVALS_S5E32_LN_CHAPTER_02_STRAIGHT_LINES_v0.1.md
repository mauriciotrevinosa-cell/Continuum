# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 34 — Straight Lines

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 34
**Continuity state:** R29. Coco shares a permitted exercise (plain pencil, no ink, no glyph) with Mau, with Qifrey present and her mother choosing to join; the atelier does not become Mau's laboratory; the ink's privacy holds

*When: Day 303, afternoon. Rain in the morning, cooler after. The Witch Hat room has its window open.*

Coco asked him at breakfast, very formally, with her hands folded on the table.

"Would you like to try an exercise? This afternoon. In the Witch Hat room." She paused. "It's not magic. Qifrey said I could show you because it's not magic. It's just lines."

Mau put down his spoon.

"Lines?"

"Straight ones. And curves. With a pencil." Coco looked at him very seriously. "It's what we do before. Before anything. To make our hands listen." She glanced at Qifrey, who was at the far end of the table with his tea. "Qifrey says it's the most important thing he ever taught us, and the most boring."

Qifrey, without looking up, said, "Both of those are true."

"I'd like to try," said Mau.

Coco's face did something quick and pleased, and then went back to being serious.

"Two o'clock," she said. "Don't be late. Agott hates late."

* * *

He was not late.

The Witch Hat room smelled of rain through the open window and of paper and, faintly, of ink, though there was no ink on the table. Coco had cleared it. There was a stack of plain paper, the rough kind that came from Hollis's cart, and a row of pencils, sharpened very carefully, and a small wooden ruler that Coco pushed to one side.

"You don't get the ruler," she said. "Nobody gets the ruler. The ruler is for afterward, to check."

Agott was at her table in the alcove, pretending to read. Tetia was on her bed, cross-legged, watching openly. Richeh was asleep against the wall, which was normal. Qifrey stood by the door, the way he always stood by doors.

And Coco's mother was on the end of Coco's bed, with her sewing in her lap, not sewing.

She looked up when Mau came in and smiled at him. He smiled back.

"Mama wanted to watch," said Coco. "Is that all right?"

"Of course."

"She's going to try too," said Coco. "After. She said so."

Coco's mother looked a little alarmed at that, but did not argue.

* * *

The exercise was this.

A line. Straight. From one side of the page to the other. In one stroke, without stopping, without lifting the pencil, without going back.

"That's all?" said Mau.

"That's all," said Coco. "Try."

He picked up the pencil. He put it on the left edge of the paper. He drew a line to the right edge.

It was not straight.

He looked at it. It had started straight and then, about a third of the way across, it had begun to drift upward, and by the end it was a gentle slope, like a road going up a hill.

"Hm," said Agott, from the alcove, without looking up.

"Again," said Coco.

He drew another one. This one drifted down.

"Again."

The third one wobbled in the middle, where he had noticed it starting to drift and tried to correct it, and the correction made it worse.

Coco looked at the three lines for a long time.

"You're thinking about it," she said.

"Of course I'm thinking about it."

"That's the problem," said Coco. "You're watching the pencil. You're watching the line. You're trying to make it straight." She took the pencil out of his hand and put it down. "You have to watch where it's going. The end. The edge. Not the line. Your hand knows how to go straight if you let it look at where it's going."

Mau looked at her.

"Who taught you that?"

"Qifrey," said Coco. "When I first came. I drew a hundred lines and they were all like yours." She pointed at the road-going-up-a-hill. "And he said, *you're looking at your feet, Coco. Look at where you're walking to.*"

At the door, Qifrey had gone very still.

"I said that?" he said.

"You said it on the first day," said Coco. "I wrote it down."

Qifrey did not say anything. But he turned his face toward the window, very slightly, for a moment.

* * *

Mau drew lines for an hour.

He looked at the right edge of the paper instead of the pencil. The lines got better. Not straight. But better. Drifting less. Wobbling less. On the twentieth, or the twenty-fifth, he lost count, he drew one that went from one edge to the other and did not seem to drift at all.

He stopped. He looked at it.

Coco picked up the ruler and laid it along the line.

There was a gap. In the middle. About the width of a pencil lead. The line bent, very slightly, very gently, away from the ruler and back.

"Close," said Coco.

"It looked straight."

"It looked straight," Coco agreed. "That's the first part. Looking straight. The second part is being straight." She put the ruler down. "That takes months. Agott's are perfect. Mine are nearly. Tetia's are perfect on Tuesdays."

"Only Tuesdays," said Tetia, from the bed, cheerfully.

"Why only Tuesdays?" said Mau.

Tetia thought about it.

"I don't know," she said. "It's a mystery."

Agott said, "Hm," again, but this time it sounded a bit like a laugh.

* * *

Then it was curves.

"Curves are harder," said Coco. "Because you can't look at the end. The end keeps moving." She drew one herself, a long smooth arc from one corner of the page toward the other, in one stroke, without stopping. It was beautiful. Even Mau could see it was beautiful. "You have to look at the whole thing at once. Where it starts and where it's going and the shape in between."

Mau tried. His curve looked like a worm that had been stepped on.

Coco looked at it.

"That's a very honest curve," she said.

"Thank you."

"It's honestly not a curve."

Tetia fell over on her bed laughing.

* * *

It was Coco's mother who surprised everyone.

She had watched the whole hour without saying anything. She had watched Mau's lines go from hills to almost-straight. She had watched Coco draw the beautiful curve. She had put her sewing down at some point and not picked it up again.

When Coco said, a little shyly, "Mama? Do you want to try?" she hesitated for a long moment.

Then she came to the table.

She sat down in the chair Mau gave up for her. She picked up a pencil. She held it the way you hold a needle, Mau noticed: lightly, between the fingertips, not gripping.

She looked at the right edge of the paper.

And drew a line.

It was straight.

Not almost straight. Straight. From one edge to the other, in one stroke, without drifting, without wobbling. Coco picked up the ruler and laid it along the line, and there was no gap at all.

The Witch Hat room went completely silent.

Coco's mother looked at the line. Then at her daughter. Then at the line again.

"Oh," she said. "Is that right?"

"Mama," said Coco. Very quietly. "How did you do that?"

Her mother looked down at the pencil in her fingers.

"I sewed," she said. "Every day. All my life. Hems. Seams. You have to look at where the seam is going, not at the needle. Or it wanders." She put the pencil down. "I didn't know that was the same thing."

Agott had come out of her alcove. She was standing behind the chair, looking at the line over Coco's mother's shoulder, with her arms folded and her face severe.

"Do another," said Agott.

Coco's mother drew another. Straight.

"Do a curve," said Agott.

Coco's mother looked at the page for a long moment. Then she drew a curve. A long, smooth, even arc, like the edge of a sleeve, from one corner toward the other.

It was not as beautiful as Coco's. But it was close.

Agott looked at it for a long time.

"Hm," she said. And this time it was definitely, unmistakably, a compliment.

* * *

Qifrey walked Mau to the door when it was over.

The light had gone gold through the open window. Coco and her mother were still at the table, side by side, drawing lines. Coco was showing her mother how to do a curve that went two ways at once. Her mother was laughing, softly, because hers kept going only one way.

"Thank you," said Qifrey quietly. "For coming. For drawing badly in front of them."

"I'm good at drawing badly."

"I know," said Qifrey. "That's why I asked Coco to ask you." He looked back at the table. "She needed to teach someone. Something she knows. Not something someone else knows that she's trying to learn. Something that's hers." He paused. "And her mother needed to find out she already knew something."

Mau looked at the two of them at the table.

"You planned that?"

"I planned the first half," said Qifrey. "I didn't know about the sewing." He almost smiled. "Nobody plans the good half."

He went back into the room and closed the door.

Through it, very faintly, Mau heard Coco say *Mama, again*, and her mother laughing, and the small scratch of two pencils on rough paper, going straight.
