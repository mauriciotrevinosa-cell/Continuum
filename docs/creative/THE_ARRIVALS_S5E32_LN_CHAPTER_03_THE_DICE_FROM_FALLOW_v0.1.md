# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 35 — The Dice from Fallow

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 35
**Continuity state:** R33. Maomao and Jinshi join an evening dice game at the long table with Mau, Umaru and Yuka; Maomao stays because she likes it, not to supervise; Jinshi talks about something other than his records; no acceleration of their relationship, Maomao is not reduced to a doctor

*When: Day 304, evening. Hollis left a set of bone dice from Fallow with the last load, "for the children", and the children have not let anyone else touch them for two days.*

The dice were Hollis's fault.

He had left them with the last load, in a small cloth bag, with a note in his careful merchant's hand: *For the children. A game from Fallow. Five dice, a cup, and a lot of shouting.* Umaru had confiscated them for the Ministry within an hour. Yuka had appealed. The appeal was still pending. In the meantime, the two of them had played the game forty-one times on the floor of the fort, and the shouting had been, as promised, considerable.

Tonight they had brought it to the long table, because Umaru said the floor of the fort was not a dignified place to lose.

"I haven't lost," said Yuka.

"You've lost nineteen times."

"Out of forty-one. That's not losing. That's statistics."

Mau sat down across from them because Umaru had pointed at the chair.

"Rules," said Umaru. She put the cup down on the table, upside down, and lifted it, and there were five small bone dice underneath, each with dots carved into the faces. "You roll all five. You keep the ones you like. You roll the rest again. Twice more. Then you count." She looked at him sternly. "Fives and sixes are good. Ones are terrible. Three of the same is very good. All five of the same is impossible."

"It's not impossible," said Yuka. "Milim did it."

"Milim cheated."

"She didn't cheat. She just looked at them very hard."

Mau picked up the cup.

* * *

He lost the first game, which was expected, and the second, which was also expected, and in the third game he rolled three fours on the first throw and Umaru looked at him with deep suspicion and Yuka shrieked, and then he rolled the other two and got a one and a two, and kept them by mistake, and lost.

"You kept the *one*," said Umaru.

"I panicked."

"You *never* keep the one."

"I know that *now*."

Maomao, passing behind his chair with a cup of tea, stopped.

She looked at the dice on the table. Then at the cup. Then at Umaru's tally, which was on the back of the Ministry slate and very long.

"Bone," she said. "From the ankle of a sheep. Probably." She picked one up and turned it over in her fingers. "Carved by hand. This one's not quite square." She put it down. "It'll come up two more often than it should."

Umaru stared at her.

"How do you know?"

"I've thrown dice before," said Maomao. "In a place where people threw a great deal of dice, for a great deal of money, and some of the dice were not quite square on purpose." She looked at the tally. "You should take that one out. Or everyone should know which one it is."

"That's cheating," said Yuka.

"That's *information*," said Maomao.

She did not leave.

Mau noticed. She stood there behind his chair with her tea, looking at the dice, and she did not go back to the clinic or the stillroom or wherever she had been going. She just stood.

"Do you want to play?" said Mau.

Maomao looked at him.

"I'm busy," she said.

"Are you?"

Maomao considered this honestly. She looked at her tea. She looked at the dice. She looked at the long table, where the evening was going on around them: Momo and Okarun arguing about a pot, Kessoku in the corner tuning, Milim upside down on the bench, the witches at their end with the good teapot.

"No," she said, with some surprise. And sat down.

* * *

Jinshi came in an hour later, looking for her.

He stood in the doorway of the common room with a ledger under his arm, because he had finished the evening receipts and wanted to tell her something about a line that did not add up, and he found her at the long table between Mau and Umaru with a cup in her hand and five bone dice in front of her and an expression of intense concentration.

He stopped.

Maomao did not look up.

"Sit down," she said. "You're in the light."

Jinshi looked at the ledger under his arm. Then at the dice. Then at her.

"I was going to show you—"

"Later," said Maomao. "I'm winning."

"She's winning," said Yuka, with great bitterness. "She's won four in a row. She knows which one's not square."

"Everyone knows which one's not square," said Maomao. "I told you."

"You told us and then you *kept winning*."

"Knowing is not the same as using," said Maomao. "Sit down, Jinshi."

Jinshi sat down.

He put the ledger on the bench beside him, very carefully, closed. He looked at it for a moment, as if he expected it to open by itself. Then he looked away from it.

"How do you play?" he said.

* * *

Jinshi was very bad at dice.

Not as bad as Mau. Nobody was as bad as Mau. But Jinshi kept the wrong ones, and rolled the right ones away, and once counted his score aloud and got it wrong by eleven, which made Umaru put her head down on the table.

"You keep the *books*," said Umaru, into the table. "You count *everything*. How are you bad at *counting*?"

"I count things that are written down," said Jinshi. "These keep moving."

"They're *dice*."

"Exactly," said Jinshi.

Maomao, beside him, was smiling into her tea. Mau saw it. It was a small smile, and she turned it into her cup before anyone else could see it, but it was there.

"In the palace," said Jinshi, after his fourth loss, "there was a game like this. With tiles instead of dice. The women played it in the afternoons, in the inner courts." He turned one of the dice over in his fingers. "I was never allowed to play. It wasn't proper. I used to hear them, through the walls. Laughing. Shouting when someone won." He put the die down. "I always wondered what it was like. To be on that side of the wall."

The table was quiet for a moment.

"And?" said Maomao. Without looking at him.

Jinshi looked at the dice. At Umaru, with her head still on the table. At Yuka, glaring at the tally. At Mau, who had kept a one again by mistake.

"It's very loud," he said. "And I'm very bad at it." He picked up the cup. "I like it."

Maomao did not say anything. But she moved the not-quite-square die across the table, very slightly, so it was in front of him.

"That one," she said. "It comes up two. If you want twos."

"I don't want twos."

"Then don't keep it," said Maomao. "But now you know."

Jinshi looked at her for a long moment.

Then he rolled.

* * *

They played until Momo came to clear the table and found all five of them still at it, with the Ministry slate covered in tallies on both sides and Umaru keeping score on her arm.

"It's late," said Momo.

"One more," said Yuka. "Jinshi's about to win."

Jinshi was not about to win. Jinshi was about to lose for the ninth time. But he had rolled three fives on his first throw and was looking at them with an expression of total astonishment, and nobody wanted to stop.

He rolled the other two.

A five. And a two. The not-quite-square one.

Four fives.

The table went silent.

"That's very good," said Maomao. Quietly.

"Is it?"

"It's very good," said Mau. "It's better than anything I've done all night."

"That's not hard," said Umaru. But she was looking at the four fives with something like respect.

Jinshi looked at the dice for a long time. Then he looked at Maomao.

"Did you know it would do that?"

"No," said Maomao. "Nobody knows. That's why it's a game." She stood up, and took her empty cup, and looked down at him. "You can show me the receipt line now. The one that doesn't add up."

Jinshi looked at the ledger on the bench beside him, closed, where he had left it two hours ago.

"Tomorrow," he said.

Maomao looked at him.

Then, for the second time that evening, she smiled. She did not turn this one into a cup.

"Tomorrow," she agreed, and went.

Umaru wrote, at the bottom of the Ministry slate, under all the tallies, very small:

*J. — four fives. (Witnessed.)*
