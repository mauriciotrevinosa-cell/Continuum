# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 36 — Twenty-Three

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 36
**Continuity state:** R60, part 1. Umaru decides on her own that the house is "ready and safe" (her S4 condition from "Two Older Brothers", Day 41: a real room with a door, a window and somewhere to cook; a place where he will not be scared); she tells Mau first, asks Fern by the List procedure, answers "what lives in him" (nothing but him); she uses the coin from the drawer marked LATER; the arrival itself is the next chapter

*When: Day 306, evening. Hot and still. The empty room at the end of the west wing still says For whoever comes. The fireflies are out in the north field again; Ori has taken Milim and Nazuna to see them.*

Umaru closed the shutter of the toll booth at dusk, and the fort knew at once that something was wrong.

The shutter was never down. It had been down exactly once, as far as anyone in the fort remembered, on a night in the winter, after the List. The OPEN sign with the hamster drawn beside it had faced the common room every day since. Now the sign was gone, rolled up against the ceiling of the booth, and there was a line of lamplight underneath the shutter, and from inside there was no sound at all.

Yuka saw it first. She went and stood in front of the booth and looked at the shutter for a long time. Then she went to find Mau.

"She's in there," said Yuka. "With the shutter down. She's not crying. I listened." She paused. "I think that's worse."

Mau put down his book.

* * *

He sat on the step outside the booth, with his back against the shutter, the way he had sat once before.

For a long time neither of them said anything.

The common room was almost empty. Most of the house was out on the roof or in the yard, where it was cooler. The fort's curtain was tied back. The cushions were empty, more of them now than in the winter, because the fort had grown. On the far wall, by the light of the lamp that was always left burning over the scratches, he could see the old chalk board with the List on it, rubbed out and rewritten so many times that it was more smudge than writing.

"Non-citizens," said Umaru's voice through the shutter, "may sit on the step."

"I know the law," said Mau.

"You've broken it several times since."

"I thought the fort let me in," said Mau. "Eventually. It's on a slate somewhere."

A pause.

"Provisionally," said Umaru.

Mau waited.

He heard something click on the counter inside the booth. A small sound. Metal on wood. The sound of a coin being turned over, and over, and over.

"I opened the drawer," said Umaru.

* * *

"I've been checking," she said. "For a month. Since the gate moved inside. Every night, after the fort goes to bed. I've been checking."

"Checking what?"

"The list." The coin clicked. "My list. From the winter. You remember. *Ready and safe.*"

"I remember."

"A room," said Umaru. "A real one. With a door. With a window. In the real house, not a cushion, not a corner." The coin clicked again. "There's a room. The empty one, at the end of the west wing. *For whoever comes.* I went and stood in it last night. It has a window. It looks at the ink tree. The door shuts properly. Wakana fixed the latch."

"I know. I watched him."

"Somewhere to cook," said Umaru. "Because he'll want to cook. He always wants to cook." Her voice did something. "There's the kitchen. There's the coverage. Momo said anyone who stands in her kitchen long enough gets taught something. Yuta's learning soup. Mau, *Yuta's learning soup*." The coin stopped. "If Yuta can learn soup in Momo's kitchen, my brother can cook in it."

"He can," said Mau.

"And safe."

The common room was very quiet.

"That's the hard one," said Umaru. "Safe. I said I wasn't bringing him here to be scared. He's been scared for me his whole life." She was quiet for a moment. "And it isn't safe. Not really. There are things in the woods. There's a clerk in the river towns saying we're monsters. Sukuna came and stood in the field. The gate went red with six people in it." Her voice went very small. "It's never going to be *safe*. Not the way I meant in the winter."

Mau did not say anything.

"But I counted," said Umaru. "Last night. After I stood in the room. I came back here and I counted." He heard paper rustle. "Every time something bad happened since the winter. And then every time something bad happened and somebody stood in front of it. Mikasa at the north end. Aira at the bean-girl's door. Frieren lighting the platform from the far end first. You standing in the light all night so the thing in the road would look at you instead of the cottage." The paper rustled again. "The second list is longer."

Mau looked at the shutter.

"It's not safe," said Umaru. "But nobody here lets anything happen to anyone without standing in front of it first. Not one time. I counted." She paused. "That's not the same as safe. But I think it might be better."

* * *

The shutter went up.

Umaru was sitting on the floor of the toll booth in her sash and her hood, exactly where she had sat in the winter. But her face was not wet this time. It was very still and very serious, the President's face, the one that had counted blankets in an evacuation. She had two sheets of paper in her lap, covered in her square handwriting. And in her hand, the coin.

Beside her, the drawer under the counter stood open. On the front of it, in crayon, faded now: *LATER.*

"Twenty-two times," said Umaru. "I moved it twenty-two times in the winter. And I said twenty-two was the last one." She held it up. Small, blank, warm in the lamplight. "This is twenty-three."

Mau looked at the coin. Then at her.

"You're telling me first," he said.

"You're one of the two," said Umaru. "Two older brothers. You said. One from before and one from after." She looked at him very steadily. "I'm not going to do it without telling the one from after."

Mau did not say anything for a moment. He found he could not.

"I'm scared," said Umaru.

"I know."

"Not of the coin. Of the floor." She looked down at the boards of the booth. "That he'll walk in and I'll be her again. The minute I see him. On the floor in a blanket saying *Onii-chan, chips*."

"You might be," said Mau.

Umaru looked up sharply.

"For a minute," said Mau. "You might. People do that, with people from before. Frieren still sleeps in when Fern comes to wake her, and she's been alive a thousand years." He leaned his head back against the booth. "But then you'll get up. Because there's a fort that needs its President. And a Ministry with forty-one rulings on dice. And Yuka needs someone to beat at cards." He paused. "And I'll be standing right there. I've seen the President. I won't let you forget her."

Umaru looked at him for a long moment.

"You said that in the winter," she said.

"It's still true."

"It's on your sash," said Umaru. And something at the corner of her mouth moved, very slightly.

* * *

They went to Fern together.

Fern was at the head of the long table with the ledger, finishing the day's entries, the ones Jinshi had left with their questions marked. She looked up when they came in. She looked at Umaru's face, and at the coin in her hand, and at Mau standing a pace behind her, and put her pen down.

"Sit down," said Fern.

Umaru sat on the bench across from her. Mau stayed standing.

"I want to use my coin," said Umaru. "For my brother. Taihei."

Fern opened the ledger to the page of the List. Mau could see it from where he stood. The old lines, in different hands, with dates and *HERE* written beside them. Nijika's. Rem's. Yuta's. Coco's. Senku's. And near the top, in Umaru's own square letters, a line that had been there since the forty-first day, with nothing after it.

*Umaru: Taihei. LATER.*

Fern looked at it for a long time.

"Is he ready?" said Fern. "Is it ready? You said ready and safe."

Umaru put the two sheets of paper on the table and pushed them across.

Fern read them. Both. Slowly, the way she read everything that mattered. The first list, short, in Umaru's square hand: *room (door, window) — kitchen — Momo says yes — safe?* And the second one, long, the one with every time something bad happened and somebody stood in front of it.

When she had finished, she was quiet for a while.

"There's one more question," said Fern. "Since the summer. Every petition." She turned the ledger back a page, to the line in her own hand from the night of the soup. *Every petition from now on: ask what lives in them.* She looked at Umaru. "Does anything live in him? That isn't him?"

Umaru stared at her.

Then, very suddenly, she laughed. A small, startled, wet laugh.

"No," she said. "Nothing. He's just—he's just Onii-chan. He works in an office. He cooks. He worries. He buys me chips." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. "There's nothing in him but him. There's never been anything in him but him."

Fern looked at her for a long moment.

Then she picked up her pen, and beside the old line, where it said *LATER*, she drew one neat line through the word. And wrote, beside it, very small, the date.

"Yes," said Fern.

* * *

Umaru did it in the toll booth, with the shutter half up.

Not alone. She had asked Mau to sit on the step, and he sat on the step. And Yuka had come back from wherever she had gone and was sitting on the floor of the fort just inside the curtain, very quiet, with her knees up. And at some point Frieren had come in from the yard and stood at the end of the common room, not coming any closer, just there.

Umaru sat on the floor of the booth with the coin in both hands.

She closed her eyes.

Mau did not know what she thought about. He would never ask. But he could guess. An apartment in another world. A man coming home late from an office with a bag of groceries, tired, and taking off his shoes at the door, and finding his little sister on the floor in a blanket, and not once, not one single time, telling her to leave.

The voice came.

From nowhere. From inside the coin, and inside everyone's head in the common room at once.

*You are sure?*

*Taihei Doma.*

Umaru did not open her eyes.

"Yes," she said. Very clearly. The President's voice. "I'm sure. I'm ready."

The coin went warm. Then warmer. Then so warm that she gasped, and opened her hands.

There was nothing there. Her palms were pink.

She sat on the floor of the toll booth and looked at her empty hands for a very long time.

Then she looked up, through the half-open shutter, at Mau on the step.

"Eighth bell," she said. "That's when they come. In the morning."

"Eighth bell," said Mau.

"Arrival House is closed," said Umaru suddenly. Her face went white. "Jinshi closed the book. Nobody's on the desk. Nobody's—"

"It's not locked," said Mau. "It's just closed. In case." He stood up. "And I know someone who'll want to light the stove."

* * *

The register girl was in the kitchen, drying cups, when Mau found her.

He did not have to explain very much. He said *Umaru's coin*, and *eighth bell*, and *Arrival House*, and the register girl put down the cup she was drying and the cloth, very carefully, side by side on the counter.

"The book's on Fern's shelf," she said.

"I'll ask Fern."

"The stove needs lighting by six. Or the hall's cold."

"It's summer."

"The hall's always cold," said the register girl. "It's the stone floor." She was already taking off her apron. "And the bed in the room at the end of the west wing needs making. With the good sheets. Not the ones from the refuge. The good ones."

She went past him toward the door. Then she stopped, and turned round.

"Who is he?" she said. "Umaru's brother. What's he like?"

Mau thought about it. About a man in another world, who had worked all day at an office and then come home and cooked, and cleaned, and done his little sister's laundry, and never once told her to leave.

"He's someone who's going to want to help you make the bed," said Mau. "And you're going to have to tell him no."
