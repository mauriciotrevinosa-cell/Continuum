# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 37 — The Eighth Bell

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 37
**Continuity state:** R60, part 2. Taihei Doma arrives in the intake hall of Arrival House at the eighth bell; the register girl has reopened the hall for the morning; Umaru meets him standing, as the President, not on the floor; he learns where he is and what the coin means; the register gets its first name since it was closed

*When: Day 307, morning, the eighth bell. The register girl lit the stove at five. Fern lent her the book. Half the house is in the plaza, pretending not to wait.*

The register girl had the stove lit by five, and the hall was still cold.

It was always cold. It was the stone floor. She knelt in front of the stove with the door open and fed it, piece by piece, the way she had fed it every morning for a year and a half before the book was closed, and watched the light come up in the high windows, grey and then gold. She did not hurry. There was nothing to hurry for until the eighth bell.

At six, Jinshi came in.

He did not say anything. He looked at the stove, lit. At the desk, with the book open on it to a fresh page and a pen beside it, sharpened. At the benches along the walls, where she had wiped the dust off at half past four. He looked at all of it for a long moment.

Then he went and stood in the doorway, with his back to the plaza, the way he had always stood.

"You don't have to," said the register girl. "Fern gave you a new job."

"It starts at nine," said Jinshi. "The bell is at eight."

She did not argue.

* * *

By half past seven, the plaza was full of people pretending not to wait.

The fort was there, in a heap at the edge, in hoods. Yuka at the front of it, very straight. Kessoku had come out with their instruments, as if they might be about to play, which they were not. Momo was at the kitchen door of the main house with a cloth over her shoulder and a pot of something already on, though nobody had asked her. Gojo was leaning against the well, blindfold up, eyes bright. Frieren was on the bench under the ink tree with a book she was not reading.

Umaru stood in the middle of the plaza, alone.

She had not slept. Mau could see it in her face. She had come out of the fort at dawn in her sash and her hood, the hamster hood, the one she wore when she was President, and stood in the middle of the plaza facing the door of Arrival House, and not moved.

Mau went and stood beside her. Not close. A pace to one side.

"You can come in with me," said Umaru, without looking at him. "If you want. When it's time."

"I'll stand in the door," said Mau. "With Jinshi. You go in."

Umaru looked at him at last.

"You're sure?"

"It's your brother," said Mau. "You go first."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the President's nod, and turned back to face the door.

* * *

At the eighth bell, the air in the middle of the intake hall moved.

Not a light. Not a sound. The air, the way air moves over a road in summer. The register girl stood up behind her desk with her pen in her hand. Jinshi did not move from the doorway. Umaru, who had walked in at the first stroke of the bell and stopped in the middle of the floor, did not breathe.

And then there was a man standing on the boards.

He was tall, and thin in the way of people who work long hours and forget to eat, and he was wearing a shirt with the sleeves rolled up and an apron over it. Ordinary trousers. Socks, no shoes. There was flour on his hands. He was holding a wooden spoon.

He looked down at the spoon. Then up, at the high windows, and the clay walls, and the stove in the corner, and the register girl behind her desk.

"Huh," said Taihei Doma.

His voice was quiet. Tired. Very calm, the way a voice is calm when its owner has decided that panicking would not help and would also be a lot of effort.

"I was making dinner," he said, to the register girl. Conversationally. "Curry. The kind she likes." He looked at the spoon again. "I'd just put the onions in."

The register girl did not seem able to say anything.

Then Taihei turned round. And saw the girl standing in the middle of the floor.

* * *

He did not say anything for a long time.

He looked at her. At the hood, with its small round ears. He knew the hood. Mau could see him know it. And then he looked at the rest of her, and his face changed, slowly, the way a face changes when it is looking at something familiar and finding it different.

She was standing up.

That was the first thing. Mau saw him see it. She was standing in the middle of the floor, very straight, with her hands at her sides and her chin up. Not slouching. Not lying down. Not curled in a blanket on a floor with a game controller and a bag of chips. Standing, in a sash with something embroidered on it, and boots, with her hair tied back, and her face very still and very serious and very wet.

"Umaru?" said Taihei.

It came out very small.

"Onii-chan," said Umaru.

She did not run.

She stood there, very straight, the President, and her face crumpled, all at once, and she put her hands over it. And she did not run to him. She stood in the middle of the intake hall with her hands over her face and her shoulders shaking and her hood falling back.

Taihei put down the wooden spoon on the floor.

He crossed the intake hall in five steps, in his socks, with flour on his hands. And he put his arms round his little sister, very carefully, the way you hold something you thought you had lost, and she put her face against his apron and held on, and neither of them said anything at all.

In the doorway, Jinshi turned his face toward the plaza.

The register girl sat down very suddenly behind her desk.

* * *

It was a long time before either of them said anything.

When Taihei did, it was into her hair.

"You've been gone," he said. "So long. Nobody knew. The police. Everyone. They looked. I looked." His voice cracked. "I kept making dinner. Every night. In case. I kept making the curry you like. In case you came home and wanted it."

Umaru made a sound against his apron.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I couldn't, there wasn't a way, there was a voice and a field and I couldn't—"

"Shh," said Taihei. "Shh. It's all right. You're here. I'm—" He stopped. He looked over her head, at the high windows, at the clay walls, at Jinshi in the doorway and Mau beside him. "Where *is* here?"

Umaru stepped back. Not far. She kept hold of his apron with both hands. She wiped her face with her sleeve, hard, the way children wipe their faces when they have decided to stop crying.

And she told him.

Not everything. Not yet. She told him the important parts, in order, standing in the middle of the intake hall holding her brother's apron. A world. A Goddess. A field, and waking up in it. A house. People from everywhere. A fort. She was President. She had a constitution.

Taihei listened to all of it. His face did several things. At *Goddess* it went very still. At *President* it did something that might have been the beginning of a laugh and was not quite.

"And you're here," said Umaru, "because of a coin."

"A coin?"

"Everyone who stayed got one. You can use it to bring someone. From home." She swallowed. "But it only works if you love the person. And they love you back. Both. Or it goes cold, and nothing happens."

Taihei looked at her.

"It didn't go cold," said Umaru. Very quietly. "It went warm. And you came."

Taihei did not say anything.

He stood in the intake hall in his socks and his apron, with flour on his hands, looking at his little sister. And then his face did something Mau had seen on a great many faces in this hall. On Senku's father. On Coco's mother. On Ram. The face of somebody understanding that they had been wanted, across a distance nobody could measure.

He put his floury hand on top of her hood.

"Of course it went warm," said Taihei. "Idiot."

Umaru laughed. Wetly, helplessly, through her crying.

* * *

The register girl wrote it down.

She did it while they were still standing in the middle of the floor. She picked up the pen, and found the fresh page in the book, and wrote the date at the top in her best handwriting, which was still not very good. And under it, the first name in the book since it was closed:

*Taihei Doma.*

She looked at it. Then she looked at the two of them, the tall tired man in the apron and the girl in the sash with her hood fallen back, and added, under the name, small:

*Brother of the President.*

Taihei saw her writing. He turned round, with his hand still on Umaru's head.

"Is that a register?" he said.

"Yes," said the register girl.

"Do I need to sign it?"

The register girl looked at him in astonishment. Nobody had ever asked to sign it. People arrived and she wrote them down and that was all.

"You don't have to," she said.

"I'd like to," said Taihei. "If that's all right. It seems polite."

He came to the desk. He looked at the page, at his name in her handwriting, and the line under it. He looked at it for a long time. Then he took the pen, and wiped his floury fingers on his apron first, and signed underneath, neatly, in a script the register girl could not read.

He handed the pen back.

"Thank you," he said. "For lighting the stove. It's very cold in here."

"It's the stone floor," said the register girl faintly.

"I thought so," said Taihei.

* * *

In the doorway, Mau had not moved.

Umaru looked over at him. Her face was red and wet and she was holding her brother's sleeve now, the way she had held the apron.

"Onii-chan," she said. "This is Mau."

Taihei turned. He looked at Mau standing in the doorway beside Jinshi.

"He's—" said Umaru. She stopped. She started again. "He's my brother too. Here. From after." She lifted her chin. "You're from before. He's from after. I have two."

Taihei looked at Mau for a long moment.

Mau did not know what to say. He had not planned anything. He stood in the doorway of Arrival House with the morning behind him, and the whole plaza behind that, and looked at the tall tired man in the apron who had made curry every night for months in case.

"She counted blankets," said Mau. "In an evacuation. Nobody asked her to. She just did it." He paused. "I thought you'd want to know."

Taihei's face did something very complicated.

Then he crossed the hall, and held out his floury hand.

"Thank you," he said. "For looking after her."

Mau shook it.

"She looks after me," he said. "Mostly."

Taihei looked down at his sister, holding his sleeve, in her hood and her sash, standing up straight.

"Yes," he said slowly. "I can see that." He looked back at Mau. "That's new."

"It isn't," said Umaru. "It's just new to you."

And the three of them went out of the intake hall into the morning, Umaru in the middle, holding a sleeve on each side, into a plaza full of people who had very definitely not been waiting, and who cheered anyway.
