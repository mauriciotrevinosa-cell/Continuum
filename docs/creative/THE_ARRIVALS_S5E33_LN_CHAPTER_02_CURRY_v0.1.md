# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 38 — Curry

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 38
**Continuity state:** R60, part 3. Taihei's first day: the room at the end of the west wing; Umaru shows him the fort and the Ministry; he asks Momo for a corner of her kitchen and makes the curry he left on his stove; Momo stays in charge; Umaru has one "floor" moment and gets up on her own; Taihei and Mau talk on the step; Taihei has his own judgement and is not a replacement chef

*When: Day 307, from late morning to night. The card on the room at the end of the west wing says For whoever comes, and under it, in Fern's hand, Came.*

Umaru showed him the room first, because she had stood in it two nights ago and decided it was ready, and she wanted him to see that she had been right.

Taihei stood in the doorway for a long time.

It was a plain room. A bed, made with the good sheets, because the register girl had insisted. A chair. A shelf with nothing on it yet. A window, with the shutter open, looking out over the yard at the ink tree, dark-leaved, and beyond it the roofs of the west wing.

And on the door, a card in a little brass holder. *For whoever comes.* And under it, in Fern's small upright hand, written that morning: *Came.* And under that, in Umaru's square letters, because she had asked Fern if she could and Fern had given her the pencil: *Taihei. (Onii-chan.)*

Taihei looked at the card for a long time.

"Who's Fern?" he said.

"She keeps the ledger," said Umaru. "She counts everyone. She said yes to the coin." She paused. "She's very scary. You'll like her."

Taihei went into the room. He looked at the bed. At the shelf. At the window. He went to the window and stood at it with his hands on the sill and looked out at the yard and the tree and the strange sky.

"There's a door," said Umaru, behind him. Very fast. "That shuts. Wakana fixed the latch. And a window. And there's a kitchen, downstairs, a big one, and Momo runs it, and she said you could—" She stopped. "I made a list. In the winter. Ready and safe. I checked everything. I wanted it to be ready before I—"

Taihei turned round.

He looked at his little sister standing in the doorway of a room in another world, in her sash and her hood, talking very fast about latches and kitchens and lists, the way she had never in her life talked about anything except games and snacks.

"It's ready," he said.

Umaru stopped talking.

"It's a very good room," said Taihei. "You did a very good job." He looked at the card on the door again. "*Onii-chan.*" He almost smiled. "In brackets."

"Fern said it should be your name. So I put your name. And then I put—" Umaru went red. "In case people didn't know."

"I think they know," said Taihei.

* * *

She showed him the fort next.

She did it properly. The President's tour. She walked him through the arch past the toll booth, with the OPEN sign back on the shutter, and explained the toll (one item, any kind, or a story) and waived it for him, officially, in writing. She showed him the cushions, all of them. The Milim Beam. The hamster flag. The RECORDS crate. The constitution, which she took out of the drawer marked LATER, where it had always lived under the coin, and unfolded on the floor, and let him read.

Taihei read the whole thing.

It took a long time. It was long, and written in crayon in several different hands, with amendments and counter-amendments and a section on the rights of honorary citizens that was mostly about Rimuru. He read every word, sitting cross-legged on a cushion in his socks and his apron, with twenty small faces in hoods watching him from every corner of the fort.

When he finished, he folded it very carefully and handed it back.

"You wrote this?" he said.

"Most of it," said Umaru. "The fort wrote some. Coco wrote the part about ink. Yuka wrote the part about appeals." She put it back in the drawer. "I'm the President."

"I read that," said Taihei. "Article one."

"It's the most important article."

"I could tell," said Taihei. "It's in capitals."

From the corner of the fort, Yuka said, very loudly, "The appeals section is better."

Taihei looked at her. Then at his sister. Then back at Yuka.

"Who's that?"

"Yuka," said Umaru. "She's my rival. At cards. And at dice. And at appeals." She paused. "She's my friend."

Yuka went very red and pulled her hood down over her face.

* * *

At noon, he asked Momo.

He did it properly too. He went to the kitchen door, and stood in it, and did not come in. And when Momo looked up from the big pot, he bowed. A small, polite bow, the kind you give to someone whose house you are standing in.

"I'm sorry to ask," said Taihei. "I know it's your kitchen. My sister says it's the most important room in the house." He straightened. "I was making curry. When the coin came. I'd just put the onions in." He looked at the floor. "It's the kind she likes. I've made it every night since she disappeared. In case." He paused. "I'd like to finish it. If there's a corner I could use. I won't get in the way."

Momo looked at him for a long moment.

She looked at his floury hands, which he had not washed yet, because he had not stopped moving since the eighth bell. At the apron. At the face, very tired, very polite, very carefully not asking for more than a corner.

Then she put down her ladle.

"The left end of the long counter," she said. "The small pot on the second shelf. Spices are in the jars above the window, but I'll tell you which ones, because the labels are in four languages and two of them are Seiko's." She folded her arms. "And you'll wash your hands first."

Taihei looked at his hands.

"Yes," he said. "Sorry."

"And you'll make enough for the table," said Momo. "Not just her. Everyone. Forty people." She paused. "If it's the kind she likes, everyone will want to know why."

Taihei looked at her.

"Forty?"

"Forty-three, today," said Momo. "Counting you."

* * *

It took him all afternoon.

Momo did not leave the kitchen. She worked on the other end of the long counter, on the evening soup, and did not look at him very much. But Mau, coming through for water, saw her look twice. Once when Taihei asked which of the jars was closest to the thing he needed, and described it, and she handed him one without a word and he smelled it and said *oh, that's better than mine*. And once when he tasted the curry, very carefully, and stood very still for a moment with the spoon in his hand, and then turned away from the pot and wiped his eyes with his wrist.

Momo did not say anything about that. She went on with her soup.

Okarun came in at four and stopped dead in the doorway.

"What's that smell?"

"Curry," said Momo. "The President's brother is making it."

"The President has a brother?"

"Since this morning," said Momo. "Wash your hands. You can cut onions for him."

* * *

At dinner, the long table ate curry.

Forty-three people. The big pot came out from the kitchen, carried by Okarun and Taihei between them, and went down the middle of the table, and the smell of it went all the way to the end where the witches sat with their good teapot, and Daphne looked up with her mild hungry eyes and did not look down again.

Taihei served it. Momo let him.

He went down the table with the ladle, the way Rem went down it with the teapot, and he did not know anyone's name, and he asked each one. *I'm Taihei. What's your name?* And they told him. And he said it back, carefully, and served them, and went on.

When he got to Umaru, at the fort's end, he did not ask her name. He filled her bowl, and put it down in front of her, and stood there.

Umaru looked at the curry.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she picked up her spoon and tasted it.

Her face crumpled.

"It's the same," she said. "It's exactly the same."

"I know," said Taihei. "I've made it every night."

Umaru put her spoon down. And then, very suddenly, in front of the whole long table, she slid off the bench and lay down on the floor, flat on her back, with her arms out, and said, to the ceiling, in a small voice:

"Onii-chan. I can't move. It's too good. I'm going to lie here forever. Bring me chips."

The long table went quiet.

Taihei looked down at his sister lying on the floor. Mau, across the table, saw his face. It was not surprised. It was not disappointed. It was something very old and very tired and very fond, the face of a man who had seen this exact thing a thousand times in another world.

Mau did not say anything. He waited.

Umaru lay on the floor for a count of about five.

Then she sat up. She looked round at the long table, at forty-two people looking at her. At Yuka, with her mouth open. At the fort, in a row, staring. At Mau.

She got up.

She brushed off her sash. She climbed back onto the bench, very straight, and picked up her spoon, and ate a mouthful of curry, and swallowed.

"That was a test," she said. "Of the emergency floor protocol. It works." She looked at Yuka. "Write that down."

Yuka, after a moment, very slowly, wrote it down on the Ministry slate.

Taihei was still standing beside the bench with the ladle. He looked at his sister, eating curry very straight-backed at a table full of strangers who were not strangers to her. And then he looked across the table at Mau.

Mau looked back.

*I told you,* he did not say. *I'd be standing right here.*

Taihei nodded, very slightly, as if he had heard it anyway. And went on down the table with the ladle.

* * *

Later, when the table had been cleared and the pot washed and Momo had told Taihei, in front of everyone, that the curry was *acceptable*, which made Okarun drop a plate, Taihei found Mau on the back step in the dark.

He sat down beside him. He had taken off the apron at last.

For a while they did not say anything.

"She lay on the floor," said Taihei.

"For five seconds," said Mau.

"For five seconds," Taihei agreed. "At home she'd have lain there for five hours." He looked at the dark yard. "And then she got up. By herself. Nobody told her to." He was quiet. "I used to tell her to. Every night. *Get up, Umaru. Do your homework, Umaru.* She never did."

"She does now."

"She does now," said Taihei. He looked at his hands. "I don't know what to do with that. I've spent years looking after her. Cooking. Cleaning. Worrying." He laughed, very quietly. "And I came here and she showed me a constitution."

Mau did not say anything for a moment.

"You can still cook," he said. "Momo let you. That's very rare."

"Is it?"

"Very," said Mau. "And you can still worry. Everyone here worries. It's the main hobby." He paused. "And she'll still want you. She just wants you differently. Not to look after her. To see her." He looked at the dark ink tree. "She's been waiting a long time for someone from before to see who she is now."

Taihei was quiet for a long time.

"Two older brothers," he said at last. "She said."

"She said."

"One from before and one from after." Taihei looked at him sideways, in the dark. "Which of us does the laundry?"

Mau laughed, so suddenly and so loudly that somebody inside said *shh*.

"Neither," he said. "She does her own. She has a rota."

Taihei stared at him.

"She has a *rota*?"

"It's an amendment," said Mau. "On the back. In Yuka's handwriting."

Taihei put his face in his hands. His shoulders shook. Mau could not tell, for a moment, whether he was laughing or crying. Then he decided it was both, and that was fine, and sat beside him on the step in the dark without saying anything else, the way you sit beside a brother.
