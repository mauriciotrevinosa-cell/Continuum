# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 39 — The First Leaf

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 39
**Continuity state:** R32 return. Ten days after planting, the first seedlings show in the box outside the clinic; nothing can be compared yet (no true leaf); Shoko reacts on her own terms; Anko announces she has decided on her request and still does not say what it is; Taihei meets the clinic and is told not to help

*When: Day 308, early morning. The fireflies are thinning. Taihei has been in the house for a day and a half and has already been told, by four different people, to sit down.*

Mau checked the box every morning. He had told Shoko he would, and Shoko had said he would want it to grow faster, and he did.

Every morning for ten days he came down before breakfast and went round the side of the clinic to the bench where the box sat in the sun, and looked at it. Dark soil. Damp, because he watered it, very carefully, from a cup, the way Daphne had shown him. Nothing else.

On the eighth day, Frieren came with him. She looked at the box for a long time and said, "It's thinking," and went back to bed.

On the ninth day, Anko came, and looked at the box, and looked at him, and wrote something in her notebook, and left.

On the tenth day, there was something.

He almost missed it. He had already turned to go. Then the light came round the corner of the clinic and fell on the soil at an angle, and he saw it.

A thread. Pale green, almost white, no thicker than a hair, standing up out of the soil in a tiny bent hook, as if it had pushed its way up with its head down and had not yet decided to look up.

Mau stood very still.

Then he saw another one. A little way off. And a third.

He did not touch them. He did not breathe on them. He stood beside the bench in the early morning light with the empty cup in his hand and looked at three pale threads in a box of dirt, and something in his chest went very quiet.

He went to get Shoko.

* * *

Shoko was in the clinic, in the half-light, sorting the day's list. She looked up when he came in. She looked at his face.

"What," she said.

"Come and see."

She put the list down.

She came out round the side of the clinic and stood beside the bench and looked at the box. She did not say anything for a long time. Then she bent down, very close, her face a hand's breadth from the soil, and looked at the three pale threads with the same attention she gave a wound or a pulse or a boy who had fallen out of the sky.

"Three," she said.

"Three," said Mau. "So far."

Shoko straightened up. Her hand had gone to the cigarette behind her ear. Not taking it out. Just touching it, the way you touch something to be sure it is still there.

"Can Ciel tell?" she said.

*Not yet,* said Ciel. *These are seed leaves. They are the same in a great many plants. The first true leaf will come after. That is the one I could compare.* A pause. *A week, perhaps. If it continues.*

Mau said it aloud.

Shoko looked at the threads for a while longer.

"So it might be nothing," she said. "Some weed from a valley past Fallow."

"It might."

"Or it might be the thing."

"It might."

Shoko nodded slowly.

"I don't mind," she said.

Mau looked at her.

"Whichever," said Shoko. She did not look away from the box. "If it's a weed, it's a weed someone traded a lamp chimney for. That an old man grew on the edge of his field for his pipe. That Hollis kept dry for two weeks in his coat." She paused. "That you watered every morning with a cup."

She was quiet for a moment.

"I've been given a lot of things in my life," she said. "Most of them were people to fix." She looked at the three pale threads. "Nobody ever gave me something that was just going to grow."

Mau did not say anything. He could not think of anything that would not be too much.

So he stood beside her at the bench in the early light, and they looked at the box together, and after a while Shoko took her hand away from the cigarette and put it in her pocket.

* * *

Anko arrived at breakfast time, as if she had been waiting round the corner. Mau suspected she had.

She looked at the box. She bent down and looked at it very closely, the way Shoko had. She counted, with her finger hovering above the soil, not touching. There were five threads now.

"Five," said Anko.

"Five," said Mau.

Anko straightened up. She opened her notebook. She turned to a page near the back, which had a heading Mau could not read upside down, and three short lines under it, numbered. She looked at them for a long time.

Then she took her pen and drew a firm circle round one of them.

"I've decided," she said.

Mau waited.

Anko closed the notebook.

"I'm not telling you," she said.

"You said when it sprouts."

"I said when it sprouts, I'd *decide*," said Anko. "I've decided. I didn't say I'd *tell* you." She put the notebook in her inside pocket, where she kept it in the river towns when she was wearing brown. "I'll tell you when there's a leaf. A real one. One Ciel can look at."

"That's a week."

"Then you'll be nervous for a week," said Anko. "Again."

She looked at Shoko.

"It's a good garden," she said. Grudgingly. "For a box."

Shoko almost smiled.

"Thank you," she said.

"I'm still here first," said Anko, and went in to breakfast.

* * *

Taihei found them there a little later.

He had come out of the main house with two cups of tea, because he had been told by Momo that he could not cook breakfast, he was a guest for at least three days, and by Rem that he could not pour, and by Fern that he could not touch the ledger, and by Umaru that he could not do her laundry because she had a rota. He had been very politely forbidden from doing anything at all. So he had made tea, because nobody had thought to forbid tea, and come looking for someone to give it to.

He held out the cups.

"I'm not allowed to do anything," he said. "I'm told it's a rule. So I made tea. Is that allowed?"

"It's allowed," said Shoko, and took one.

Taihei looked at the box on the bench.

"Is that a garden?"

"It's a box," said Mau. "With seeds. For Shoko." He paused. "To say thank you. It might not be the right plant."

Taihei bent down and looked at the five pale threads.

"Five," he said.

"Five."

Taihei looked at them for a long moment. Then he straightened up and looked at Shoko, the doctor, with her cup of tea, standing beside a box of seedlings with an expression he did not know her well enough yet to read.

"I can help," he said. "With the watering. If—"

"No," said Shoko and Mau together.

Taihei blinked.

"It's his," said Shoko, nodding at Mau. "He waters it. Every morning. With a cup." She drank her tea. "That's the point."

Taihei looked from one of them to the other.

"Ah," he said slowly. "I see."

He did not offer again. He stood beside them at the bench, with his own cup of tea that he had made for nobody in particular, and looked at the box.

"In my world," he said after a while, "I used to grow tomatoes. On the balcony. In a pot." He smiled a little. "Umaru ate them all before they were ripe. Every summer."

"Did they grow?" said Mau.

"Every summer," said Taihei. "Even though she ate them." He looked at the five pale threads. "Things grow anyway. If someone waters them."

Shoko looked at him.

Then she looked at Mau, and something at the corner of her mouth moved.

"I like your brother," she said.

"He's Umaru's brother," said Mau.

"He's both of yours," said Shoko. "I heard. It's all over the house."

Taihei went very red and drank his tea.
