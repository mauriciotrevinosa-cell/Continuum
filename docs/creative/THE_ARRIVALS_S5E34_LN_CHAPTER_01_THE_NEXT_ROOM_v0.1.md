# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 41 — The Next Room

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 41
**Continuity state:** Rimuru's rule (one empty room, always): Taihei took the last one, so Wakana chalks the next at the end of the west wing; Taihei asks to help, by choice, and is allowed; Marin is sewing something for herself "in case there's a festival" (a seed; nothing is decided); Wakana and Marin's own project and pleasure, not labour owed to the house

*When: Day 310, afternoon. Cooler, with cloud. Taihei's third day, which Momo has declared the last day he is a guest.*

Wakana chalked the next room on the third day, because Rimuru's rule said there had to be one, and there wasn't.

He did it the way he always did. He went to the end of the west wing, past the door that now said *Came* and *Taihei (Onii-chan)* on its card, and knelt down on the boards of the corridor where the house stopped, and took a stick of chalk from his pocket, and began to draw a rectangle on the floor.

He did not measure. He never measured the first time. He drew where his hand thought the walls should go, and then he stood up and looked at it, and then he knelt down and rubbed one side out with his sleeve and drew it again, a little further over.

Taihei watched him from his doorway.

He had been watching for some time. He had opened his door to go downstairs and found a young man on his knees in the corridor drawing on the floor, and he had stopped, and stood there with his hand on the latch, and not gone anywhere.

"What are you making?" he said at last.

Wakana looked up. He went slightly red, the way he always did when someone watched him work.

"The next room," he said. "The empty one."

"For who?"

"For whoever comes," said Wakana. He looked down at the chalk lines. "Rimuru's rule. There's always one empty room. In case. Yours was the empty one, until you came." He paused. "So now there needs to be another."

Taihei looked at the rectangle on the floor for a long time.

"Every time someone comes," he said slowly, "you build a room for the next one."

"Yes."

"Before you know who it is."

"That's the point," said Wakana. "If you wait until you know, it's not ready."

Taihei did not say anything for a moment.

"Can I help?" he said.

* * *

Wakana looked at him in alarm.

"You're a guest," he said. "Momo said. Three days. You're not supposed to do anything."

"It's the third day," said Taihei. "Momo said the third day is the last day I'm a guest." He came out of his doorway. "And nobody said I couldn't help *you*. They only said kitchen, and laundry, and the ledger, and pouring." He looked at the chalk. "Nobody mentioned floors."

Wakana looked at him for a long moment, considering this argument with the seriousness he gave a joint that might not hold.

"It's very boring," he said. "Most of it. Measuring. Marking. Carrying." He paused. "Rubbing things out."

"I'm good at boring," said Taihei. "I worked in an office for nine years."

Wakana knew what an office was. His grandfather had complained about them for years. But he heard something else in the way Taihei said it, and he handed him the chalk.

"The far wall," he said. "I always get the far wall wrong. Draw where you think it goes."

Taihei took the chalk. He knelt down on the boards. He looked at the rectangle, at the near wall and the side walls, at the place where Wakana's far wall was not quite square.

He drew a line.

Wakana looked at it. Then he took a short rule out of his pocket and laid it along Taihei's line, and along his own side wall, and checked the corner.

It was square.

"Oh," said Wakana.

"I used to do the plans," said Taihei. "For the shelves. At home. Umaru wanted a shelf for every console she owned." He sat back on his heels. "She owned a lot of consoles. I got very good at square."

* * *

Marin found them at three, both on their knees in the corridor, arguing quietly about where the window should go.

She had come up from the workshop with a length of cloth over her arm and pins in her mouth, looking for Wakana to ask him something, and she stopped in the corridor and took the pins out of her mouth and stared.

"You've got a helper," she said.

"He's very good at square," said Wakana.

"*He's* very good at square?" Marin looked at Taihei. "Nobody's good at square. I've been trying to get Wakana to draw square for a year."

"I do get it square," said Wakana. "Eventually."

"*Eventually*," said Marin, "is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

Taihei looked from one of them to the other. There was something in the way they argued that he recognised, though it took him a moment to place it. Then he did. It was the way his sister argued with the girl called Yuka at cards. As if the argument were a thing they had built together and were both very fond of.

He went back to the window.

Marin sat down on the floor of the corridor with her length of cloth across her knees. It was a deep, soft blue, with a pattern woven into it so faint you could only see it when the light caught it. She began to pin something along one edge.

"What's that?" said Wakana, without looking up from the chalk.

"Nothing."

"It's not nothing. You've had pins in your mouth for three days."

Marin did not look up either.

"It's a dress," she said. "For me. Just in case."

"In case of what?"

Marin was quiet for a moment, pinning.

"Hakari said something," she said. "At breakfast. About the festival. The one at the inn, at the beginning. She asked if anyone thought there'd be one this summer." She put a pin in. "Nobody's said there will be. Nobody's said there won't." She put in another pin. "So I'm making a dress. In case. Because if there is one and I haven't made anything, I'll be very cross with myself."

Wakana looked up at last.

He looked at the blue cloth across her knees. At her hands, pinning very carefully. At her face, which was doing the thing it did when she wanted something very much and was pretending she did not.

"It's a good blue," he said.

"I know it's a good blue. I traded Hollis two pots of Kusuri's cream for it."

"You traded cream for cloth?"

"For *this* cloth," said Marin. "Look at it." She held up one edge so the light caught the pattern. "Look."

Wakana looked.

"It's a very good blue," he said, more quietly.

Marin went back to pinning, and did not say anything else, but she was smiling at the cloth.

* * *

They finished the chalk by four.

A room. A rectangle on the boards at the end of the west wing, square at every corner, with a gap for the door and a mark on the far wall where the window would go. Taihei had wanted the window to look at the ink tree, like his. Wakana had said it couldn't, from that wall, the angle was wrong. It would look at the hills instead. Taihei had thought about it and said the hills were fine. Somebody might like hills.

Wakana took a small card from his pocket and a stub of pencil, and wrote on it, carefully, in his slightly uneven letters. Then he propped it against the chalk line where the door would be.

*For whoever comes.*

Taihei looked at the card for a long time.

"That's what my door said," he said. "Before."

"Yes."

"Before anyone knew it would be me."

"Yes," said Wakana. He stood up and brushed the chalk off his knees. "Somebody wrote it on yours too. Before. And then you came."

Taihei did not say anything. He looked at the chalk rectangle on the floor, the room that did not exist yet, for someone who did not exist yet, or who did but did not know it.

"Who wrote it on mine?" he said.

Wakana thought about it.

"I chalked it," he said. "Fern wrote the card. Rimuru made the rule." He paused. "And Umaru stood in it two nights ago and decided it was ready."

Marin, still sitting on the floor with her blue cloth, said, without looking up, "That's how it works here. Everyone makes a bit. Nobody makes all of it."

Taihei looked at her. Then at Wakana. Then at the chalk on the floor.

"Can I help with the walls?" he said. "When you build them?"

"It'll take weeks," said Wakana.

"I'm not going anywhere," said Taihei.

Wakana looked at him for a long moment, considering, the way he considered a joint.

"The far wall," he said. "You can have the far wall."

* * *

That evening, at dinner, Momo put a bowl in front of Taihei and said, "You're not a guest any more. Tomorrow you can have the left end of the counter. Mornings only. And you'll wash your hands."

Taihei looked at her.

"Yes," he said. "Thank you."

Across the table, Umaru was watching him. She had been watching him all through dinner, with an expression Mau could not read.

"What?" said Taihei.

"You've got chalk on your knees," said Umaru.

Taihei looked down. He had. White, both knees, all over his borrowed trousers.

"I was helping Wakana," he said. "With a room."

Umaru stared at him.

"You were *helping*," she said. "On your *third day*. With a *room*."

"Nobody said I couldn't help with floors."

Umaru put down her spoon.

"Onii-chan," she said, very seriously. "That's a loophole."

"It is a loophole," Taihei agreed.

Umaru looked at him for a long moment. Then she picked up her spoon again.

"Good," she said. "I'll put it in the constitution."
