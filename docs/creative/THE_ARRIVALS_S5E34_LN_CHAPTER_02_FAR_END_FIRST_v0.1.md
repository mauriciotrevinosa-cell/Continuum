# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 42 — Far End First

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 42
**Continuity state:** Sixth tower visit, from the floor 5 transfer point: Mau (owner) with Frieren, Yuka and Fern; Frieren keeps her promise to teach Yuka the "far end first" trick; floor 7 is a long dark arcade of shopfronts with a lamp objective; Yuka plans the approach herself with Frieren's guidance; level and points rise in Mau's branch only; no extraction

*When: Day 312, morning. Two and a half hours outside. Yuka has been reminding Frieren of her promise every morning at breakfast since Day 302.*

Yuka had been reminding Frieren every morning for ten days.

Not loudly. Yuka did not do things loudly when they mattered. She just sat down across from Frieren at breakfast, every morning, and looked at her, and said, "Next time I come," and then ate her porridge.

On the tenth morning, Frieren put down her spoon.

"Tomorrow," she said.

Yuka stopped eating.

"Tomorrow?"

"Mau's going in tomorrow. From five. There's room for four." Frieren looked at Mau, who was carefully eating his porridge and not looking at either of them. "He's already put your name on Anko's board."

Yuka looked at Mau.

"You did?"

"Three days ago," said Mau. "She asked me to."

Yuka looked back at Frieren. Her face did something very fast and complicated.

"You remembered," she said.

"You reminded me every morning," said Frieren. "For ten days." She picked up her spoon again. "It would have been very difficult to forget."

* * *

Fern came too. Frieren had asked her, at the tea they had now on the days when neither of them needed anything recorded, and Fern had said yes before Frieren finished asking.

"Somebody has to keep you away from the chests," said Fern.

"There are no chests on seven," said Frieren. "We haven't seen seven."

"Then somebody has to keep you away from the chests we haven't seen."

Anko turned the glass. Mau took out the key. Under START FROM on the board, Anko had written *5*, and under it, in the names column, *Mau, Frieren, Fern, Yuka.* And in the margin, small, because Yuka had asked her to: *(lesson)*.

They came out on the terrace. Yuka looked at the tree with its one bare place and did not say anything. Frieren looked at the tree too, for a long time, with her head a little on one side.

"It's very old," she said. "Older than the tower, maybe." She did not explain how she knew. "It grew here before the rest."

They walked down the spiral to six, which was empty now, with the shutter standing open at the bottom. Fern stopped at the iron wheel and looked up the shaft for a long time.

"Gojo did this?"

"With a marble," said Mau.

"Hm," said Fern. It was not quite Agott's *hm*. But it was close.

They went up the ramp beyond the shutter, toward the light. At the top, a pale seven on the wall.

* * *

The seventh floor was a street indoors.

A long, wide corridor under a high roof of grimy glass, with shopfronts on both sides. Dozens of them. Their windows were broken or dark, their signs faded, their shutters half down. Some had goods still in them, dusty and strange: shoes in rows, shapes that might have been clothes, glass cases full of things that caught the light. The floor was tiled, cracked, with weeds coming up between the cracks. At intervals down the middle of the corridor, tall lamps on poles. All dark.

And at the far end, a long way off, a single lamp, lit. Blue.

Yuka stopped.

She looked at the long corridor. At the dark lamps, one after another, all the way down. At the blue one at the far end. At the shopfronts, dozens of them, dark and quiet, with their shutters half down.

She looked at Frieren.

"It's the same," she said.

"It's a little different," said Frieren.

"It's the *lamps*. Like the platform. Dark ones, and one lit at the end." Yuka's voice had gone very quiet and very fast. "That's the trick. You light the far one first. And walk back, lighting, so the thing has to come into the light."

Frieren did not say anything.

The notice came up.

*Floor 7 of 100.*

*Objective: light the arcade.*

Yuka read it over Mau's shoulder. Then she looked at Frieren again.

"Isn't it?"

"You tell me," said Frieren.

Yuka stared at her.

"That's the lesson," said Frieren. "Not the trick. Anyone can be told the trick." She looked down the long corridor. "The lesson is looking at a place and working out whether the trick fits it. Before you use it." She folded her hands on her staff. "So. Does it fit?"

* * *

Yuka looked for a long time.

She did not rush. Mau watched her not rush. She walked a few paces into the arcade and stopped and looked at the shopfronts on the left. Then the right. Then up at the grimy glass roof. Then down at the cracked tiles. She crouched and put her hand flat on the floor, the way she did before she called the white dog, and was very still.

"There's no water," she said at last.

"No," said Frieren.

"On the platform, the thing came out of the water. The channel. It was in one place, down the tunnel, and you knew where it would come from." Yuka stood up. "Here there's no water. There's—" She looked at the shopfronts. Dozens of them. Dark, with their shutters half down. "There's lots of places. It could come from any of them."

Fern had gone very still beside Mau.

"So if we walk to the far end first," said Yuka slowly, "past all the dark shops, in the dark, and something's in one of them—" She stopped. "It doesn't have to come into the light. It's already beside us. In the dark. The whole way."

Frieren did not say anything. But something at the corner of her mouth had moved.

"So the trick doesn't fit," said Yuka. She sounded almost upset about it. "Not all of it."

"Not all of it," Frieren agreed. "What part fits?"

Yuka looked at the lamps.

"The light," she said. "Things here don't like the light. The platform thing didn't. So we still want light." She looked at the nearest dark lamp, a few paces in. "We just—start here. Near the stair. Where we came in. Light this one. Then the next. And walk forward into the light we've already made. So we never have dark on both sides. Only in front."

She stopped. She looked at Frieren.

"Is that wrong?"

Frieren looked at her for a long moment. At the girl with the rope over her shoulder and grass seeds still in the seams of her coat from the terrace, who had looked at a corridor full of dark shops and seen that a trick that worked on a platform would get them killed here.

"No," said Frieren. "That's right."

Yuka's face did something she could not stop.

"Light the first one," said Frieren. "Your plan. I'll follow it."

* * *

It worked.

Not easily. The things that lived in the dark shops were small and fast and many, and they came out from behind the half-down shutters in twos and threes whenever the light reached the edge of a new shop. Pale, thin, like wet paper folded into the shape of a hand. They hated the light. They came anyway.

But they came only from in front. Never from behind, where the lamps were already lit. Never from both sides at once.

Yuka called each lamp. Mau put the crystal in. Fern and Frieren took the paper-hand things as they came out into the light, one shot each, clean. Yuka called the white dog twice, short, to check the shopfronts ahead before they reached them, and sent it back to her shadow both times before she was tired, and drank water after, without being told.

Halfway down the arcade, Fern looked at Frieren.

"She's good," said Fern quietly.

"She's very good," said Frieren.

"You taught her that? The looking?"

Frieren was quiet for a moment, watching Yuka crouch at the edge of the light, her hand flat on the tiles, listening.

"No," she said. "Somebody asked me that question once. A long time ago. Whether a trick fit the place I wanted to use it." She watched Yuka stand up and point at the next lamp. "I didn't have an answer. I've been waiting a very long time to ask it to someone else."

Fern did not say anything. But she moved a little closer to Frieren as they walked.

* * *

The last lamp was the blue one, at the far end.

When Mau put the crystal in the lamp beside it, the whole arcade was lit. Soft blue light under the grimy glass roof, all the way back to the stair, falling on the dusty shopfronts and the weeds in the cracks and the strange goods in the broken windows.

The notice came up.

*Arcade lit. Route to Floor 8 opened.*

Level nine. Forty points. Mau showed them. Yuka looked at the number and then away from it, as if it did not interest her, which was how Mau knew she was too pleased to look at anything for long.

"Your plan," said Mau.

"Frieren's trick," said Yuka.

"Your plan," said Frieren. "My trick would have got us killed. You saw that. I didn't tell you." She looked down the long blue arcade. "That's the lesson. Not the trick."

Yuka did not say anything for a long moment.

Then she took her small notebook out of her coat, the one she had started carrying since the Red Gate, and opened it to a fresh page, and wrote at the top, in her careful letters: *7.* And under it: *Not far end first. Near end first. No dark on both sides.*

And under that, smaller: *Does it fit?*

She showed it to Frieren.

Frieren read it. Then she took the pencil out of Yuka's fingers and, under *Does it fit?*, wrote one more word.

*Always.*

* * *

They came home through the small frame on five at an hour and fifty minutes. Anko wrote it down.

Maki was waiting by the screen. She had not said she would be. She was simply there, with her arms folded, the way she was always simply there.

Yuka went straight to her and held out the notebook.

Maki read it. Both lines, and Frieren's word underneath.

She looked at her granddaughter for a long moment.

"You changed the plan," she said.

"The plan didn't fit," said Yuka. "So I changed it."

Maki closed the notebook and gave it back.

"Good," she said.

That was all. But Yuka walked back to the house beside her grandmother with her chin up and the notebook held against her chest, and Mau, watching them go, thought that *good* from Maki was worth about a hundred of anyone else's.
