# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 44 — A Petition for Lights

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 44
**Continuity state:** The five, Marin and Kessoku bring a petition for a summer festival to the long table, written up by the Ministry; the house agrees in principle to a festival before the summer ends, echoing the S2E22 festival at the inn "with more people"; the date and shape are left to be decided (creator); no outside-facing decision, so the two faces are not needed

*When: Day 315, evening, after dinner. The table has not been cleared. Fern has the ledger open because Jinshi left a question in it.*

The petition was written on the back of the Ministry slate, because Umaru said a petition of this importance could not go on ordinary paper.

It was very long. It had been drafted over three days in the five's room, around the summer kotatsu, with Kusuri lying flat on the floor contributing clauses from under a damp cloth, and then carried to the fort for review, and then to the music room, where Seika had read it and crossed out four lines and written *no* beside a fifth, and then back to the fort, where Umaru had copied the final version onto the slate in her best square capitals. It was so long that it went round the edge of the slate and up the side.

Hakari carried it to the long table after dinner.

She did not carry it alone. Karane was on one side of her, very red, with her arms folded. Kusuri was on the other, with a list of herbs. Shizuka and Nano came behind, Shizuka holding her book, Nano holding nothing at all but looking as if she were prepared to hold anything that needed holding. Behind them, Marin, with a length of deep blue cloth over her arm that she had not meant to bring and had forgotten she was carrying. Behind her, Kessoku: Bocchi with her guitar against her chest, Kita with one hand on Bocchi's back, Nijika with her sticks, Ryo looking bored in a way that fooled nobody. And Seika, last, with her arms folded, looking at the whole procession as if she were reviewing a band that had not yet earned the stage.

And at the very back, Umaru, in her sash and her hood, with Yuka beside her, and Taihei, who had no idea what was happening and had been told only that he should come, and stand at the back, and look supportive.

They stopped at the head of the long table, in front of Fern.

Fern looked up from the ledger.

She looked at Hakari, holding the slate. At the procession behind her, all the way back to Taihei. She put her pen down.

"Yes?" said Fern.

Hakari held out the slate.

"It's a petition," she said. "From the five. And Marin. And Kessoku. And the Ministry, which wrote it down." She took a breath. "We'd like a festival."

* * *

The long table went quiet from the head outward.

Not silent. A house this full could not be silent. But the conversations stopped, one after another, down the bench, until the only sound was Milim, upside down at the far end, saying "*what?*" very loudly, and Ori telling her to hush.

Fern took the slate.

She read it. All of it. It took a long time, because it was very long, and because it went round the edge and up the side and she had to turn the slate twice. Nobody interrupted her. The procession stood at the head of the table and waited, the way you wait for Fern.

When she had finished, she put the slate down on the ledger.

"*Whereas*," she read aloud, "*the house had a festival at the inn, at the beginning, when the outside world was not ready to celebrate with us, so we celebrated one another.*" She looked up. "*And whereas there are now forty-three people in the house, which is more than there were then.*" She looked at the slate again. "*And whereas it is summer, and summer will end.*"

She was quiet for a moment.

"*Therefore,*" she read, "*the undersigned request that the house hold a festival. Before the summer ends. With lights, and music, and food, and one stall of herbs, under supervision.*" She looked at Kusuri. "Under supervision."

"That was Seika's amendment," said Kusuri, with some bitterness.

"It's a good amendment," said Seika, from the back.

* * *

Fern looked down the long table.

At all of them. Forty-three faces, turned toward the head of the table, waiting. Rimuru at the cold-hearth end, in human shape for once, with his chin on his hand. Mau halfway down the bench, beside Frieren. Momo at the kitchen door with a cloth over her shoulder. Gojo and Shoko, side by side, not quite touching. Coco's mother, who had never been to a festival in this world, looking at the procession with a small surprised smile. Byakuya, who had once lived in a village with festivals every season, sitting up very straight.

"This isn't mine to decide," said Fern.

The procession looked at her in alarm.

"It's not money," said Fern. "Well. It's some money. Lamp oil. Food. Hollis will want paying for whatever we ask him to bring." She glanced at the ledger. "That part's mine. And I can find it." She looked up again. "But a festival isn't the ledger. It's the house. So the house says."

She looked down the table.

"Rimuru?"

Rimuru took his chin off his hand.

"Is it outside?" he said. "Does the outside world see it? The towns?"

"No," said Hakari quickly. "Inside. Inside the lime. Like the one at the inn. Just us."

"Then it doesn't need two faces," said Rimuru. "It's not a deal with anyone. It's a party." He looked down the table at Mau. "So it's just the house."

Fern nodded.

"Then the house says," she said. And she stood up, which Fern never did at the long table, and looked down the length of it at all of them. "Who wants a festival?"

* * *

Milim's hand went up first. Both hands. And her feet, because she was still upside down.

Then Ori's. Then the fort's, all of it, twenty small hands in hoods going up at once like a field of something growing. Then Yuka's. Then Umaru's, from the back of the procession, very straight, the President voting on her own petition, which Seika said afterward was a conflict of interest and Umaru said was democracy.

Then Momo's, from the kitchen door, still holding the cloth.

Then Gojo's, lazily, without looking up. And Shoko's, beside it, a moment later.

Then Coco's mother, who put her hand up very hesitantly, as if she were not sure she was allowed, and Coco beside her, who put her own hand up and then reached over and pushed her mother's a little higher.

Then Byakuya, enormous, both hands. Senku beside him, one hand, with an expression that said he was voting under protest and would be calculating the lamp oil.

Then hands all down the table, faster and faster, until it was hard to see who had not put a hand up, and the answer was almost nobody.

Mau put his hand up. He looked beside him.

Frieren had not put her hand up. She was looking at the procession at the head of the table. At Bocchi, holding her guitar. At Marin, with her blue cloth. At Hakari, holding out the slate.

"Frieren?" said Mau.

Frieren looked at him.

"At the inn," she said quietly. "At the festival. You said yes to everything. Every time someone called your name. You said yes three times to Momo, and you never said yes to Momo." She paused. "And I asked you why. And you said *festival*."

Mau looked at her.

"I remember," he said.

"I asked if we could do it again," said Frieren. "The next year." She looked at the procession. "We didn't. There was Sukuna. And everything after." She was quiet for a moment. "This is the next year. A long way late."

Then she put her hand up.

* * *

Fern did not count. There was no need.

She sat back down. She picked up her pen. She turned the ledger to a clean page, and wrote at the top, in her small upright hand:

*Day 315.*

And under it:

*Festival. Before the summer ends. Inside the lime. Lights, music, food, one stall of herbs (supervised).*

She looked at it. Then, after a moment, she added:

*Date: to be decided. By the house.*

And under that, smaller, because she could not help it:

*Lamp oil: a great deal.*

She closed the ledger.

At the head of the table, the procession had not moved. They were standing there in a row, from Hakari to Taihei, looking at Fern, waiting to be told.

"Yes," said Fern.

Hakari burst into tears.

Karane, beside her, said "*Oh, for*—" and put an arm round her, very stiffly, and did not take it away. Kusuri threw her list of herbs in the air. Shizuka held up her book, already open to a page she must have written in advance, which said, in large careful letters: *THANK YOU.* And Nano, beside her, very quietly, took the pencil out of Shizuka's hand and added, underneath, in her own precise small writing: *(N.)*

Bocchi, in the middle of Kessoku, had gone completely white.

"We'll have to play," she said, very small. "At the festival. In front of everyone. Forty-three people."

"Forty-three," said Kita, beside her, with her hand on Bocchi's back. "You played for everyone at the inn."

"That was the music corner. That was—" Bocchi stopped. "That was before."

"It's still us," said Kita. "And I'll still stand close enough." She leaned her head against Bocchi's for a moment. "You won't notice them."

Bocchi looked at her. Then at the long table, at forty-three faces, all turned toward the head of the table where she was standing.

She held the guitar a little tighter.

"Okay," she said.

* * *

At the back of the procession, Taihei leaned down to his sister.

"What just happened?" he said.

"We're having a festival," said Umaru. She was very straight, very serious, very pleased. "I wrote the petition. Well. I wrote it down. The five wrote it. Seika edited it." She paused. "I voted for it."

"Is that allowed?"

"It's democracy," said Umaru firmly. "Seika says it's a conflict of interest. I say it's democracy." She looked up at him. "There'll be lights. And music. And food. You can cook. If Momo says." She paused. "And I'll show you everything."

Taihei looked down at his little sister, in her sash and her hood, in the middle of a crowd of people who were all talking at once, about lights and stalls and music and lamp oil.

"You'll show me everything," he said.

"Everything," said Umaru. "The whole festival. The fort's going to have a stand." She frowned. "Probably. I'll have to petition."

"Another petition."

"I'm very good at petitions," said Umaru. "It's in the constitution."

Taihei laughed, quietly, and put his hand on top of her hood.

Down the long table, under all the noise, Frieren had not taken her hand down. It was still raised, a little, resting on the table now, palm up, between her and Mau.

Mau looked at it.

Then he put his hand in hers.

"Next year," said Frieren. Very quietly. "A long way late."

"A long way late," said Mau. "But it came."
