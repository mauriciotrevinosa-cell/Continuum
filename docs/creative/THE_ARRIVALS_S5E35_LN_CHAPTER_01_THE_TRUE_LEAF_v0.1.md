# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 45 — The True Leaf

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 45
**Continuity state:** R32 return. The first true leaves open in Shoko's box; Ciel compares them with the reference she observed in Shoko's cigarette: the same family, close but not identical, so a real starting point and not a finished supply; Anko reveals her request at last (a box of her own, with nothing in it, that Mau must water); Shoko's cigarette stays unlit

*When: Day 316, morning. Eight days since the first threads. There are eleven seedlings in the box now; Mau has stopped counting out loud because Anko kept writing the numbers down.*

The first true leaf opened on the eighteenth day after planting, and Mau almost cried, which he would deny later.

It was not much. A seedling, one of the eleven, a little taller than the others, had put out a leaf that was not like the seed leaves. Those had been round and plain and pale, the same, Ciel said, as a great many plants. This one was different. Longer. Pointed at the tip. A deeper green, with fine veins running out from the middle like the lines on the back of a hand. And when Mau bent close, very close, he thought there was a smell. Faint. Green and a little bitter, and under it something warm, like hay in the sun.

He stood beside the bench for a long time.

Then he went to get Shoko. And Anko. In that order, because Shoko first was the point, and Anko second was the law.

* * *

Shoko did not say anything when she saw it.

She bent down, very close, the way she had bent to the first threads, and looked at the leaf. She did not touch it. She breathed in, once, slowly, near it, and Mau saw her face change. Very slightly. The face of someone who has smelled something from a long way off and a long time ago.

She straightened up.

"Ask her," said Shoko.

*I can compare now,* said Ciel. *If she permits. The reference I observed before, without breaking it. And this leaf, without taking it.*

Mau said it aloud.

Shoko took the cigarette from behind her ear and laid it on the bench beside the box. Very carefully. The way she had laid it on the desk before.

"Go on," she said.

Ciel was quiet for a long time. Longer than Mau had expected. He could feel her working, the way he felt her when she read something carefully: turning it over, holding it up against the other thing, looking at the places where they matched and the places where they did not.

*The same family,* said Ciel at last. *The structure of the leaf. The veins. The compounds I can observe without breaking anything. Close. Very close.* A pause. *Not identical. This is a cousin, not a twin. Grown in different soil, for a long time, by people who wanted something slightly different from it.* Another pause. *If it were dried and cured the way hers was, it would not taste the same. But it would not be a stranger either.*

Mau said it aloud. All of it. Slowly.

Shoko did not say anything for a long moment.

She picked up the cigarette from the bench. She held it between two fingers, the way she always held it, and looked at it. Then at the leaf.

"A cousin," she said.

"Not the same," said Mau. "I'm sorry. I wanted it to be the same."

"I know you did." Shoko put the cigarette back behind her ear. "I'm glad it isn't."

Mau looked at her.

"If it were the same," said Shoko, "I'd smoke this one." She touched the cigarette. "I'd think, there's more, so this one doesn't have to be the last. And I'd smoke it. And then it'd be gone, and the new ones would be just new ones." She looked at the leaf. "This way it stays the last one from home. And these—" she nodded at the box "—these are the first ones from here."

She was quiet for a moment.

"That's better," she said. "I think that's better."

Mau did not say anything. He could not.

So he stood beside her at the bench, and they looked at the box together, at eleven seedlings and one true leaf, pointed and green and smelling faintly of hay in the sun.

* * *

Anko came round the corner of the clinic at exactly the moment Mau had expected, which meant she had been waiting.

She looked at the box. She looked at the leaf. She bent down very close and looked at it for a long time, and breathed in, and straightened up with an expression Mau could not read.

"That's a leaf," she said.

"That's a leaf," said Mau.

"A real one. Not a seed one."

"Ciel says a cousin. Close. Not the same."

Anko nodded slowly. She opened her notebook. She turned to the page near the back, the one with the three short lines on it, one of them circled. She looked at it for a long moment.

"You said you'd tell me," said Mau. "When there was a leaf."

"I did say that," said Anko.

She closed the notebook.

"A box," she said.

Mau looked at her.

"A box," said Anko again. "Like that one." She pointed at the box on the bench, the one Wakana had made out of offcuts on Day 266, with holes in the bottom and a tray underneath. "Mine. Of my own." She paused. "With nothing in it."

Shoko had turned round and was looking at her.

"Nothing?" said Mau.

"Nothing yet," said Anko. "Soil. Empty. On the bench outside my door." She folded her arms. "And you have to water it. Every morning. With a cup. Like that one." She nodded at the box. "Until I decide what goes in it."

Mau stared at her.

"That's it?" he said. "That's what you want? For your years of service?"

Anko looked at him for a long moment.

"I've watched you water that box for eighteen days," she said. "Every morning. Before breakfast. With a cup. Before anyone else was up." Her voice did not change. It was her investigator's voice, flat and careful. "I watch everything. It's my job. I've watched you do a great many things in this house. Fight. Fall. Come back. Stand in the light all night so the thing in the road would look at you." She paused. "I've never watched you do anything as slowly as that box."

Mau did not say anything.

"I want a box," said Anko. "That you water slowly. Every morning. For me." She looked away from him, at the clinic wall, as if the wall had said something interesting. "It doesn't matter what grows in it. I'll decide later. Maybe nothing. Maybe a weed." She paused. "I just want to come out of my door in the morning and see that somebody watered it."

The bench was very quiet.

Shoko had put her hand over her mouth.

Mau looked at Anko. At the woman in the brown coat she still wore sometimes, even in summer, with her notebook in her inside pocket and her face very carefully not doing anything, standing beside a box of seedlings asking for an empty box of her own.

"Every morning," he said.

"Before breakfast," said Anko.

"With a cup."

"With a cup." Anko nodded once. She opened her notebook again and wrote something, very firmly, and closed it. "I'll tell Wakana. He has offcuts."

She turned to go.

Then she stopped, and turned back, and looked at the box on the bench, with its eleven seedlings and its one true leaf.

"It's a good garden," she said. "For a box." She looked at Shoko. "Still here first."

"Still here first," Shoko agreed. Her voice was not quite steady.

Anko went.

* * *

Mau stood by the bench for a long time after she had gone.

"Did you know?" he said at last. "That she'd ask for that?"

"No," said Shoko. "I thought she'd ask for something enormous. To make you suffer." She took her hand away from her mouth. "I think she did ask for something enormous."

Mau looked at her.

"She asked for the thing you do for me," said Shoko. "Not the plant. The watering." She looked at the box. "Somebody who comes out every morning and checks that you're still growing." She was quiet. "Nobody does that for Anko. She does it for everyone. She's watched every one of us for years. Nobody ever watched her back."

Mau did not say anything for a long moment.

"Every morning," he said again. Quietly. To himself, this time.

"You'll have two boxes," said Shoko.

"I'll have two boxes."

"You'll want them both to grow faster."

"Definitely."

Shoko almost laughed.

"Don't you dare do anything to either of them," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it."

Mau did not deny it.

That evening, Wakana brought a box to the bench outside Anko's door. Shallow. Offcuts. Holes in the bottom and a tray underneath. Filled with soil from the edge of the south field, where the pale grass with the yellow flowers had grown back.

Empty.

The next morning, before breakfast, Mau watered it. Then he watered Shoko's. With a cup.

When he came back past Anko's door, she was standing in it, in her nightclothes, with her hair down and no notebook. She looked at the box. Damp. Watered. Nothing in it at all.

She did not say anything. She went back inside.

But she left her door open.
