# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 47 — The Stopped Train

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 47
**Continuity state:** Seventh tower visit, from the floor 5 transfer point (walking 5–7): Mau (owner) with Kaneki, Stark and Milim; floor 8 is a long stopped train in a dark tunnel; Kaneki's hearing leads in the cramped dark; Stark keeps the axe short; level and points rise in Mau's branch only; no extraction

*When: Day 319, morning. Two and a half hours outside. Kaneki asked for this visit himself, which he has not done before.*

Kaneki asked at breakfast, which was the first surprise.

He did not usually ask for anything. He worked the night shift at the timber yard and the kilns, and came in at dawn with sawdust in his sleeves, and ate what Momo put in front of him, and went to bed, and did not ask. But this morning he came in from the yard and sat down across from Mau at the long table, and put his hands flat on the board, and said:

"I'd like to come. Next time. To the tower."

Mau looked at him.

"You came the first time," he said.

"The first time I listened at a door," said Kaneki. "I'd like to come and listen properly." He paused. "Anko says there's a floor coming where it's dark. Frieren drew the stair. It goes down into a tunnel." He looked at his hands. "Dark is where I'm useful."

Mau did not say anything for a moment.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Stark and Milim are already on the board. There's room for one more."

Kaneki nodded once. Then, after a moment, he picked up his spoon, and Mau saw that his hands were not quite steady.

* * *

They walked the five floors from the terrace: down the spiral, up the ramp past the raised shutter, through the long blue arcade, where Yuka's lamps were still lit, every one. Stark stopped at the arcade and looked at the shopfronts for a long time.

"Yuka did this?"

"Near end first," said Mau. "No dark on both sides."

Stark looked at the lit lamps, all the way down.

"That's clever," he said. "I wouldn't have thought of that."

"Frieren wouldn't have either," said Mau. "She said so."

Stark looked at him in surprise. Then he looked at the arcade again, with something like respect.

At the far end of the arcade, past the last blue lamp, the stair went down. Not up. The pale eight was painted on the wall at the top. And below it, the stair went down into a darkness that was not like the platform's darkness, or the spiral's. It was close. It smelled of iron and old oil and something stale.

Kaneki stopped at the top.

He stood there for a long time with his eyes half closed and his head a little on one side.

"There's a lot down there," he said quietly. "Moving. Not much. A little. In a line." He opened his eyes. "Like people sitting very still in a long room."

* * *

The notice came up at the bottom of the stair.

*Floor 8 of 100.*

*Objective: reach the front carriage.*

They were standing on a platform. Not like the flooded one. Dry, narrow, with a low ceiling and a wall of dark tiles. And alongside the platform, filling the tunnel from one end to the other, a train.

Mau had never seen a train. He knew that, the way he knew things about himself without remembering them. He looked at it for a long time. A long metal box on wheels, and another behind it, and another, and another, going away into the dark in both directions, joined together. Windows down the side, dark. Doors, closed, in pairs. Painted on the side, faded, a stripe of colour and a line of writing that was almost words.

Stark was staring at it with his mouth slightly open.

"What is it?"

"A train," said Kaneki.

Everyone looked at him.

"For carrying people," said Kaneki. "Underground. Fast." He was looking at the dark windows. "In my world, I rode them every day. To the café. To university." He was quiet for a moment. "They were always very crowded. Everyone sitting very still, not looking at each other."

"Like people sitting very still in a long room," said Mau.

Kaneki looked at him.

"Yes," he said. "Like that."

* * *

The front carriage was a long way off. Mau could see it on the little map, at the far end of the line, past nine carriages. To reach it, they would have to go through them. One after another. Through the doors in the ends, from one carriage to the next, in the dark.

"I'll go first," said Kaneki.

Stark looked at him.

"It's narrow," said Kaneki. "Your axe won't swing in there. Not properly. You'll hit the ceiling." He looked at Milim. "And she'll go through the walls if she tries to hit anything. And then the whole tunnel might come down." He looked at Mau. "I don't need to swing anything. I just need to hear them before they move."

Mau looked at him for a long moment.

"First," he said. "But if you say stop, we stop. And if I say stop, we stop."

"Yes," said Kaneki.

"And Stark goes second. With the axe short."

Stark looked at his axe. Then he shifted his grip, high up the haft, near the head, so it was more like a hatchet than an axe.

"Short," he said.

* * *

The carriages were full of people who were not people.

Mau saw the first one when Kaneki forced the door at the end of the first carriage and they stepped through. Seats down both sides. Poles to hold. And on the seats, sitting very still, in a row: shapes. Thin, grey, slumped, with their heads bowed, the way tired people sit at the end of a long day. Their faces were not faces. They were smooth and grey like stone.

None of them moved.

Kaneki stopped just inside the door. He stood very still, listening.

"They're waiting," he said, very low. "For something to sit down."

"What happens if something sits down?" said Milim, in what she thought was a whisper.

"I don't want to find out," said Kaneki.

They walked through. Down the middle of the carriage, between the two rows of grey shapes, very carefully, touching nothing. Kaneki first, then Stark with his axe held short, then Mau, then Milim at the back, floating a hand's breadth above the floor so her boots would not make any sound.

The grey shapes did not move.

At the end of the carriage, Kaneki forced the next door.

Second carriage. The same. Third. The same. Mau began to breathe a little easier.

In the fourth carriage, one of them moved.

* * *

Kaneki heard it before anyone else. He stopped dead in the middle of the aisle and put out one hand behind him, flat, and Stark stopped, and Mau stopped, and Milim stopped in the air.

At the far end of the carriage, one of the grey shapes had lifted its head.

It was looking at them. Not with eyes. With the smooth grey nothing where a face should have been. And as Mau watched, it began to stand up, very slowly, the way a person stands up when their stop is coming.

Then the one beside it lifted its head.

Then the one beside that.

"They're getting off," said Kaneki, very quietly. "We're at their stop." He did not move. "Don't run. If you run, they all stand up."

"What do we do?" said Stark, through his teeth.

Kaneki was listening. All of him. His head on one side, his eyes half closed.

"Only some of them," he said. "The ones at the far end. Three. Four." He opened his eyes. "The rest are still asleep. If we stay quiet, only the four come. If we're loud, all of them."

"So we're quiet," said Mau.

"So we're quiet," said Kaneki.

The four grey shapes came down the aisle.

They were not fast. They moved the way tired people move, shuffling, heads down. But they came straight down the middle, toward the four of them, and when the first one got close it lifted one long thin grey arm, and the hand at the end of it opened, and the fingers were much too long.

Kaneki moved.

Mau did not see him move. He was standing very still, and then he was not. He was inside the reach of the long grey arm, close, very close, and he had his hand on the grey shape's chest, flat, and he pushed. Not hard. Exactly. And the shape went backward into the next one, and both of them folded down into a seat, and did not get up again.

Stark took the third. Short. The axe came down from high on the haft in a tight hard arc that did not touch the ceiling, and the shape came apart in grey dust.

The fourth reached past Stark for Mau.

Milim caught its wrist.

She did not hit it. She did not do anything at all except hold its wrist, in the air, very still, the way she had learned, this summer, that you could hold a thing without breaking it. And she looked at it, with her face very small and very fierce.

"No," said Milim. Very quietly. "Not him."

Kaneki put his hand on its chest and pushed, exactly, and it folded down into a seat.

The carriage was still. The rest of the grey shapes, all down both sides, had not lifted their heads.

* * *

Nobody said anything until the fifth carriage.

Then Stark said, very low, "How did you know? Which ones would stand?"

Kaneki did not look round.

"They breathe," he said. "Very slowly. Like people asleep. The ones who are about to wake up breathe faster." He forced the next door. "In my world, on the trains, you learned to tell who was about to get off. You could hear it." He paused. "I didn't know I still could."

They went through four more carriages. Twice more, a few of the grey shapes woke, and Kaneki heard them first, and they were quiet, and only the few came. And each time, Kaneki pushed them down exactly, and Stark kept the axe short, and Milim held the ones that reached past without breaking them.

The front carriage was empty.

No seats. A small room at the very front, with a window looking out into the dark tunnel, and a chair, and a panel of dead switches and dials. Mau stood in the doorway and looked at it.

The notice came up.

*Front carriage reached. Route to Floor 9 opened.*

Level ten. Forty-five points. He showed them.

Milim looked at the number, and then at Kaneki.

"You did most of it," she said. "Not the number. The quiet."

Kaneki looked at her in surprise.

"You held them," he said. "Without breaking anything. In a train. That's harder than it sounds."

Milim went pink. She did not say anything. But she floated a little higher.

* * *

They walked back through nine carriages of sleeping grey shapes, very quietly, and none of them woke.

At the platform, at the bottom of the stair, Kaneki stopped and looked back at the long dark train for a long time.

"I used to hate them," he said. "Trains. All those people, sitting very still, not looking at each other. Every day." He was quiet. "And then I lived through a lot of things. And afterward, I used to ride them just to sit somewhere ordinary for an hour." He turned away. "I didn't think I'd ever see one again."

Mau did not say anything.

"Thank you," said Kaneki. "For letting me come."

"You asked," said Mau.

"I don't usually ask," said Kaneki.

"I know," said Mau. "That's why I said yes."

Kaneki looked at him for a moment. Then, very slightly, he smiled. And they went up the stair.
