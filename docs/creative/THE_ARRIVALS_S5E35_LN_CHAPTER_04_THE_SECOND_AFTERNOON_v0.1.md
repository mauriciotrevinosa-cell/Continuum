# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 48 — The Second Afternoon

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 48
**Continuity state:** Gojo and Shoko's second afternoon, as agreed ("next week"); she chooses again; they talk as two people from the same world and the same school (no "in my world" between them); the tobacco seedlings and Gojo's tower research come up lightly; no confession contest, no power demonstration

*When: Day 320, afternoon. Maomao has told the clinic Shoko is busy. Nobody has argued with busy.*

This time Shoko asked him.

She did it at breakfast, in front of everyone, which Gojo would complain about for days. She put her cup down, and looked across the long table at him, and said, in exactly the voice she used to ask a patient where it hurt:

"It's next week. Are you busy?"

The long table went quiet from her end outward.

Gojo, who had been in the middle of telling Yuta about the marble in the car park for the third time, stopped with his spoon in the air.

"No," he said.

"Then two o'clock," said Shoko. "The same place."

She picked up her cup again.

Across the table, Maki looked at Yuta. Yuta looked at his porridge. Mau, beside Frieren, studied the grain of the table with great care.

"You're all looking," said Gojo, to nobody.

"We're eating," said Frieren, without looking up from her book.

"You're eating *loudly*."

* * *

They walked out along the inside path at two.

Past the workshop, where Wakana and Taihei were fitting the frame of the far wall of the next room, and both of them looked up and then very carefully looked down again. Past the bench with three faces on Ori's map, which nobody sat on, because Ori had declared it a monument. Along the bank, under the trees, to the river bend where the water went clear over pale stones.

Gojo had brought the blanket again. And two cups. And the cold water from Senku's jar.

And one more thing, which he did not take out.

Shoko sat down on the blanket. Gojo sat beside her. Not the careful distance this time. A little closer. She noticed. She did not say anything.

For a while they watched the river.

"You told everyone about the car park," said Shoko.

"I told Yuta."

"Three times."

"It's a good story," said Gojo. "The marble went up the shaft. In the dark. And it was *easier*." He looked at his hand. "I wanted someone to know."

"I know," said Shoko. "You told me twice."

Gojo looked at her.

"You were listening?"

"I'm always listening," said Shoko. "It's the job." She drank her water. "And I want to know why it's easier. Not just that it is." She looked at him sideways. "You said that. *I want to know why.*"

"I did say that."

"You never used to want to know why," said Shoko. "At school. You just did things. And they worked. And you never once asked why." She looked at the river. "You'd blow up half the training ground and come in grinning, and Yaga would shout, and I'd patch whoever got in the way. And you never asked why anything worked. You didn't need to."

Gojo was quiet for a moment.

"I need to now," he said.

"I know," said Shoko. "That's what I'm saying. It's new." She paused. "I like it."

* * *

After a while, Gojo took out the thing he had brought.

It was a small pouch. Cloth. Tied with string. He held it in his hand for a moment without opening it.

"I asked Hollis," he said. "Last run. When he brought the paper for the lanterns."

Shoko looked at the pouch.

"He said the old man past Fallow," said Gojo. "The one with the leaf. He said the old man dries it in his barn, hung up in bunches, for a month. And then he rubs it between his hands and puts it in his pipe." He turned the pouch over. "I asked Hollis to ask him how. Exactly. The drying. How long. How warm. How you know when it's ready."

He held out the pouch.

"He wrote it down. The old man. On a piece of paper. Hollis brought it back." He paused. "I don't know if it's right for your plant. Ciel said cousin. Not twin. But it's how *he* does it."

Shoko did not take the pouch at once.

She looked at it in his hand. At the string. At his face, which was not doing anything bright at all.

"You did this," she said slowly. "Without telling Mau."

"Mau's watering the box," said Gojo. "That's his part." He looked at the river. "I wanted a part."

Shoko took the pouch.

She untied the string. Inside was a folded piece of rough paper, the kind Hollis brought. She unfolded it. On it, in a careful, shaky, old man's hand, in a language she could not read, a few lines. And under them, in Hollis's merchant's writing, a translation. *Hang in bunches, leaves down. Dark barn. Not too warm. One moon. Ready when it bends and does not break.*

Shoko read it twice.

"*Ready when it bends and does not break,*" she said.

"That's what Hollis said he said."

Shoko folded the paper very carefully and put it back in the pouch and tied the string.

"When the leaves are big enough," she said. "In a few weeks. If they keep growing." She looked at the pouch in her hand. "Will you help? With the drying?"

Gojo looked at her.

"Not Mau?"

"Mau's watering," said Shoko. "That's his part." She looked at him. "You said you wanted a part."

* * *

They stayed until the light went gold.

At some point Shoko lay back on the blanket with her eyes closed, the way she had the first time, and Gojo sat beside her and watched the river. He did not stare this time. He watched the river properly, the clear water and the pale stones and the small fish, and when he looked at her it was only sometimes.

"You're not staring," said Shoko, without opening her eyes.

"I'm looking at the river."

"The river's on the right side this time."

"I've been practising," said Gojo.

She smiled. He could see it, because he could see everything.

"Satoru," she said.

She said it the way she said it at the long table, the way she had said it at school, as if it were the most ordinary word in the world. It had taken him a long time, in this world, to stop going still when he heard it.

"Mm," he said.

"The last one," said Shoko. "The cigarette. I'm still not going to smoke it."

"I know."

"Even when these are ready. Even if they're good." She opened her eyes and looked up at him. "It stays the last one from home."

"I know," said Gojo. "You said. Mau told me." He paused. "It's a good reason."

Shoko looked at him for a long moment.

"But when the first ones from here are ready," she said, "I'll smoke one of those. On the clinic step. In the evening." She paused. "Will you sit there?"

Gojo looked down at her.

"On the step," he said. "Like the infirmary."

"Like the infirmary," said Shoko. "Except nobody's coming back hurt." She closed her eyes again. "That's the difference I want."

Gojo did not say anything for a long time.

Then he lay down on the blanket beside her. Not touching. Close. With his hands behind his head, looking up at the leaves.

"I'll sit there," he said.

* * *

They walked back at dusk. Not holding hands. Side by side, closer than the first time, past the workshop, where Taihei had gone in and Wakana was sweeping, and looked up, and very carefully looked down again.

At the clinic door, Maomao was waiting with her arms folded.

"Nobody needed you," she said to Shoko. "Somebody stood on a nail. I fixed it. It was boring." She looked at the pouch in Shoko's hand. Then at Gojo. "What's that?"

"Instructions," said Shoko.

"For what?"

"For drying something," said Shoko, "when it bends and does not break."

Maomao looked at her for a long moment. Then at Gojo. Then, very slightly, she rolled her eyes.

"Next week," she said. "I'll tell them she's busy."

She went inside.

Shoko and Gojo stood on the clinic step in the dusk.

"Next week," said Gojo.

"Next week," said Shoko.

She went in. He stood on the step a moment longer. Then he sat down on it, in the evening, the way he would sit there someday when the first leaves from here were ready, and looked at the box on the bench beside the door, with its eleven seedlings and its true leaves, pointed and green.

Mau, coming round the corner with the cup to water Anko's empty box, saw him there, and did not say anything, and went the long way round.
