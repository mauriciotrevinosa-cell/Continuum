# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 49 — The Bell Tower

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 49
**Continuity state:** Rem and Ram take time of their own (planned for this stretch of S5); Ram decides, Rem lets herself be decided for; they climb the bell tower and look over the settlement; Rem's laugh (first found in "Only When It Rains", S4 V25) has become ordinary; the boy from Rem's world stays unnamed; no task, no household work

*When: Day 321, from mid-morning to afternoon. The kitchen coverage is Taihei and Okarun's turn. Rem has tried twice to help and been turned away twice.*

Ram decided at breakfast, the way Ram decided everything: by announcing it as if it had already happened.

"We are going out today," she said. "You and I."

Rem looked up from the teapot. She was halfway down the long table with it. She had stopped going down the table with it every morning in the spring, after the narrow room and the rain, but some mornings her hands still picked it up before she remembered, and this was one of them.

"Out?"

"Out," said Ram. "Of the kitchen. Of the house. Of the coverage, which is not yours today." She held out her hand for the teapot. "Give that to someone else."

Rem looked at the teapot. Then at her sister.

"Where are we going?"

"Somewhere high," said Ram. "I haven't decided which."

Rem gave the teapot to Okarun, who happened to be nearest, and who took it with an expression of mild panic and then, after a moment, began to pour.

* * *

Ram chose the bell tower.

It stood over the plaza, and nobody went up it much. Okarun and Momo had gone up it on the dating day, once, with Vamola guarding the ladder. The fort used it sometimes for important announcements. Otherwise it stood there with its one bell, ringing the hours, and nobody climbed it.

The ladder was steep. Ram went first. Rem followed, and halfway up, she stopped.

"Ram."

"What?"

"Your horn. Does it—" She stopped. "Are you all right? To climb?"

Ram did not stop climbing.

"Frieren filled me three days ago," she said. "I'm full. I can climb a ladder." She reached the top and pulled herself through the hatch and looked back down. "And if I fall, you'll catch me. You always do."

Rem looked up at her.

"I've never caught you."

"Then today you'll have the opportunity," said Ram, and disappeared through the hatch.

Rem climbed the rest of the way.

* * *

The top of the bell tower was a small square platform with a low wall round it and the bell hanging in the middle under a little roof. It was windy up there, even on a still day. The wind came off the hills and pulled at their hair and their skirts and made the bell hum, very faintly, without ringing.

And the whole settlement was laid out below them.

Rem stood at the low wall and looked.

She had never seen it from above. She had lived in it for more than a year, longer than she had lived anywhere since she was very small, and she had never once seen all of it at the same time. The long roofs of the real house, bark shingles, dark. The yard with the ink tree in the middle, black-leaved. The plaza. Arrival House, with its door shut. The workshop, where she could see two small figures fitting a frame. The fields, gold and green, and the south field, pale and soft now with its new grass. The white line of the lime, running round all of it like a chalk mark round a drawing. And beyond the lime, the hills, and the low woods, dark, and the road.

"It's small," said Rem.

"It's very small," Ram agreed. She stood beside her at the wall. "From up here. Smaller than the mansion."

Rem looked at her.

They did not talk about the mansion very often. Not here. It belonged to the other world, the one before, with the people in it they had left. Ram said it the way you say the name of a place you used to live, carefully, as if checking it was still there.

"It's bigger than the mansion," said Rem. "There are more people."

"There are more people," Ram agreed. "And fewer rooms."

"Every room has someone in it."

"Every room has someone in it," said Ram. "And one empty one. For whoever comes." She looked down at the end of the west wing, where the next room was half-built. "That's the difference."

* * *

They sat down with their backs against the low wall, out of the wind, under the bell.

For a while they did not say anything. Ram had brought a small bag. She took out two pieces of Momo's pear bread, wrapped in a cloth, and gave one to Rem.

"You stole these," said Rem.

"I requisitioned them," said Ram. "From the coverage. Taihei didn't notice. He was too busy being polite to Momo."

Rem looked at the pear bread. Then she started to laugh.

It came out of her very easily. Not the small surprised laugh from the narrow room, the one that had happened to her face for the first time that rainy night, that she had put her hand over as if she had not meant to. Just a laugh. Ordinary. The kind of laugh people do every day without noticing.

Ram watched her laugh.

She did not say anything. She just watched, with her pink eyes very steady, until Rem had finished and was wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist.

"What?" said Rem.

"Nothing," said Ram.

"You were looking at me."

"I was looking at you laugh," said Ram. She took a bite of pear bread. "You didn't use to do that. In the mansion. Or here, at first." She chewed. "Now you do it at pear bread."

Rem looked down at the bread in her hands.

"Is that bad?"

"No," said Ram. "It's the best thing in this whole world." She looked out at the hills. "I came back to you through a coin. Because you held it in your hand and thought about me. And I came, and you were—" She stopped. "You were already better. You'd already done most of it. Without me." She was quiet. "I didn't know what I was for, at first. If you didn't need me to hold you together."

Rem did not say anything.

"And then I watched you," said Ram. "For months. In the kitchen. At the table. In your narrow room in the rain." She took another bite. "And I worked it out."

"Worked what out?"

"I'm not for holding you together," said Ram. "You did that. I'm for this." She held up her pear bread. "Stealing bread. Climbing towers. Making you laugh at nothing." She looked at Rem. "That's what sisters are for, when nobody's falling apart."

Rem looked at her for a long time.

Then she leaned sideways, and put her head on her sister's shoulder, the way she used to when they were very small, in another world, before everything.

"I'm glad you came," she said.

"I know," said Ram. "The coin went warm."

* * *

They stayed until the bell rang noon, right above their heads, so loud that both of them put their hands over their ears and Rem laughed again and Ram pretended not to.

Below, in the plaza, small figures looked up.

Ori, at the edge of the fort's Summer Annexe, shading her eyes. Milim, floating, waving both arms. Taihei, at the kitchen door with a cloth over his shoulder, looking up at the bell tower with an expression of great confusion. Momo beside him, laughing.

Ram stood up and looked down at them over the low wall.

"We've been seen," she said.

"We've been seen," Rem agreed.

"Good," said Ram. "Let them see. The two of us, on a tower, doing nothing." She brushed the crumbs off her skirt. "It's good for them."

Rem stood up beside her.

At the end of the west wing, very small, the frame of the next room stood half-finished against the sky. Empty. For whoever comes.

"Ram," said Rem.

"What?"

"Thank you. For deciding."

Ram looked at her sister, standing at the wall of the bell tower with the wind in her blue hair and crumbs on her skirt and her face not careful at all.

"Someone had to," she said. "You'd never have decided to do nothing on your own." She went to the hatch. "Come on. If we're late for lunch, Taihei will have given it to someone else to be polite."

She went down the ladder.

Rem stood at the wall a moment longer. Looking at all of it, the whole small settlement inside its white line, with someone in every room.

Then she followed her sister down.
