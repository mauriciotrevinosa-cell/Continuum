*When: Day 328, dusk. The day after the tenth floor. Yuta is asleep with his boots on. Maki is oiling her spear on the back step and pretending she isn't watching the road. Ori and Yuka are drawing the rooftop with no sky on the back of a ledger page. Two people are walking the outside of the lime for no reason at all.*

There was no reason.

That was the best part.

Not a patrol. Nobody had put them on a list. Anko's round was at three and this was not three. Mikasa had stood her hour at the north end and gone in. Himmel was not on his alternate day. Nobody had asked them to walk the outside of the white line in the last of the light.

They were doing it because Frieren had come out of the workshop with her sleeves pushed up and ink on two fingers and said, "Walk with me," and Mau had put down the bowl he was drying and gone.

"Where?" he had said.

"Nowhere," Frieren had said. "Around."

So they went around.

* * *

The lime ran white across the brown ground in the dusk, a little greyer where the rain had got at it in spring. They walked along the outside of it, slowly, Frieren on the side nearer the field, Mau on the side nearer the dark. Not because anyone had decided that. It was just where they had ended up.

"Ten floors," said Frieren.

"Ten floors."

"Yuka says the rooftop had no sky."

"It had no sky," said Mau. "Just grey. All the way up. Like the inside of a lid."

"And you finished it." She looked at the bandage on his forearm. "Yourself."

"Maki opened it. I just pushed."

"Anko's board says you finished it." She was quiet. "And then the tower threw you all out the first time, because you hadn't."

"Operator," said Mau. "May not carry."

"I read it," said Frieren. She did not say anything else about it. Not then.

"And you kept the guardian."

"I kept it. And walked home with nothing in the cup." He shrugged. "Next time I'd rather have a little left."

Frieren looked at him sideways.

"You keep saying that."

"It keeps being true."

She did not answer. She walked. The grass on the outside of the lime was going gold at the tips, the way it did at the end of summer, and the crickets had started in the north field. The last of the light lay low and orange across everything, and the house behind them was beginning to light its first windows, one and then another.

"I like this," said Frieren.

"Walking?"

"Walking nowhere." She looked at the field. "With nothing to look for. Himmel used to make us do this. In villages. Walk around for no reason." She was quiet. "I used to think it was a waste of an evening."

"And now?"

"Now I've got the evenings," said Frieren. "So I can waste some."

He bumped her shoulder with his. She bumped him back, harder, and nearly knocked him over the lime, and he laughed, and she looked pleased with herself in the particular way she only looked when nobody else could see.

* * *

They came round toward the north gate.

The timber frame stood over the north road with its one hung leaf open, the way it was always open in daylight. Beyond it the road went off brown and straight between the low hills, toward the woods and the bend and, much further, the ford.

There were people on the road.

Mau saw them first. Five shapes, a hundred paces out, past the lime, walking toward the gate without hurrying. Four of a size, slim, in pale clothes. One much bigger. Wrong in its outline. Too many joints in the shoulders.

Frieren saw them a breath after he did.

She stopped walking.

He felt her change. Not her face. Her face never did much. Something under it, going still and cold and very, very attentive. The way it had gone on the day of the horned strangers at the bend, when she had said *I know their kind* and her staff had already been up.

Her staff was up now.

"Horns," she said quietly.

He looked again. In the low light, at the four pale figures. Small curved horns at the temple. Pleasant faces. Hands loose and open at their sides.

"Demons," said Mau.

"Demons," said Frieren. "And something else." She looked at the big one. "That isn't mine."

The big one turned its head toward them. Its face was not a face. It was the idea of a face that something frightened had once tried to remember.

"Devil," said Mau.

* * *

They stopped twenty paces outside the lime.

The four demons stood in a line, polite, hands open. The devil stood behind them, a head and a half taller, breathing in a way that did not quite match anything that breathed. The light was going. The windows behind Mau were lighting one at a time.

The demon in the middle smiled.

"Good evening," it said.

Its voice was warm. Pleasant. A young man's voice, a little shy, the kind of voice that asks for directions.

"We've come a long way," it said. "We have orders. We don't want to frighten anyone."

"Orders from who?" said Mau.

"From the new union," said the demon. "Of the empires. And the otherworlders who stand with them." It said *otherworlders* carefully, the way you say a word you have practised. "Against the monsters of this settlement."

Behind Mau, very far off, a shutter opened on the last cottage and closed again.

"The one who makes monsters," said the demon, gently. "Shadows. Suns. Other things." Its eyes, pale and pleasant, rested on Mau. "We know who you are. Everyone knows now."

"And?"

"And it doesn't need to be everyone," said the demon. Kindly. As if it were offering him something. "Come with us. Only you. And the rest of them live." It opened its hands wider. "The children. The old woman with the high chair. The girl who draws. They don't need to be part of this. Give us the monster, and we'll leave the house alone."

Frieren did not move.

Mau felt her get ready to.

* * *

She did not get the chance.

Because Mau was not beside her any more.

He was twenty paces out, past the lime, standing between two of the demons with an arm over each of their shoulders. Easily. Like a man at a festival with two friends he has not seen in years. He had not run. Nobody had seen him go. One moment he was on the lime, and the next he was there, between them, and his arms were round their necks, and he was smiling.

The two demons went very still.

"See, here's the thing," said Mau. Pleasantly. Conversationally. "You think we're going to believe you."

The one on his left tried to step away. His arm did not let it.

"From what I understand," said Mau, "you use our language to lie. You learned it the way a hunter learns a bird call." He tilted his head toward the one on his right. "You find the word that opens the shutter. *Mother.* *Healer.* *The children live.*" He smiled at it. "Frieren explained it to us. At dinner. She was very clear."

The demon in the middle had stopped smiling.

It looked past Mau, at the figure still standing on the lime with her staff up and her face perfectly calm in the dusk.

Its pleasant voice went flat.

"Frieren," it said. "The Slayer."

* * *

Mau turned his head and looked at her.

Over his shoulder. With his arms still round the necks of two demons, twenty paces out in the dark.

Frieren looked back at him. Unmoved. Staff up.

"What?" she said. "I don't like monsters."

He laughed.

He could not help it. It came out of him all at once, loud and real, out there on the north road with his arms round two creatures that had come to take him away. *I don't like monsters.* From her. To him. The one they had all just called the monster. He laughed so hard the demon on his left flinched.

Frieren's mouth moved. Very slightly. At one corner.

She stepped off the lime toward him.

* * *

And he felt it.

Not saw. Felt. The way the Noise had always felt things before he did. A line. Thin. Very fast. Coming out of the dark of the woods to the left of the road, low, flat, level, the way a scythe goes through grass.

Not at him.

At her.

Frieren felt it too. He saw her feel it. Her head turned, a fraction, toward the woods. Her staff came round. And in front of her, between her and the dark, the planes of her shield went up. Transparent. Sharp-edged. Bright. The shield that had stopped a demon's first spell outside the lime on the day of the bend. The shield he had seen stop everything.

He saw it go up.

And for one second—

One second.

He let himself breathe.
