# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 54 — The Last Firefly

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 54
**Continuity state:** Close of V29. Nazuna has counted the fireflies every night since Day 318 ("Ask Nazuna"); on the night she expects the last one, she tells Ori first; the house's own lights will be lit the next night (the festival, which opens V30); Ori, Milim, Yuka, Nazuna, Frieren and Mau go out; Ori's Day 296 line "so it's not a hundred years again"; the summer has turned; no threat at the volume's end

*When: Day 336, night. Three nights since the roof. Nazuna has counted eleven fireflies, then six, then two. Ori has gone out with her every night.*

Nazuna counted them every night.

She had been counting all summer, though nobody knew that until the day Frieren said *when the fireflies are gone*, and *Nazuna will know*, and Fern wrote *(Ask Nazuna.)* in the ledger. That evening Nazuna came down at dusk and found the ledger open at the festival page, and read the line, and wrote a number under it, small, without telling anyone.

After that, she said it out loud, every evening. She came down the stairs from the loft over Anko's bed at dusk, with her hair in her face and her coat half on, and went to the kitchen for something to eat, and on the way past the long table she said a number. Thirty-three. Twenty-eight. Twenty. Fifteen.

Nobody asked her to. She just did it. The way she sat on top of Anko's red stake every night watching the north field. The way she had kept the fireflies secret all summer so that somebody small could find them first.

Eleven. Six. Two.

On the eighteenth night after the ledger, she came down the stairs and did not say a number.

She went straight to the fort, instead. To the curtain. She stood outside it, the way non-citizens stood, and said, very quietly, through the cloth:

"Ori. Tonight. I think tonight's the last one."

* * *

Ori came out of the fort with her shoes in her hand.

Milim came out behind her, already floating, already awake, because Milim had refused to go to sleep for three nights in case it was the last one. And behind Milim, Yuka, who had not been invited and had come anyway, and nobody told her to go back.

Ori did not go to find Mau and Frieren. She did not need to. They were already on the back step, waiting. They had been on the back step every night for three nights, waiting for Nazuna to come down the stairs and not say a number.

Frieren stood up.

"Tonight?" she said.

"Nazuna thinks so," said Ori.

Frieren looked at the girl in front of her, with her shoes in her hand and her face very serious and very bright.

"Then we go," she said.

* * *

They walked out past the end of the street, along the edge of the north field. Nazuna at the front, without a lantern. Ori beside her. Milim floating behind. Yuka. Mau and Frieren at the back.

The grass was long and pale and dry. Summer had dried it. It crackled a little under their feet now, where at the start it had been soft and green and wet at the roots. The ditch where the fireflies had been thickest was almost dry too. Only a thin line of water at the bottom, catching the starlight.

Nazuna stopped them at the same place as the first night. Well inside the lime. By the ditch.

"Sit down," she said. "Don't talk. Let your eyes get used to the dark."

They sat down in the dry grass, in a row. Ori in the middle. Milim on one side, Yuka on the other. Frieren beside Yuka. Mau beside Frieren. Nazuna a little apart, with her knees up.

It was very dark.

The hills, and the stars above them, and the white line of lime, faint. Nothing else. No small green-gold lights rising out of the grass. No drifting curves.

They waited.

* * *

They waited for a long time.

Milim lasted about four minutes before she started to wriggle. Yuka put a hand on her arm. Milim stopped wriggling. That was a record too, Mau thought.

Ori did not move at all. She sat in the dry grass with her knees up and her hands round them and her eyes on the ditch, very still, the way Frieren sat when she was waiting for something she did not want to miss.

Mau did not know when Ori had learned to sit like that. He thought maybe she had always known.

The stars wheeled very slowly.

"Maybe it's already gone," whispered Milim, at last. "Maybe it went last night and Nazuna missed it."

"I don't miss them," said Nazuna, very quietly.

"Maybe—"

"Shh," said Ori. "Wait."

They waited.

And then, low, in the dry grass by the ditch, a light.

One. Small. Green-gold. On.

Off.

On.

It rose, very slowly, out of the grass, in a long lazy curve, the way they had risen on the first night, when there had been hundreds. Just one, now. On its own. Drifting up into the warm dark above the ditch, blinking, on, off, on, as if it did not know it was the last, or knew and did not mind.

Nobody breathed.

It drifted toward them. Slowly. Over the dry grass. It came past Nazuna, and past Milim, who went perfectly rigid, and hung for a moment in the air in front of Ori, close enough to touch.

Ori did not touch it.

She held out her hand, palm up, very still, the way you hold out your hand when you are not reaching, just offering.

The firefly drifted down.

It landed on her palm.

On. Off. On. Very small. Very bright. Sitting in the middle of Ori's hand in the dark.

Ori did not move. She did not breathe. She looked at the small green-gold light on her palm with her whole face.

Frieren, beside Yuka, had put her hand over her mouth.

On. Off.

On.

Then it lifted off Ori's palm, slowly, and rose, up and up, past all of them, into the dark above the field. Higher than any of them had seen one go all summer. Up toward the strange stars, blinking, smaller and smaller, until Mau could not tell any more which light was the firefly and which was a star.

Then he could not see it at all.

* * *

Nobody said anything for a long time.

The north field was dark. No small lights in the grass. No drifting curves. Just the dry pale grass and the thin water in the ditch and the stars.

"That was the last one," said Nazuna. Very quietly.

Ori was still holding out her hand. Palm up. Empty.

"I know," she said.

She closed her hand, very gently, as if there were still something in it.

Then she turned to Frieren.

"A hundred years," said Ori. "You said. The first night. That you hadn't seen them in a hundred years. That you always meant to go back and never did."

Frieren took her hand away from her mouth.

"I said that."

"It's not a hundred years now," said Ori. "It's this summer. All of it. Every night we came." She looked at her closed hand. "So it's not a hundred years again. Not this time."

Frieren did not say anything.

She could not. Mau, beside her, could see that she could not. Her face was doing the thing it did very rarely, the thing that was not serene and not calm, the thing that was a thousand years of meaning to go back and never going and then, one summer, going every single night with a child.

She reached across Yuka. And put her hand on Ori's closed one.

"Next summer," said Frieren. Her voice was not steady. "Every night. Again."

"Every night," said Ori.

"And the summer after."

"And the summer after."

"And—" Frieren stopped.

"And every summer," said Ori. "Until you're bored."

Frieren made a sound that was half a laugh and half something else.

"I won't get bored," she said.

"I know," said Ori. "That's why I said it."

* * *

Nazuna stood up first.

She stood in the dry grass by the dark ditch and looked out at the north field for a long time. Then she looked back at the house. At the long roofs. At the ink tree in the yard, with its pale unlit paper lanterns hanging in the branches, dozens of them, waiting.

"Tomorrow," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"The fireflies are gone," said Nazuna. "Frieren said. When the fireflies are gone, the house lights its own." She looked at Ori. "So tomorrow."

Ori stood up. She had her shoes in her hand still. She had not put them on all night.

"Tomorrow," she said. "The festival."

Milim floated straight up into the air, a full body-length, and came down again, and did not say anything, because Yuka had her hand over Milim's mouth.

"I have to tell Fern," said Ori. "And Umaru. And Momo. And Kessoku. And the five." She was already walking. "And Taihei, because of the curry. And Wakana, because of the lanterns. And Coco, because of the lights."

"It's midnight," said Mau.

"Then I'll tell them at breakfast," said Ori. "First thing. Before anyone else." She stopped walking and turned round and looked at him, very serious. "I told you first. About the fireflies. At the beginning."

"You did."

"So I get to tell everyone else about the festival first."

"That's fair," said Mau.

Ori nodded, satisfied, and walked on toward the house through the dry grass, with her shoes in one hand and her other hand still closed, very gently, as if there were still something in it.

* * *

Frieren and Mau walked back last.

Behind Nazuna, who had gone back out to the red stake for the rest of the night shift. Behind Milim and Yuka, arguing in whispers about whether a firefly could reach a star. Behind Ori, small and straight, going home in the dark.

At the end of the street, Frieren stopped.

She looked back at the north field. Dark. Dry. No small lights at all.

"It went up," she said. "Higher than the others. All summer, they stayed low. In the grass. And the last one went up."

"Toward the stars," said Mau.

"Toward *Still Here*," said Frieren. "I watched. It went that way. Over the hills."

Mau looked up. Low over the hills, a little apart from the others, the small steady star he had named on the roof with the telescope.

He did not know if she was right. He did not think it mattered.

"Tomorrow," said Frieren.

"Tomorrow," said Mau.

She took his hand. They walked back to the house, where every room had someone in it, and one empty one, half-built, for whoever comes. And in the ink tree in the dark yard, dozens of paper lanterns hung pale and unlit in the branches, waiting for the house to light its own.
