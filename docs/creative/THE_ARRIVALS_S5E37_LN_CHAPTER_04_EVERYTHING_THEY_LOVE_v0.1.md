## Chapter 57 — Everything They Love

*When: Day 328, the first minute of real dark. In the clinic, Shoko's hands are on the line and will not come off it. At the north end of the lime, Gojo is watching the road and has told Yuta not to cross. Rimuru is halfway between.*

He came back the way he had left.

Without a door. Without a step. He was not on the road, and then he was, kneeling in the dark exactly where he had knelt, in the long stain where she had fallen, with her staff lying across the ruts beside his knee.

He picked it up.

He stood.

None of them had moved. Three demons in a line on the road, where four had been, with a small grey pile of ash at the end of it. The big devil behind them, hunched, its wrong shoulders up round its head. And at the edge of the cut woods, among the long pale rows of fallen trees, the tall one. The one that had made the line.

Mau looked at it.

Only at it.

* * *

The ground did not shake this time.

That was worse. They felt it be worse. The tremor had been the sound of something waking up. This was the silence after something is awake.

His shadow lay out from his feet across the whole width of the road and past the edges of it into the grass, and it was not still. It turned. Slowly. Things in it turned with it. A blue the size of a marble. A red. A white shape like a door. Lines like ink in water. The shape of a hand much too large for any arm. A pale glyph from a hat-witch's page, sparking. The long curved shadow of a dog with one white ear, lying very low, very still, with its head on its paws.

None of it was new.

All of it had been there yesterday. And the day before. Since the Muslin. Since the clearing. Folded away behind walls he held shut every single morning by choosing to.

He was not choosing to.

* * *

The tall devil moved first.

It was the only one that had any right to. It lifted one long arm, and Mau felt the line come, the same line, low and flat and level, the line that went through things as if they had been told to come apart.

It came at him.

He did not move out of its way.

Something in his walls knew that shape. Had known it for a long time. Had kept it, folded small, since the night Sukuna had stood behind him, close enough to share a breath, and the Noise had read everything he was. The Noise always read. It had taken the shape of his cutting the way it took the shape of everything, and put it away, and never once let it out on purpose.

It came out now.

The line from the devil met another line coming the other way, and they went through each other, and the devil's did not reach him.

His reached the devil.

* * *

It did not happen once.

It happened the way the woods behind the devil had happened. In rows. One line, and then another, and then another, level, neat, at the same height, the way corn lies down behind a scythe. The devil did not fall. It came apart standing up, piece by piece, line by line, the way it had laid the forest down to reach her. It made no sound. There was not enough of it left in one place to make a sound.

When the lines stopped, there was a long stretch of grass at the edge of the cut woods with nothing standing in it.

Mau had not looked away from it the whole time.

He looked at the big one.

* * *

The big devil broke.

Not its body. Its nerve. It turned with its wrong shoulders and its idea of a face and ran, back up the north road toward the dark, on legs that did not bend the right way, faster than anything that size should run.

A small blue point opened in the air ahead of it.

The size of a marble. The size Gojo's was. No bigger. It hung in the dark over the road, very still, very blue.

The devil ran into it.

It did not stop. It could not stop. The blue took it the way a drain takes water, all of it, from the front backward, its wrong shoulders and its running legs and its face that was not a face, folding inward into a point the size of a fist, then a walnut, then a marble.

Then the blue went out.

There was nothing on the road.

* * *

Two of the three demons tried to speak at once.

Mau knew what they would say. He had heard it from their kind before. *Mother. Healer. Please. We were only following. We didn't know.* The right words. The words that opened shutters.

He did not let them find out which word would work.

The light came off his hand the way it came off Frieren's staff. White. Narrow. Clean. The spell that a demon had made, a long time ago, to kill people. The spell that people had stolen back, and studied, and made into something ordinary. *Zoltraak.* He had watched her cast it a hundred times. It had been in his walls for a long time, with everything else of hers, and he had never once let it out.

He used it.

One. Then the other.

They went to ash. Two small neat piles on the road beside the first.

* * *

One was left.

The one in the middle. The one who had spoken first. The one with the warm young voice that asked for directions.

It was on its knees on the road. Mau did not remember telling it to kneel. It might have done that itself. Its pleasant face had come apart into something that did not know how to be pleasant. Its hands were open on the road in front of it, palms up, empty. Nothing in them. Nothing to lure with.

Mau walked to it.

He did not hurry. He stopped in front of it, close, with Frieren's staff in one hand and her blood drying dark on both arms to the elbow. He looked down at it.

The demon looked up.

His left eye was black and red. The other was his own. Brown. Ordinary. Utterly empty.

* * *

"You're going to go back," said Mau.

His voice was quiet. Level. Almost kind. It was the most frightening thing the demon had ever heard.

"To whoever sent you. Your union. Your empires. Your otherworlders." He did not blink. "You're going to tell them what you saw."

The demon could not speak. Its mouth moved.

"And you're going to tell them this," said Mau. "Exactly this. Word for word. You're good with words. Your kind learns them so carefully."

He crouched. Down on his heels in the road, so that his face was level with the demon's.

"If anyone," said Mau, "ever again, so much as raises an eyebrow at her—" He did not say her name. He did not need to. "—if anyone disrespects her, touches her, looks at her the wrong way, says one word about her being weak—"

He stopped.

"I won't stop at them," he said. "Not at the ones who come. Not at the ones who sent them. I'll find everything they love. Every city. Every house. Every field. Every single thing they love." His voice did not rise. "And I will flatten it. The way you flattened those trees. Until there's nothing left standing anywhere that they ever cared about."

The road was perfectly silent.

"Say it back," said Mau.

The demon said it back. Word for word. Its warm voice shaking so badly the words came out in pieces. It got every one of them right.

"Good," said Mau.

He stood up.

"Go."

* * *

It went.

It did not walk. It did not look back. It scrambled up off the road on its hands and its feet like an animal, and ran, up the north road, toward the dark and the hills and whatever had sent it, as fast as anything with legs had ever run. Before he could change his mind. It knew he might. It could see in his face that he might.

He did not watch it go.

The pressure lifted.

All of it, at once, the way it had lifted in the clinic. The turning shadow drew in. The blue and the red and the white door and the hand and the glyph and the dog went back down into the dark under him and were gone, folded small, behind walls that came back up one after another like doors closing down a long corridor. His left eye closed. The crickets in the north field started again, one, then two, then all of them.

The woods stayed cut. Long pale rows of them, lying down in the dark, as far as he could see.

He did not look at them.

He did not look at the ash.

He turned round, with her staff in his hand, toward the house. Toward the six lit windows and the one that was the clinic.

And began to walk.
