# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 59 — I'm Hungry

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 59

*When: Day 330, first light. Two nights and a day since the north road. The clinic let her be moved to the east room yesterday, on Shoko's word and four people's arms. On the shelf, the old grimoire with four flowers in it and the fifth from the date. Her staff is leaning against the bed where someone put it, within reach.*

She woke with her hand on her chest.

She did not remember putting it there. It was just there when she came up out of the dark. Her right hand, flat, over the middle of her chest, the way you put your hand on something to make sure it is still where you left it.

Under her palm, something small.

A ridge. Very slight. Smooth. No longer than the top joint of her finger. Over her heart.

She lay still and felt it.

It did not hurt. Nothing hurt. She felt heavy, and slow, and very thirsty, and the light coming in at the east window was grey and early. But nothing hurt.

She remembered a road. Dusk. Walking nowhere. Laughing.

She remembered a line coming out of the dark, and her shield going up.

And then she remembered nothing.

* * *

Her other hand was being held.

She knew it before she turned her head. She knew that hand. Every one of the fingers on it. The crooked one that had healed badly. The calluses from the axe and the bowls and the tower. She had held it in a hundred dark rooms. She knew it the way she knew her own.

It was cold.

And it was holding hers so tightly that her fingers had gone a little numb.

She turned her head.

* * *

Mau was sitting in the chair beside the bed.

He had pulled it right up against the frame. He was sitting forward in it, bent over, both his hands round her left one, his forehead nearly resting on them. He was still in the shirt. She could see the dark on it, dried brown now, all down the front and up both sleeves to the elbow. Nobody had made him change it. Or someone had tried and he had not let go of her hand long enough.

His face.

She had seen his face in a great many states. Laughing. Furious. Asleep. Wet. Grey after the Trial. Blank with exhaustion after the bay night, when the hole had opened again.

She had never seen it like this.

It was hollow. Grey under the eyes, grey round the mouth. His lips were cracked. He looked as if he had not slept in a week, or eaten, or drunk, or moved. His eyes were open, fixed on her hand in his, and they had the look of someone who had been staring at one thing so long he had forgotten there was anything else to look at.

He had not noticed she was awake.

* * *

Through the connecting door, open a crack, a small voice.

"Mau."

Ori. In the doorway, in her nightdress, with her hair in her face. She had not come in. She was standing on the threshold, the way she stood when she had been told to stay out and was deciding how far *out* went.

"Mau," said Ori again. Very quietly. "Shoko says you have to sleep. She said it three times." A pause. "Maomao brought soup. Again. It's on the floor. It's cold."

He did not move.

"You don't have to go away," said Ori. "You can sleep in the chair. Or on the floor. I'll watch her. I'll wake you up if she moves." Her voice did something. "Please. At least sleep. Just a little bit. *Please.*"

He did not answer.

He did not look up.

Frieren saw Ori's face, through the crack in the door. Ori had been crying. Ori did not cry. Not often. Frieren could count the times. She had been crying, and had stopped, and had come to the door to try again.

Frieren moved her fingers.

Just a little. Inside his hands.

* * *

He went still.

Completely. All of him. The way an animal goes still when it hears a sound in the dark. His eyes did not move from her hand. As if he did not dare look anywhere else in case it had not happened.

She moved them again.

He looked up.

He looked at her face, and she watched him see that her eyes were open, and she watched what that did to him, and she would remember it for the rest of her very long life.

It was like watching someone come back from the dead. Not her. Him.

His face moved. All of it. As if it had been frozen for two days and had only now been allowed to thaw. His mouth opened. His eyes went wide and then wet, all at once. His hands, round hers, tightened, so hard it hurt, and then loosened, immediately, terrified of hurting her.

"Hi," said Frieren.

Her voice came out as a scrape. She had not used it in two days.

"I'm all right," she said. "I'm here." She tried to smile. It was probably not much of one. "I'm fine. Mau. I'm—"

* * *

"I'm sorry," said Mau.

He said it before she could finish. It came out of him all at once, broken, the way something comes out that has been held behind a wall too long.

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm sorry, Frieren, I'm so sorry—"

"Mau—"

"I'm sorry." He could not stop. His forehead went down onto their hands. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

His hands were shaking.

She felt it. Round her fingers. A fine, fast tremor, all the way through both of them, that did not stop. She did not know if it was exhaustion. Two days without sleeping, without eating, without moving from a chair. Or if it was the other thing. The thing she had felt on the road, at the very end, before the dark. Or both.

"I saw your shield," said Mau. Into her hand. "I saw it go up. I saw it, and I—" His voice broke. "I stopped. For one second. I thought, *she's got it, her shield's up, she's fine,* and I breathed. I *breathed.*" His shoulders shook. "And it went through. And if I hadn't—if I'd just—one second, Frieren. One second. I was right there. I was *right there*—"

"Mau."

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry—"

* * *

She understood.

Not all at once. In pieces. The way she understood most things that mattered, slowly, long after anyone else would have.

The chair pulled up against the frame. The shirt nobody had got off him. The soup on the floor, cold. Ori in the doorway, asking him to sleep for the third time. His cracked lips, his grey face, his hands that would not stop shaking.

He had not moved.

For two days, he had not moved. Not to eat. Not to sleep. Not to wash her blood off his arms. He had sat in that chair with her hand in his and watched her breathe, every breath, because he had not believed it would keep happening unless he watched.

He had thought she was gone.

He had held her on that road and thought she was gone, and some part of him had not stopped thinking it since. Not when Shoko said she was healed. Not when they moved her up here. Not through two nights of watching her breathe. He had been sitting in a chair for two days waiting to lose her.

He was so frightened.

She had never seen him this frightened. Not of the Trial. Not of the Noise. Not of Sukuna. Not of anything that had ever been done to him.

Only of this.

* * *

She did not tell him it was not his fault.

She knew that would not work. Not now. Not with his forehead on her hand and the word *sorry* still coming out of him in pieces. She knew him. She knew that if she said *it's not your fault*, he would hear *you're lying to be kind*, and keep going.

So she said something else.

"Mau," said Frieren.

He did not stop.

"*Mau.*"

He stopped. He lifted his head. His face was wet and wrecked and terrified.

"I'm hungry," said Frieren.

* * *

He looked at her.

For a moment he did not understand. She watched him not understand. Watched the word go into him and find nowhere to land, because it was so small, and so ordinary, and nothing in the last two days had been small or ordinary.

"I'm hungry," said Frieren again. Patiently. "Very. I haven't eaten in two days." She looked at him. "Neither have you."

He breathed.

Out. A long, ragged breath, all the way out.

And something in his face came back. Not all of it. Some. The part that knew what to do with *I'm hungry*. The part that had dried bowls and carried trays. The part that knew how to look after someone.

"Okay," said Mau.

His voice was very quiet.

"Okay."

* * *

He did not let her walk.

She could have. She thought she could have. Slowly. With a hand on the wall. She started to sit up to show him.

He did not let her. He stood up out of the chair, on legs that nearly did not hold him, and bent down and gathered her up off the bed. One arm under her knees. One behind her back. Carefully. So carefully. As if she were made of something that would break if he breathed on it.

She put her arm round his neck.

He was shaking. All of him. She could feel it through his shirt, through his arms. But he did not drop her. He would not have dropped her if the house had fallen down round them.

Ori was in the doorway with both hands over her mouth.

"She's hungry," said Mau.

Ori took her hands away from her mouth.

"*Momo,*" she said. And ran.

* * *

He carried her down the stairs.

Slowly. One step at a time. The stairs she had climbed a thousand times. Past the corridor and the long room and into the kitchen, with the early light coming in grey through the high windows and the smell of bread.

The house was at breakfast.

Not all of it. Most. The ones who were up early, and the ones who had not really slept. Taihei at the stove. Momo with the bread knife. Fern at the head of the table with the ledger open and her pen not moving. Stark beside her. Himmel at the far end, with his hair not brushed at all. Gojo leaning on the wall by the door with a cup he was not drinking from, and Shoko beside him. Yuta and Maki. Rimuru, with Diablo. Kessoku in a clump at the end of the bench, Bocchi with her face hidden. Seiko in the high seat. Anko by the board.

Nobody was eating.

They were all looking at the stairs. Ori had come down them at a run and shouted *she's hungry*, and every face in the room had turned to the doorway, and now they were looking at it.

And Mau came through it, carrying Frieren.

* * *

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

Then Momo put down the bread knife.

"Sit her down," said Momo. Her voice was very rough. "Sit her down, idiot. Not on the bench, she'll fall off. The chair. With the arms."

Taihei was already moving a chair. The one with arms. He put it at the corner of the table by the stove, where it was warm.

Mau put Frieren in it. Carefully. He knelt beside it. He did not let go of her hand.

Fern had stood up. She came round the table and stopped beside the chair and looked at Frieren for a long time without saying anything. Then she put her hand on Frieren's head, the way Frieren used to put her hand on Fern's, a long time ago, when Fern was small. Just for a moment.

"Good morning, Frieren-sama," said Fern. Her voice did not quite work.

"Good morning, Fern," said Frieren.

* * *

Himmel was at the far end of the table.

He did not come closer. He stood up. He looked at her, across the whole length of the long room, with his hair not brushed and his face doing something it had not done since a hundred villages and a long road ago. Then he smiled. The real one. Not the one for villages.

He sat back down.

He did not say anything. He did not need to.

* * *

Taihei put a bowl in front of her. Porridge. With honey. Too much honey. He put another in front of Mau, on the floor beside the chair, because Mau was kneeling on the floor and showed no sign of getting up.

"Both of you," said Taihei. "Eat."

Frieren picked up her spoon.

Mau did not pick up his.

Frieren looked at him. Then she took a spoonful of her own porridge, and held it out. Not to her own mouth. To his.

He looked at it.

"I'm not eating until you do," said Frieren.

He opened his mouth. She fed him the spoonful. He swallowed it. His eyes closed.

Then he picked up his own spoon.

Shoko, by the door, let out a breath she had been holding for two days.

* * *

Across the kitchen, by the board, Anko took the stub of pencil from behind her ear.

She had not written anything for Day 328. She had looked at the empty space on the plank for two days and not known what to put there. Not the road. Not the woods. Not the ash. The house knew all that already. It had felt it in its teeth.

She looked at the two of them in the corner by the stove. The elf in the chair with the arms, eating porridge with too much honey. The boy on the floor beside her, eating out of a bowl with a hand that was still shaking, not letting go of her other hand.

She wrote:

*Day 328. North road.*

*Day 330. She's hungry.*

She put the pencil back behind her ear.

Outside, beyond the lime, to the north, the woods still lay in long pale rows where they had been cut. Somewhere much further than that, a demon with a warm young voice was still running, carrying a message word for word to whoever had sent it.

In the kitchen, nobody was thinking about that yet.

Frieren finished her porridge. Then she held out the bowl to Taihei.

"More," she said.
