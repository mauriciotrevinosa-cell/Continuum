# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 29
## Chapter 60 — Five on the Roof

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-17)
**Volume:** 29
**Reading order:** 60
**Continuity state:** The seventh-day roof night (every 7 days from Day 81; Day 333 = 81 + 7x36) with Rimuru, Yuta, Gojo and Mau, and Taihei invited for the first time by Mau, as "the other older brother"; ordinary talk, the festival, the tower's tenth floor; Taihei is not made into a fighter and keeps his own judgement. Five nights after the north road (DH-123): Gojo says he watched from the lime and kept Yuta back; Frieren is eating again; Mau says he will go up the tower faster, not alone and within Shoko's limits, because of the one second on the road; Gojo will come more often. No vow, no title; the word 'strongest' is not said

*When: Day 333, night. The roof hatch still squeaks a little. Yuta has not fixed it again, and Maki has stopped asking.*

Mau asked Taihei at dinner, which made Umaru put down her spoon.

"The roof," said Mau. "Tonight. It's a roof night. Every seventh day." He looked across the table at Taihei. "If you want to come."

Taihei looked at him.

Then at his sister, who was staring at Mau with an expression of deep suspicion.

"What happens on the roof?" said Taihei.

"Nothing," said Mau. "That's the point. We sit. Rimuru complains about the draught. Yuta brings the cups. Gojo arrives late and pretends he was always going to." He paused. "It's the four of us. It's been the four of us for a long time." He looked at Umaru. "I thought it might be five. Tonight. If he wants."

Umaru looked at him for a long moment.

"Two older brothers," she said slowly.

"Two older brothers," said Mau.

Umaru looked at Taihei. Then she picked up her spoon again.

"He gets the bad spot," she said. "By the draught. It's tradition."

"That's Rimuru's spot," said Mau.

"Then Rimuru gets a new spot," said Umaru. "It's a constitutional amendment."

* * *

Rimuru got to the roof first and objected to it.

"I was told," he said, as Mau came up through the hatch, "that I am being displaced. From my spot. By constitutional amendment."

"Umaru's amendment."

"I wasn't consulted."

"You're an honorary citizen," said Mau. "You don't get consulted. You get right of passage."

Rimuru opened his mouth. Then closed it.

"That's annoyingly accurate," he said.

Yuta came up with the cups, five of them this time, on a tray, and set them down on the flat part of the roof where the boards still held a little of the day's heat. Then Taihei came up the ladder behind him, slowly, carefully, a man who had not climbed many ladders onto many roofs, and stood at the top of the hatch looking round.

At the stars. Too many of them, strange ones. At the dark yard below, with the ink tree a black shape in the middle of it. At the long roofs of the house, bark shingles, going off into the dark. At the white line of the lime, faint, running round everything like a chalk mark. At the paper lanterns Wakana and Shizuka and Nano had already started hanging in the ink tree, unlit, pale shapes in the branches, waiting.

"Oh," said Taihei.

"You can sit there," said Rimuru, with great dignity, pointing at his own former spot. "By the draught. It's yours now. By law."

Taihei sat down by the draught.

"It's very drafty," he said, after a moment.

"Yes," said Rimuru. "It is. Welcome."

* * *

Gojo came up twenty minutes later and pretended he had always been going to.

He stopped at the top of the hatch and looked at the five of them. At Taihei, by the draught.

"There's five," he said.

"There's five," said Mau.

Gojo looked at Taihei for a long moment with his bare eyes. Then he sat down, in his usual spot, which nobody had amended.

"Good," he said. "Four was getting predictable."

* * *

For a long time they did not say very much.

That was the point of the roof. Nobody had to. They drank whatever was in the cups, which Yuta said was tea and Rimuru said was a crime against tea. The wind came off the hills, warm, smelling of the dry summer grass. Somewhere far out in the north field, a few fireflies were still blinking, not as many as before, low in the long grass. Below, in the house, someone was laughing, and a door closed, and Milim's voice said something about biscuits and Ori's voice said no.

"Ten," said Yuta, after a while.

Everyone looked at him.

"The tower," said Yuta. "Ten floors. Maki and Yuka and me. And Mau." He turned his cup in his hands. "Mau finished the guardian. Maki opened it for him. Inside its reach." He looked at his cup. "The first time, she nearly finished it herself. I'd forgotten what that looked like."

"You hadn't forgotten," said Gojo. "You just hadn't seen it."

Yuta looked at him.

"That's what Maki said."

"Maki's usually right," said Gojo. "It's very annoying."

Taihei, by the draught, was listening. He had been listening the whole time, very quietly, the way he had listened to the whole constitution.

"A tower," he said. "With a hundred floors. That you go into. To fight things."

"Sometimes," said Mau. "Sometimes you just light lamps. Or ring a bell."

"And my sister's friend went in. Yuka. And caught a—" He stopped. "A boss. With a rope."

"Its ankle," said Yuta. "She caught its ankle."

Taihei was quiet for a long moment.

"I don't think I'd be any use," he said. "In a tower."

Nobody said anything.

"I worked in an office," said Taihei. "For nine years. I can do spreadsheets. I can cook. I can fold laundry so it fits in a drawer." He looked at his hands. "I can't fight a boss with a rope."

"Nobody's asking you to," said Mau.

"I know," said Taihei. "That's what's strange. Nobody's asked me to do anything. Except sit down." He almost laughed. "And then Wakana let me help with the far wall. And Momo let me have the left end of the counter. Mornings." He paused. "I'm just trying to work out what I'm *for*. Here. Everyone here is *for* something."

Rimuru, beside him in his new spot, which was not by the draught, turned his head.

"I asked that," said Rimuru. "When I first came. In my old world, I built a whole nation. I was for everything. I was the one everyone came to." He looked at the stars. "And here, at first, I was a slime on a pillow. And everyone else was for something. And I wasn't." He was quiet. "It took me a long time to work out you don't have to be *for* something. You just have to be *here*."

Taihei looked at him.

"You're the one with the rule," he said. "About the empty room."

"I'm the one with the rule," said Rimuru.

"Wakana said everyone makes a bit. Nobody makes all of it." Taihei looked down at the yard, at the half-built frame at the end of the west wing, pale in the dark. "I'm making the far wall."

"Then you're for the far wall," said Rimuru. "That's enough. For now."

* * *

The fireflies blinked low in the north field. Beyond them, past the lime, past the road, the woods were a darker dark than they used to be. Lower. Flatter. Nobody on the roof looked at them. Everybody on the roof knew they were there.

"I watched you," said Gojo.

He did not say from where. He did not need to. The north end of the lime. Five nights ago.

Mau did not say anything.

"I didn't come out," said Gojo. "I told Yuta not to cross the line." He turned his empty cup in his long fingers. "I didn't think you'd know us."

Mau looked at the dark where the woods had been.

"I don't know if I would have," he said.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Good call," said Mau at last. Quietly. To Gojo. "Not coming."

"I know," said Gojo. "I hated it."

Taihei, by the draught, was very still. He had been in the kitchen on the morning of the porridge. He had moved the chair with arms.

"Is she all right?" he said. "Now?"

"She ate three bowls this morning," said Mau. "She told Momo the honey was wrong."

"That means she's all right," said Rimuru.

"That means she's all right," agreed Mau.

* * *

He did not say the next thing for a long time.

When he said it, he said it to the stars. Not to any of them.

"I'm going to go up faster," he said. "The tower."

Yuta looked at him.

"Not alone," said Mau, before Yuta could say it. "Not stupid. Not more than Shoko lets me. The way we've been doing it. With people. With a little left." He was quiet. "But faster. I stopped for one second on that road. Because I thought she had it." He looked at his hands. "I don't want there to be a second next time. I want to be—" He stopped. He did not say the word. "Enough. So nobody has to have it. So I don't have to breathe."

Nobody told him not to.

Gojo looked at him for a long time, with his bare eyes, in the dark.

"Faster," said Gojo. "Not alone."

"Not alone."

"Then I'm coming more often," said Gojo. He put his cup down. "Someone has to make sure you keep a little left."

* * *

Later, much later, when the cups were empty and the tea-that-was-a-crime was finished and the fireflies in the north field had gone down to two or three, Gojo said, without looking at anyone:

"The festival."

"When the fireflies are gone," said Yuta.

"Nazuna says three more nights," said Rimuru. "Maybe four. She counts them every night." He looked out at the north field. "Then the house lights its own."

Taihei looked at the paper lanterns in the ink tree, pale and unlit.

"Umaru says the fort's going to have a stand," he said. "She's petitioning."

"Of course she is," said Mau.

"She asked me to cook," said Taihei. "At the stand. If Momo says yes." He paused. "Curry. The one she likes. For the whole festival." He went a little red. "I said yes before she finished asking."

"That's very like you," said Mau.

Taihei looked at him.

"Is it?"

"She said you never once told her to leave," said Mau. "In your world. Every night. When she was on the floor in a blanket being terrible." He looked at the stars. "Saying yes before she finishes asking sounds like that."

Taihei did not say anything for a long time.

"She told you that?"

"On a step," said Mau. "In the winter. Before she put the coin in a drawer marked LATER."

Taihei looked down at the dark yard. At the house, where his sister was asleep in the fort or in her room or somewhere, in her sash and her hood, the President.

"I didn't know she noticed," he said quietly. "That I never told her to leave."

"She noticed," said Mau. "She noticed all of it. She just couldn't say it there." He paused. "She can say it here."

* * *

They went down at midnight.

Gojo first, because he always went down first. Then Yuta with the tray. Then Rimuru, complaining about the ladder. Then Taihei, slowly, carefully, a man who had now climbed one ladder onto one roof and was not yet sure about the coming down.

Mau went last.

At the bottom of the ladder, in the dark corridor, Taihei was waiting for him.

"Thank you," he said. "For asking me. To the roof."

"You're welcome," said Mau. "Every seventh day. If you want."

"By the draught."

"By law."

Taihei almost laughed.

"Two older brothers," he said. "She keeps saying it. I keep wondering what it means."

Mau thought about it.

"It means she's got one who knows who she was," he said. "And one who knows who she is." He paused. "And now we both know both."

Taihei looked at him for a long moment in the dark corridor.

Then he put his hand on Mau's shoulder, briefly, awkwardly, the way a man who has been an older brother for a long time puts his hand on a younger one.

"Goodnight," he said.

"Goodnight," said Mau.

Taihei went down the corridor to his room at the end of the west wing, the one that said *Came*, and *Taihei (Onii-chan)*. And beyond it, at the very end, past his door, the frame of the next room stood pale in the dark. Half-built. With a card propped against the chalk line where the door would be.

*For whoever comes.*
