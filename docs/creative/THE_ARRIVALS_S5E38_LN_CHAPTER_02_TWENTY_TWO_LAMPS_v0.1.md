# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 2 — Twenty-Two Lamps

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 2
**Continuity state:** Festival preparations on Day 337. Senku and Wakana finish the lamps (oil signed out by Anko); Shizuka chooses her lantern team (Nano, Hakari, Karane); Taihei starts the curry at three under Momo's conditions; Mau helps with ordinary work, with no powers and no tower; Stark sleeps undisturbed

*When: Day 337, late morning to three in the afternoon. Hot, still, cloudless. Stark has been asleep since lunch, and Fern has put a chair outside his door with a note on it that says, in her hand, NO.*

Senku had twenty-two lamps and needed forty.

He had them laid out in rows on the workshop floor, on a sheet, like patients. Twenty-two finished: glass chimney, tin base, wick, the little brass wheel Wakana had turned on the lathe so that you could raise and lower the flame. Eighteen more in pieces. Bases without chimneys. Chimneys without wicks. A box of wicks Hollis had brought from Ashby, half of them too thick.

"Eighteen," said Senku, standing over them with his hands on his hips. "Before dusk. Ten billion percent doable." He paused. "Eight billion. Seven."

"Six?" said Mau.

"Don't negotiate with me in my own workshop."

Mau sat down on the floor next to the box of wicks. He had come to help because Wakana had asked him at breakfast, quietly, on the way past: *Senku needs hands. Ordinary ones.* It was the kind of asking Mau liked best. Nobody needed his shadow, or his eyes, or the tower. They needed somebody to cut wicks.

"How thin?" he said.

Senku held one up to the light. Then another.

"That one," he said, pointing. "That's right. Half of those are wrong. Cut them down the middle. Lengthways. With the small knife, not the big one."

Mau picked up the small knife.

* * *

He cut wicks for an hour.

It was very satisfying work. You took the thick flat wick, and you laid it on the board, and you ran the small knife down the middle of it, slowly, so that the weave didn't fray, and you ended up with two thin wicks where there had been one fat one. Then you put them on the pile. The pile grew.

Wakana fitted chimneys at the bench. Byakuya sat at the end of the bench with a book, not reading it, watching Senku's hands with an expression Mau had seen on Frieren's face when she watched someone cast a spell she did not know. Senku tested each finished lamp with a taper from the stove, turned the little wheel, watched the flame rise and settle, turned it down again, and set the lamp in the row.

"Twenty-six," said Senku.

"Did you say twenty-six?" said Byakuya.

"I counted twenty-six."

"You counted twenty-five and one you haven't tested."

Senku looked at him. Byakuya turned a page of the book he was not reading.

"Twenty-five," said Senku, "and one I haven't tested."

Mau put his head down over the wicks so that neither of them could see his face.

* * *

At eleven, Anko came with the key to the store.

She did not hand it to anyone. She walked with them to the store at the back of the barn, unlocked the door, stood in the doorway, and watched Wakana roll out the barrel of lamp oil Hollis had brought on his last run. Then she took a stub of pencil out from behind her ear and a page out of her pocket, the old loot register, and wrote it down.

*Oil. One barrel, full. Out: one and a half barrels, measured. Signed: Anko.*

"There's only one barrel," said Wakana.

"Senku's maths said one and a half," said Anko. "I'm writing down Senku's maths. When it doesn't add up, I'll know whose fault it was."

"That's not how a register works," said Senku.

"It's how mine works," said Anko, and put the pencil back behind her ear, and locked the store, and went to bed, because she had been up all night on the round and the festival was not a reason to stop sleeping.

They watched her go.

"I like her very much," said Byakuya, unexpectedly.

"Everyone does," said Wakana. "She'd hate to know."

* * *

In the yard, under the ink tree, Shizuka was folding.

She sat on a blanket in the shade with a stack of rough paper squares from Hollis and folded them, one after another, without stopping. Every lantern came out the same: a little box with no top, perfectly square, perfectly crisp, every crease exact. She did not look at her hands. She looked at the next square.

Nano sat beside her and folded too. Hers came out almost as square. She checked every crease against Shizuka's before she went on.

Hakari and Karane were the other half of the team, because Shizuka had chosen them at breakfast, which nobody had expected, least of all Hakari. Hakari folded like someone wrestling an animal. Her lanterns came out lopsided, enormous, enthusiastic. Karane folded slowly with her tongue between her teeth, and hers came out lopsided in exactly the opposite direction. When they put one of each down side by side, they leaned against each other like a pair of drunks.

Shizuka looked at the two lanterns leaning on each other for a long time.

Then she took the pencil out of the spine of her book and wrote on the little page she kept for talking, and turned it round so they could see.

*Those two go together. In the same branch.*

Hakari read it. Karane read it. Karane went very slightly pink, and Hakari didn't notice, and put both lopsided lanterns on the pile for the same branch with great ceremony.

Nano read it too.

She looked at Shizuka. Shizuka was already folding the next square, and did not look back. But the next lantern she put down, she put very close to one of Nano's, so that the corners touched.

Nano did not say anything. She folded the next one a little more slowly.

* * *

Mau came out at noon with a cup of water for each of them, because Momo had sent him, and stood for a moment at the edge of the shade looking up into the branches of the ink tree.

There were already lanterns up there. Dozens of them, hung on thin strings between the branches, pale and unlit. Somebody had climbed up to hang the high ones. He looked for footholds and found small careful scuffs in the bark, going up and up.

"Who climbed?" he said.

Nano pointed up, without looking.

Frieren was sitting in the fork of the tree, high up, where the trunk divided, with her back against one branch and her feet on another and a book open on her knees. She had been there all morning. She had hung every lantern above the second fork, because nobody else could reach, and she could float. Then she had decided the fork was a good place to read and stayed.

She looked down at him through the leaves.

"Water," said Mau, and held up a cup.

"Bring it up."

"I can't climb."

Frieren considered this, and closed her book on her finger, and came down out of the tree slowly, the way she came down out of anything, as if the ground had to be persuaded to want her. She landed beside him and took the cup and drank it, all of it, and handed it back.

"How many lamps?" she said.

"Twenty-six. Twenty-five and one he hasn't tested."

"Byakuya counted?"

"Byakuya counted."

Frieren almost smiled.

"Go back," she said. "Cut more wicks. I'll be up there."

"Reading."

"Supervising," said Frieren, and went back up the tree.

* * *

At three o'clock exactly, Taihei started the curry.

Mau knew it was three o'clock exactly because Umaru was standing at the end of the long counter with Anko's watch in both hands, borrowed for the afternoon, watching the hand, and when it reached the top she said, "*Now,*" in a voice like a starting pistol, and Taihei put the onions in.

The smell came out through the kitchen door into the yard about four minutes later.

Mau, in the workshop, put down the small knife.

Wakana, at the bench, stopped with a chimney halfway onto a lamp.

Senku sniffed. Then sniffed again. Then put the taper down.

"Is that—"

"Curry," said Mau.

"It's not supposed to be ready until six."

"It isn't ready," said Mau. "It's just started."

They all looked at the door.

Out in the yard, Hakari had stopped folding and was standing up with her nose in the air like a dog. Karane was already holding her by the back of her shirt. Under the ink tree, Shizuka looked up from her paper, toward the kitchen, for the first time all day.

And from the kitchen, faint, came Momo's voice, very level, saying, "*Slightly worse,* I said," and Taihei's voice saying, "It *is* slightly worse, it's the onions, the onions always smell like this," and Umaru's voice saying, "The President rules that the onions are within regulations."

Senku picked up the taper again. He lit the twenty-sixth lamp. He turned the little wheel. The flame came up clean and steady and settled.

"Twenty-six," he said. "Tested."

"Fourteen to go," said Byakuya.

"I know how to subtract."

Mau went back to the wicks. He cut them slowly, so the weave wouldn't fray, and put them on the pile, and listened to the house through the open door. The curry, and the folding, and the argument in the kitchen. Hakari, being made to sit back down. Up in the ink tree, a page turning.

He had cut wicks for three hours, and nobody had needed his shadow once.

It was a very good day.
