# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 3 — One Too

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 3
**Continuity state:** Marin and Wakana's festival afternoon, from Wakana's point of view. The deep blue dress Marin began on Day 310 "in case of a festival" is hers; she has also made Wakana a deep blue summer yukata, paying off "Next time, you're wearing one too" (S4 V22 "The Dress", Day 137); his grandfather's summer festivals and paper lanterns (V29 "Lamp Oil"); an existing couple, no reset

*When: Day 337, a little after four. The last of the forty lamps is in its row on the workshop floor. Wakana has sawdust in his hair and has not noticed.*

The fortieth lamp was done at four.

Senku lit it, turned the little brass wheel, watched the flame climb and settle, and set it in the row. Then he stood back with his hands on his hips and looked at all forty of them on the sheet, like a general looking at an army.

"Forty," he said. "Tested."

"Forty," said Byakuya.

Senku looked at him suspiciously.

"I counted forty," said Byakuya. "You may celebrate."

Senku did not celebrate. He did a small, contained thing with his fist that Wakana would have missed if he had not been looking, and then said, "Right, they need to go up," and went out to find a ladder.

Wakana sat down on the bench.

His hands hurt. Not badly. The good way, the way they hurt after a day at the lathe. He looked at the forty lamps and thought of his grandfather's workshop, the summer festivals, the paper and the bamboo and the smell of glue. He had done the folding, when he was small. His grandfather had done everything else. He had not thought about it for a long time, and then Senku had said *lamps* and he had thought about it all summer.

"Wakana."

Marin was in the doorway.

She was not wearing the dress. She was wearing her work clothes, the old ones with the paint on, and she had something folded over her arm, wrapped in a clean sheet, and her face was doing something he had not seen it do in a long while. She looked nervous.

Marin did not get nervous. Marin talked. Marin talked to everyone, about everything, at a speed that left most people a sentence behind.

"Can you come upstairs?" she said. "For a minute. I want to show you something."

* * *

Their room was above the workshop.

It was small and full. Her sewing things in one corner, his tools in the other. Cloth on the shelves. Sketches pinned to every wall: hers, of people in clothes they did not own yet; his, of joints and frames. The window looked out over the yard, and from it you could see the ink tree with its unlit lanterns, and Frieren up in the fork, reading.

The deep blue dress was hanging on the back of the door.

He had seen it before, in pieces. She had started it the week he chalked the next empty room, and she had been working on it in the evenings since, and she had told him it was *in case of a festival*, in a voice that meant she knew perfectly well there would be one. It was finished now. Deep blue, the colour of the sky an hour after sunset. Long. Simple. A little silver thread at the hem that caught the light from the window.

"It's beautiful," said Wakana.

"That one's mine," said Marin.

She put the thing in the sheet down on the bed. She did not unwrap it. She stood beside it with her hands knotted together in front of her.

"You remember," she said, "the night with the lantern. In the movie room. The dress you made me."

He remembered. He remembered every stitch of it. Pale blue, from her first-week descriptions in the dark. He remembered her face when she saw the slide.

"I said," said Marin, "*next time, you're wearing one too.*"

Wakana went red.

"Marin—"

"Not a dress," said Marin quickly. "Not a—I thought about it. I had a list. I had a very long list. Some of them were very funny." She took a breath. "But then you kept talking about your grandfather. All summer. The lanterns. The folding. The summer festivals at home, and how he let you help, and how you wore—" She stopped. "You told me once. What you wore. When you were small. When you went with him."

She pulled the sheet back.

It was a yukata.

Deep blue, the same blue as her dress, out of the same bolt of cloth. Plain, the way a summer one should be, with a dark sash folded on top of it. He knew it at once. He would have known it anywhere. It was the shape of every summer evening he had ever been happy in, before he came here.

Wakana did not say anything at all.

* * *

He picked it up.

He could not help it. It was the first thing his hands did with anything made of cloth: they checked. They found the seams. He turned the sleeve over and looked at the inside, the way his grandfather had taught him to look at the back of a thing before the front.

The seams were not perfect.

They were nearly perfect. She had sewn it by hand; there was no machine here. Most of the stitches were small and even. But in the left sleeve, near the cuff, there was a place where they wandered, just a little, as if her hand had got tired or her eyes had, late at night. And at the back of the collar there was a place where she had unpicked something and done it again, and you could see the tiny holes of the first attempt if you knew where to look.

He knew where to look.

"I know," said Marin. Her voice was very small. "The sleeve. I know. I did it at two in the morning and I didn't want to unpick it again because I'd already unpicked the collar three times and I thought if I did it again the cloth would—" She stopped. "I can fix it. Before tonight. If you—"

"Don't," said Wakana.

She looked at him.

He was still holding the sleeve, with his thumb over the place where the stitches wandered.

"Don't fix it," he said. His voice did not sound like his voice. "That's—" He stopped. He tried again. "My grandfather used to say, you can always tell a thing that's been made by hand. Because of the one place where the hand got tired. He said a machine never gets tired, so a machine never shows you it cared." He looked at the stitches. "That's the place."

Marin's face did something complicated.

"That's the most Wakana thing anyone's ever said," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle.

* * *

She made him put it on.

Not then. Later, after the lamps were up, after the ink tree was done, after he had washed the sawdust out of his hair because she stood over him at the washstand and made him. She tied the sash herself. She did it wrong the first time, and he showed her, and she did it right the second time, and stood back.

He stood in the middle of their small full room in a deep blue yukata with a slightly crooked left sleeve.

Marin, in the deep blue dress, looked at him.

For once in her life she did not say anything either.

Then she came across the room and straightened his collar, the place she had unpicked three times. And put her hand flat on his chest, over the sash, the way she had put her hand on his back at the east window once, months ago, when they had both been too tired to talk.

"Same blue," she said.

"Same blue," said Wakana.

"So everyone knows."

"Everyone knows already."

"Not that we're together," said Marin. "That we made each other something." She looked up at him. "Everyone thinks we only make things for other people. Since we got here. The house. The rooms. The frames. The lanterns." She smoothed the sash. "I wanted one night where everyone sees we made something for each other."

Wakana looked at her.

At the girl in the blue dress with her hand on his chest, who had sewn him a yukata at two in the morning out of things he had told her about his grandfather without knowing she was listening.

"Can I tell you something?" he said.

"Always."

"When I was small," said Wakana, "at the summer festival, my grandfather used to point at the couples. Walking under the lanterns. In matching blue." He felt his ears go hot. "And I used to think, that's the most embarrassing thing I've ever seen."

Marin laughed. A real laugh, startled out of her.

"And now?"

Wakana looked down at the sleeve. At the place where her hand had got tired.

"Now I think he was pointing so I'd remember," he said.

* * *

When they came down the outside stair into the yard, the lamps were lit.

Not all of them. Senku was still going round with a taper. But enough. The yard was full of small warm lights, on the poles along the paths and on the long table and on the posts of the fort, and the sky above the ink tree was going from blue to deeper blue, and the paper lanterns in the branches were still dark, waiting.

People turned.

Hakari saw them first, from the fort's stand, and said, "*Matching,*" at a volume that carried to the river. Karane pulled her down by the shirt. Umaru, behind the curry, made a note on her slate. Coco's mother, at the end of the long table, smiled at the blue dress, and then at the yukata she had not known about at all, and put her hand over her mouth.

Senku, with his taper, looked at Wakana for a long time.

"Ten billion percent," he said at last, "your grandfather would've cried."

Wakana did not answer. He did not trust himself to.

Marin took his hand. Her fingers found the sleeve, the place at the cuff where the stitches wandered, and rested there.

They walked down into the lights together. Same blue. So everyone would know.
