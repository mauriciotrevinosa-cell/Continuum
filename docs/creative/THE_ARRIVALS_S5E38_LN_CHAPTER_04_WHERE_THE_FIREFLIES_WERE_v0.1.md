# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 4 — Where the Fireflies Were

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 4
**Continuity state:** Dusk on festival night. Nazuna wakes and is the first to see; Shizuka's team lights the paper lanterns in the ink tree (candles, not oil); Coco, Qifrey and the Witch Hat girls send floating ink lights over the yard only where Rimuru approved, as at the S2E22 festival, never past the lime; Mau and Frieren are at ease together in public (R49 groundwork); no threat

*When: Day 337, dusk. The curry has been ready since six. The forty lamps are lit along the paths. The paper lanterns in the ink tree are still dark, because Shizuka said not until Nazuna wakes.*

Nazuna woke an hour before dinner, the way she always did, and came down the stairs from the loft over Anko's bed with her hair in her face and her coat half on, and stopped on the bottom step.

The house was full of light.

Not lamplight through windows, the way it was on an ordinary evening. Light everywhere. On the poles along the paths, small and gold and steady. On the long table, which someone had carried out into the yard. On the posts of the fort's stand, where Umaru stood in her sash behind a pot the size of a barrel, ladling curry, with a sign above her in Yuka's hand that said MINISTRY OF CURRY (OFFICIAL). On the fence by the workshop. On the steps of the clinic, where Maomao sat beside Kusuri's little table of herbs with her arms folded, supervising.

Everyone was outside.

Everyone. She could see them from the step. People she had lived with for two years and people she had lived with for two weeks, all of them, in the warm blue dusk, with plates in their hands, talking. Wakana and Marin, in matching blue. Seiko in the high seat, which someone had carried out too. The five, in a cluster by the ink tree, looking up.

And the ink tree was dark.

All its lanterns hung pale in the branches, dozens of them, unlit. Under it, on the blanket, Shizuka sat with a taper in her hand that she had not used yet. Nano beside her with another. Hakari and Karane behind them, Hakari vibrating.

They were all looking at the stair.

At Nazuna.

Nazuna stood on the bottom step with her coat half on and did not understand for a moment why everyone had gone quiet.

Then Ori came across the yard toward her, fast, in a clean dress, with her red ribbon tied round Milim's ankle and Milim floating behind her on the end of it like a kite.

"We waited," said Ori. "For you. Shizuka said. The fireflies were yours, so the first lights are yours too." She held out her hand. "Come and see."

* * *

Nazuna did not know what to do with her face.

She had never known what to do with her face when people did things for her. She came from a place where nobody did. You went out at night, and you did what you liked, and nobody waited for you to wake up before they lit anything. That was the point of the night. Nobody was waiting.

She took Ori's hand.

Ori led her across the yard, through the people, past the curry and the long table and the herb stall, to the edge of the shade under the ink tree, where it was darkest. Then she let go and stood beside her.

Shizuka looked up at Nazuna from the blanket.

She did not write anything on her little page. She just lifted her taper, a little, as if asking.

Nazuna looked at the dark tree. At all the paper lanterns hanging in it, pale shapes against the deep blue sky, the way the fireflies had hung in the long grass at the start of the summer, before anybody knew they were there.

"Yes," said Nazuna. "Go on."

Shizuka stood up.

She lit the first lantern herself. The low one, on the first branch, where she could reach. She held the taper to the little candle inside, and the candle caught, and the paper glowed, suddenly, warm and gold, a small square light in the dark tree.

Then Nano lit the next one. Then Hakari, with great ceremony, the two lopsided ones leaning together on the same branch. Karane held the taper for her because her hand was shaking. Then the rest of the team went round the low branches with their tapers, and the lanterns came on one by one, gold, gold, gold, climbing the tree.

They could not reach the high ones.

Shizuka looked up at the dark branches above the second fork.

"Frieren," said Ori.

Frieren was already there. She had come from somewhere in the crowd without anybody seeing her, with Mau a step behind. She took the taper out of Shizuka's hand, gently, and looked at Shizuka, and Shizuka nodded, and Frieren rose up into the tree, slowly, the way she rose up into anything, and went round the high branches one by one with the small flame.

From the ground, you could not see her. You could only see the lanterns coming on, higher and higher, in the dark at the top of the tree, until the whole ink tree was lit from the bottom to the top. Dozens of small square golden lights hanging in the leaves, and every leaf lit from below. The light came down through the branches onto everyone standing under it.

The yard went very quiet.

* * *

Nazuna stood under the lit tree and looked up.

It was not the same as the fireflies. The fireflies had moved. They had drifted in long lazy curves, and blinked, on and off, and you could never tell where the next one would be. These did not move. They hung where they had been hung, steady, small, warm. They had been folded by hand all afternoon, and they would burn until the candles burned down.

But they were in the same place.

She thought that, standing there. They were in the same place the fireflies had been in her head all summer. The place where the small lights went.

"It's different," said Ori beside her. Very quietly. "From the fireflies."

"Yes," said Nazuna.

"Is it all right?"

Nazuna looked down at her. At the small, serious face, lit gold from above, waiting.

"It's all right," said Nazuna. "It's better than all right." She paused. "The fireflies were for someone small to find. These are for everyone." She looked at the tree. "That's a different thing. It's allowed to be a different thing."

Ori took her hand again and did not say anything else.

* * *

Then Coco and Qifrey stepped out from the shadow of the workshop.

Coco had her pen. All the Witch Hat girls had their pens, standing in a little row behind her, Agott and Tetia and Richeh, very serious, in their hats. Qifrey stood at the end of the row with his own pen and his hand on Coco's shoulder for a moment, and then took it away, and stepped back, and let her go first.

Rimuru, from the long table, put down his cup and stood up.

He did not say anything. He looked at Coco across the yard, and nodded once. *Over the yard. Not past the lime. Nothing that burns.*

Coco drew.

Mau could not see what. Nobody could. That was the rule: nobody asks how. She turned a little away from the crowd and drew something small in the air, close to her chest, with the pen moving very fast and very precisely. And then she opened her hand.

A light came out of it.

Not a flame. Not a lamp. A small round light, pale silver-gold, the size of a plum, that rose out of her palm and hung in the air in front of her face and then drifted up. Slowly. On its own. It did not flicker. It did not blink.

It drifted out over the yard.

Then Agott drew, and another light rose. Tetia. Richeh. Qifrey, last, and his were a little larger and a little slower than the girls', and went a little higher. And then Coco again, and again, faster now, until there were dozens of them, small silver-gold lights rising from the edge of the workshop and drifting out over the yard in long slow curves.

Like fireflies.

Not quite. They did not blink. But they drifted the way the fireflies had drifted. Up and over the heads of everyone in the yard, and out toward the north field, and then back, turning, as if they had reached the line where Rimuru said *not past* and knew it. Lazy curves in the warm dark. Over the long table. Over the curry. Over the ink tree, lit gold, so that for a while there were two kinds of light up there: the steady square lanterns in the leaves and the drifting silver lights above them.

Nazuna made a sound.

Ori, beside her, looked up at her quickly. Nazuna had her free hand over her mouth.

"They're where the fireflies were," said Ori.

"They're where the fireflies were," said Nazuna, through her hand.

* * *

Mau watched the lights with Frieren beside him.

She had come down out of the tree after the last lantern and found him without looking, the way she found him now. She stood close, her shoulder against his arm, and looked up.

"The last festival," she said. "At the inn. They did this."

"I remember."

"Smaller," said Frieren. "Fewer. Coco was still learning." She watched a silver light drift over the clinic roof and turn back at the edge of the lime. "Qifrey drew most of them. This time she drew most of them."

"You noticed."

"I noticed," said Frieren. "I always notice who's learned." She was quiet for a moment. "It's a thing that happens when you're very old. You stop noticing that time passes, mostly. And then you see someone who couldn't do a thing do it, and you notice all of it at once."

Mau looked at her.

At the lights on her face, gold from the tree and silver from above. At her hand, which had found his without either of them deciding.

Across the yard, at the long table, Seiko was watching them. She did not say anything. She drank her tea and looked at their joined hands and looked extremely pleased.

At the far end of the table, Himmel was watching the lights. Only the lights. He had his chin on his hand and a plate of curry he had not finished, and his face was turned up to the silver drifting over the yard, and he was smiling a little, the way he smiled at things that were beautiful and not his.

He did not look at Mau and Frieren at all.

That was the thing, Mau thought later. He did not look at them at all.
