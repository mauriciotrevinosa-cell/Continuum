# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 8 — Later Than Next Year

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 8
**Continuity state:** After the festival. Cleanup by whoever wants to; Ori asleep on the long bench, carried in; Nazuna goes out to the red stake; Mau and Frieren alone under the ink tree with the last high lanterns; the callback is the S2E22 "Next Year" festival at the inn, where Frieren said "We should do it again next year" and Mau answered "Yes" while privately thinking there would be no next year; this is that again, later than next year; Mau keeps the small white weed-flower; no proposal, no wedding talk

*When: Day 337, past midnight. The low lanterns are out. The high ones, above the second fork, are still burning down. Taihei has finished the pot. Momo inspected it and said nothing, which is the highest mark she gives.*

The festival did not end. It wore out.

That was how Mau thought of it afterwards. Nobody said *that's it*. Nobody rang anything. People just slowly stopped. They drifted to the long table for the last of the pear bread, and then drifted away from it. They sat down on benches and did not get up for a while. Somewhere around midnight, Seiko was helped to her room by Mai and Maki, one on each side, with Turbo Granny under Mai's arm complaining that she had not been consulted about bedtime. The five went in together in a cluster, still arguing about thyme. Kessoku carried their things back to the music room, and Bocchi carried her guitar against her chest the whole way and did not let anyone take it.

Gojo and Shoko had gone at some point. Nobody had seen them go.

Ori fell asleep on the long bench.

She had not meant to. She had been sitting at the end of it, very straight, between Milim and Yuka, watching the high lanterns in the ink tree. She had told Frieren she was going to stay up until the last one went out, because the fireflies had been hers to watch to the end and the lanterns should be too. Then she had leaned against Yuka a little. Then a little more. Then her eyes had closed and stayed closed.

Milim was asleep too, floating a hand's breadth above the bench, held down by the red ribbon round her ankle, which Yuka had looped twice round her own wrist without being asked.

"I'll take them," said Yuka quietly, when Mau came over.

"Both?"

"Milim floats. I just walk." She looked at Ori against her shoulder. "Ori, I can carry. She's lighter than she looks." She paused. "Oppa. Can I tell her tomorrow that she saw the last lantern? Even though she didn't?"

Mau thought about it.

"Tell her she saw the last one that was lit when she was awake," he said. "That's true. She'll like that better."

Yuka nodded. She got up very carefully, with Ori in her arms and Milim floating behind on the end of the ribbon, and went in through the kitchen door. Mau watched her go. Seventeen, carrying a girl who looked sixteen and was much younger than she looked, towing a dragon on a ribbon, past Taihei at the sink, who held the door.

* * *

Nazuna went out to the red stake.

She came past Mau on her way, with her coat on properly now and her hands in her pockets, and stopped.

"Good night," she said.

"It's your night."

"It's everyone's night," said Nazuna. "That was the point." She looked up at the ink tree. "I'm going to sit on the stake for a while. And look at the north field. There's nothing out there now." She shrugged. "It's nice to look at nothing, after."

"After the fireflies?"

"After everybody," said Nazuna, and went off into the dark toward the north field. In a little while he saw her small dark shape sit down on top of Anko's red stake, against the stars.

* * *

Frieren was under the ink tree.

She had not moved since the song. She had stood in the dark under the low branches where the lanterns had been put out, and watched Kessoku, and then watched the yard, and then, when everyone started drifting, she had sat down on Shizuka's blanket with her back against the trunk and stayed.

Mau sat down beside her.

Above them, in the high branches, the last lanterns were still burning. Six or seven of them. Small gold squares very far up, with the dark leaves around them lit from below. The candles were getting short. You could see some of them gutter.

"Ori wanted to stay up for the last one," said Mau.

"I know."

"She fell asleep."

"I know. I watched." Frieren did not take her eyes off the lanterns. "Yuka carried her."

"Yuka asked if she could tell her tomorrow that she saw the last one."

Frieren turned her head and looked at him.

"What did you say?"

"The last one that was lit while she was awake."

Frieren looked at him for a while longer. Then she looked back up at the lanterns.

"That's a good answer," she said. "I'll use it."

* * *

They sat for a long time.

The yard was nearly empty now. Taihei had gone in. Momo had gone in. Somewhere in the house, a door closed, and somebody laughed, and then nothing. The forty lamps along the paths were still burning, because Senku had said he would put them out himself and then had been taken in by Byakuya to sleep. So the yard was lit low and gold, empty, with the long table still out and the benches pushed back and the curry pot upside down on the drying rack by the kitchen door.

Mau took the small white flower out of his pocket.

The weed from the north field. The one that had got in with the yarrow, that wasn't on Kusuri's list, that nobody had agreed on. He had carried it all night. The tiny dried flower was a little crushed now, but it was still there, still white, the size of a fingernail.

Frieren looked at it.

"Do you remember the last festival?" she said.

"At the inn."

"At the inn," said Frieren. "Smaller. Fewer people. Coco was still learning." She paused. "You gave me a bookmark. Wakana helped you make it. From a strip of old cloth."

"You still have it."

"I use it every day."

He knew. He had seen it, every night, in whatever she was reading in the chair he had said no to. A little frayed now at the edges.

"We sat outside," said Frieren. "Afterwards. Like this. And you said you liked it. All of it." She was quiet. "And I said, *we should do it again next year.*"

"I remember."

"You said yes."

"I remember."

Frieren turned and looked at him.

"You didn't think there would be one," she said. "A next year. You thought you wouldn't be here for it. I didn't know that then. I understood it later. I thought back to that night, and I understood that when you said yes, you meant *I hope so*. Or maybe you meant *no*. And said yes so I wouldn't have to know."

Mau did not say anything.

It was true. He remembered the night exactly. The lights at the inn, and Frieren leaning against him, and the word *next year* landing in his chest like a stone. He had not let the pause grow. He had said *yes*, and smiled, and she had accepted the answer.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"I'm not asking you to be sorry." Frieren looked back up at the lanterns. "I'm telling you because it's later than next year now. It's much later. More than one summer. I said next year and it took longer, and there was a great deal in between that I wouldn't have chosen." She paused. "But it came."

Above them, one of the high lanterns guttered and went out.

Frieren watched the place where it had been.

"I keep putting you in things that haven't happened yet," she said. "Maki told me that was information. At the inn, that night, after you went in. She said, *that's information. You're almost there.*" She almost smiled. "I was. It took me a while."

"I didn't know she said that."

"Nobody did. I'm telling you now. Because I'm doing it again." She took the small white flower out of his fingers, very gently, and held it up in the light of the last lanterns. "Next year. I'm putting you in it. The festival. The lanterns. Ori staying up for the last one and falling asleep. Taihei making the curry slightly worse and failing." She turned the flower in her fingers. "And the year after."

"And every summer," said Mau. "Until you're bored."

Frieren looked at him.

"Ori said that," she said.

"I heard."

"She learned it from you."

"I learned it from her," said Mau. "Last night. In the north field."

Frieren laughed. The real one. The one from earlier, under the lanterns, that came out of her without anyone working for it. Quieter this time, because it was past midnight and the house was asleep.

"Yes," said Mau.

"Yes what?"

"Next year." He looked at her. "I'm saying yes. And this time I mean yes."

* * *

She did not say anything.

She leaned against him. Her head on his shoulder, the way she had leaned at the inn. Her hand found his, with the small white flower in it, so that it was held between both their palms.

They watched the lanterns go out.

One at a time. Very slowly. The candles burned down and guttered and went dark, and the high branches of the ink tree went dark with them, one square of gold after another. Neither of them counted. They just watched. When there were two left, Frieren said, very quietly, "This is the last one that's lit while I'm awake," and Mau said, "You're not falling asleep," and she said, "I might," and didn't.

The last lantern went out.

The ink tree was dark. The forty lamps along the paths burned on, low and gold, in the empty yard. Out in the north field, on top of the red stake, Nazuna was a small dark shape against the stars, looking at nothing, because it was nice to look at nothing after everybody.

"I'm keeping the flower," said Frieren.

"It doesn't mean anything. Maomao said."

"It means next year," said Frieren. "We agreed on it. Just now." She closed her hand round it. "That's how meanings start. Kusuri said. Somebody agrees."

They went in.

On the shelf in the east room, beside the grimoire with the four old flowers in it and the fifth from the date, there was a sixth now. It was very small and a little crushed, and it was not from a trial or a date or anything at all. It had got in with the yarrow by accident, and nobody had agreed on it until two people did.
