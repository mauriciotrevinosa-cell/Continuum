# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 9 — Sixty-One Bowls

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 9
**Continuity state:** The morning after the festival, a breather. The yard is cleaned by whoever wakes; Umaru counts the fort stand's bowls (sixty-one); Momo's verdict on Taihei's curry; Yuka tells Ori she saw the last lantern that was lit while she was awake; Senku puts out the forty lamps; no task assigned; no threat

*When: Day 338, mid-morning. Everyone slept late. Momo, for the first time anyone can remember, came down after the bread and found Taihei had already made it.*

Momo came down at eight and stopped in the kitchen doorway.

The bread was in. The oven was hot. The big kettle was on. At the left end of the long counter, Taihei was kneading the second batch with his sleeves rolled up, humming something under his breath, very badly.

He looked up and saw her and stopped humming.

"I didn't—" he said. "I hope that's all right. I woke up. And nobody was down. And I thought, the house is going to wake up hungry, and there's no bread, and—" He looked at the dough under his hands. "It's your recipe. The one on the card by the flour. I didn't change anything."

Momo came into the kitchen. She looked at the oven. She opened it, a crack, and looked at the loaves inside. She closed it. She looked at the dough under Taihei's hands.

"You knead too hard," she said.

"Sorry."

"It's fine for this one. Not for the pear bread." She took an apron off the hook and put it on. "Move up. The left end is yours. The middle is still mine."

Taihei moved up.

They worked side by side without talking for a while. Momo started the porridge. Taihei finished the second batch and shaped it and put it to rise under a cloth. The kitchen got warm and smelled of bread, and outside the window the yard was full of morning light and the wreckage of the festival.

"The curry," said Momo eventually, without looking up from the porridge.

Taihei went still.

"I tried," he said. "I really did try to make it slightly worse. I took out the—there's a thing I put in at the end, a little sugar, and I left it out. On purpose. I thought that would do it."

"It didn't."

"No," said Taihei miserably. "It made it better."

Momo stirred the porridge.

"It was very good," she said.

Taihei looked at her.

"Don't make it too often," said Momo. "Or everyone will stop eating mine."

"I won't."

"Once a week."

"Once a week," said Taihei. His ears had gone red. "That's—thank you."

"That's not a compliment," said Momo. "That's a schedule." But she was almost smiling, and Taihei saw it, and went back to his dough very fast so she wouldn't see that he had.

* * *

Umaru came down at nine with her slate.

She sat at the long table, in her hood and sash, and did not eat anything. She had the slate in front of her and a stub of chalk, and she was counting.

Yuka came down and sat beside her and looked at the slate.

"What is it?"

"The stand," said Umaru. "The Ministry of Curry. Official record." She made a mark. "I kept a tally. Every bowl."

The slate was covered in marks. Little groups of four with a line through them. Rows and rows of them, very neat, with the occasional smudge where somebody had bumped her elbow.

"How many?" said Yuka.

Umaru counted the last row. Then counted it again.

"Sixty-one," she said.

Yuka looked at the marks.

"Sixty-one bowls," she said. "That's—how many people live here?"

"Not sixty-one," said Umaru. "Some people had two." She consulted a separate column. "Hakari had three. Milim had four. Milim's fourth was not authorized." She paused. "Stark had one, but he was asleep for it. Fern fed it to him."

"How do you eat curry asleep?"

"Fern is very determined," said Umaru.

She looked at the slate for a long moment. Then she rubbed out the tally with her sleeve, all of it, the whole careful evening of marks, until the slate was blank and grey. Then she wrote, in the middle of the empty slate, in large letters:

*61.*

And underneath, smaller:

*Taihei (Onii-chan). Very good.*

She propped the slate up at the end of the long table, facing the kitchen door, where Taihei would see it when he came out.

Yuka looked at her.

"You're not going to say it to him?"

Umaru pulled her hood further down.

"The Ministry," she said, "communicates through official channels."

* * *

Ori came down at ten.

She came down slowly, in her day dress, rubbing her eyes, and stopped at the bottom of the stair and looked at the yard through the open door. The long table was still out there. The benches pushed back. The curry pot upside down on the drying rack. The ink tree, with its paper lanterns hanging dark in the branches, every one burned out.

Her face fell.

"I fell asleep," she said.

Yuka got up from the table and came over.

"You did."

"I was going to stay up. For the last one. I *said*." Ori looked at the dark lanterns. "I missed it."

Yuka crouched down in front of her, so their eyes were level.

"You saw the last one," said Yuka.

Ori looked at her.

"The last one that was lit while you were awake," said Yuka. "I was there. I saw you watching it. You watched it until your eyes closed. That one was the last one, for you." She paused. "Oppa said."

Ori thought about this. Very seriously. Yuka could see her turning it over, the way she turned over everything, looking for the place where it wasn't true.

She didn't find one.

"The last one that was lit while I was awake," said Ori slowly.

"That's right."

Ori looked at the ink tree again. At the dark lanterns.

"Frieren stayed up," she said. "For the real last one. Didn't she?"

"I don't know," said Yuka honestly. "I went to bed."

"She did," said Ori, with complete certainty. "She always stays up for the last one." She looked back at Yuka. "That's all right. She can have that one. I had mine."

And she went past Yuka into the kitchen to find out whether there was porridge, and stopped at the end of the long table to read Umaru's slate, and read it out loud, which made Umaru put her face down on the table.

* * *

Senku put out the forty lamps at eleven.

He went round the paths one by one, with Byakuya walking beside him carrying a basket, and turned each little brass wheel down until the flame went out, and lifted each lamp off its post, and put it in the basket. Most of them had burned all night. Some of them had burned dry. He checked each one, and said the number aloud, and Byakuya wrote it down.

"Thirty-eight burned to the end," said Senku when they were done. "Two went out early. Bad wicks." He looked at the two. "Those are the ones I cut. Not Mau. Me. At five o'clock. I was rushing."

"You'll tell him?"

"I'll tell him his were perfect," said Senku. "It's ten billion percent true." He looked at the basket of empty lamps. "Oil?"

"Anko will want to know."

"Anko's going to be insufferable," said Senku. "I said one and a half barrels. We used a little over one." He looked pained. "She wrote down my maths. In ink."

Byakuya looked at him.

"You could tell her you were wrong."

"I wasn't *wrong*. I built in a margin."

"Of half a barrel."

"Of half a barrel," said Senku, with dignity. "In case of a second festival."

Byakuya considered this.

"Is there going to be a second festival?"

Senku looked at the yard. At the dark ink tree, and the long table still out, and the fort's stand with its sign MINISTRY OF CURRY (OFFICIAL) still hanging from a post. At Umaru's slate at the end of the table, facing the kitchen door.

"Ten billion percent," he said. "Next year."

He picked up the basket and went in. Byakuya followed him, and on the way past the slate he stopped and read it, and did not say anything, but he smiled. Behind him, in the kitchen, Taihei came out with a tray of fresh bread and saw the slate and stopped dead in the doorway with the tray in his hands.

He stood there for a long time.

Then he put the tray down very carefully on the end of the long table, beside the slate, and went back into the kitchen without saying anything. Momo, at the stove, did not look round. But she moved the porridge pot over a little, so that there was more room at the left end of the counter.
