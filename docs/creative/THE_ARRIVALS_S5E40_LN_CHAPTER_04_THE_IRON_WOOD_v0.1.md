# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 12 — The Iron Wood

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 12
**Continuity state:** Tenth tower visit, the first beyond the floor 10 transfer point: Mau (owner) with Frieren, Stark and Kaneki. Floor 11 is a forest of iron trees whose blade-leaves fall when the trunks ring; Kaneki hears them loosen; Stark's gate-forged axe; the first rare material (black iron, raw ore in a fallen trunk; not armour yet). Floors now take longer. Mau reaches level 13 (thirty unspent). No extraction offered

*When: Day 340, morning. Three hours agreed outside. Anko has written START FROM: 10 on the board for the first time, and underlined it twice.*

The eleventh floor was a forest, and every tree in it was made of iron.

Mau stood at the top of the stair and looked at it for a long time.

The trunks were dark and rough, the colour of old rust, and went up and up into a grey light that came from nowhere. Their branches spread overhead in a roof so thick you could not see past it. Their leaves were long and narrow and thin as paper, the same dark rust colour, and hung very still, because there was no wind. The ground between the trunks was covered in fallen ones. Thousands of them. Millions. A deep soft-looking bed of rust-dark leaves that went off between the trees in every direction until the grey light swallowed it.

And it was quiet. Completely quiet. No birds. No insects. Nothing moving at all.

"That's wrong," said Stark.

Everyone looked at him.

"A forest isn't quiet," said Stark. "Not like that. Even at night. There's always something." He shifted his grip on the axe. "In my village, the old hunters said if the wood goes quiet, something in it is listening."

Kaneki, beside him, had tilted his head.

He stood very still at the top of the stair with his eyes half closed and his head on one side, the way he stood at the kilns when he heard someone coming across the timber yard before anyone else could.

"It's not quiet," he said. "It's very quiet. That's different."

"What do you hear?" said Frieren.

Kaneki listened.

"The leaves," he said at last. "The ones still on the trees. They're—" He stopped. "They're not attached properly. They're barely holding on. I can hear them. Thousands of them. Like something under tension." He opened his eyes. "Like a held breath."

The notice came up.

*Floor 11 of 100.*

*Objective: reach the far gate.*

Nothing else. No warning. No *beware*.

Mau read it out.

"That's all?" said Stark.

"That's all."

Frieren looked at the bed of fallen leaves. Then up at the roof of leaves still hanging. Then at the nearest trunk, dark and rough, an arm's length from the stair.

She reached out with the end of her staff and tapped it. Lightly. Once.

The trunk rang.

* * *

It rang like a bell. A low, long, iron note that went out through the forest and did not stop. It went from trunk to trunk, picked up, passed on, so that the whole wood began to hum. The leaves overhead trembled.

And above them, all at once, a handful came loose.

They fell. Not drifting, the way leaves fall. Straight down, edge first, like knives. They came down through the grey light in a glittering rain. Kaneki moved before anyone else did. He pulled Stark back by the collar onto the stair. The leaves hit the ground where Stark had been standing and buried themselves, edge down, in the soft bed of fallen ones. Then they stood there, quivering, thin and dark and sharp.

The hum went on for a long time. Then, very slowly, it died away.

Nobody said anything.

Stark looked at the leaves standing up in the ground where his feet had been.

"Don't touch the trees," he said.

"Don't touch the trees," Frieren agreed.

* * *

They went through the forest without touching the trees.

It was very slow. The trunks were close together, and there was no path. Mau went first, because he was the owner, and he went carefully, choosing each step, his eyes behind their filter on the narrow spaces between the trunks. Kaneki walked beside him with his head tilted, listening to the leaves overhead. Every few minutes he said *left* or *wait* or *not that way*, because he could hear a patch of leaves above them that were holding on worse than the others.

Stark came behind with the axe held close against his body, so it would not swing into anything. Frieren came last, floating a little above the ground. Her feet made no sound on the leaves. She watched the roof.

They walked for an hour.

The forest did not change. Trunk after trunk, rust-dark. The bed of leaves. The grey light. The quiet that was not quiet. Twice they came to a place where the trunks grew so close together there was no way through. They had to go back and find another way, slowly, step by step, without touching anything.

"How big is this floor?" said Stark, the second time.

"Bigger," said Mau. "Each one's bigger."

"The arcade was one room."

"The arcade was seven," said Mau. "This is eleven." He looked at the trunks ahead, going on and on into the grey. "Anko said three hours. I think we're going to need them."

* * *

The wolves came in the second hour.

Kaneki heard them first. He stopped, very suddenly, with his hand up.

"Something's walking," he said. "On the leaves. Not us." He listened. "Four. No—five. Heavy. Behind us, and to the left."

Mau looked back.

Between the trunks, in the grey light, something moved. A shape, low to the ground, coming through the fallen leaves without a sound. Then another. They came closer, and he saw them properly. Wolves. Or the shape of wolves. Long-legged and lean, the same rust colour as the trees, as if they had been made out of the same iron. Their eyes were dull red, like the guardian's on ten.

Five of them. Spreading out between the trunks. Coming.

"Don't fight near the trees," said Frieren, very calmly. "If anything hits a trunk—"

"I know," said Mau.

The first wolf came.

It came fast, low, straight at Stark, because Stark was the biggest. And Stark did the thing Mau had seen him do in the red gate, at the snowline, the thing that made Fern go quiet when she talked about it. He did not swing. A swing would hit a trunk. He stepped in, inside the wolf's leap, and brought the axe up short and hard from below. It took the wolf under the jaw and threw it back, and it landed in the leaves and did not get up.

Two more came at once.

Frieren dealt with one. A thin, precise line of light from the end of her staff, straight between two trunks without touching either. The wolf dropped where it stood.

Kaneki dealt with the other.

Mau did not see how. He saw Kaneki move, faster than a man that size should move. Then the wolf was on the ground, and Kaneki was standing over it, breathing a little harder, with his sleeve torn to the elbow. He pulled the torn sleeve down and did not say anything.

The last two hung back between the trunks. Dull red eyes. Watching.

Then one of them lunged, not at any of them, but past them.

At a tree.

Its iron shoulder hit the trunk full on. The trunk rang. A great low note, louder than Frieren's tap, much louder, going out through the whole forest, from trunk to trunk to trunk.

The roof shivered.

"*Down,*" said Kaneki.

* * *

Mau did not think. He did the thing his body knew.

He put up his hand. Infinity. Not much. Not the wide thing he had used in the red gate. A small flat space over the four of them, a roof under the roof, just big enough. The leaves came down like rain. Thousands of them, edge first, glittering. They stopped. A hand's breadth above Frieren's hat, they slowed and slowed and never arrived, and hung there in the air above the four of them in a thin dark sheet.

All around them, outside the small space, the leaves came down and buried themselves in the ground. The two wolves were outside it. They did not get up.

The hum died away.

Mau let the space go, carefully, at the edges first. The sheet of hanging leaves slid off it to the sides and fell harmlessly into the leaf-bed, and stood there, quivering.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Thank you," said Stark.

"Gojo would say it was sloppy," said Mau.

"Gojo isn't here," said Frieren. She looked at the hand he had used. "How much?"

"Not much. A little." He flexed his fingers. "Not the reserve. The other thing."

She nodded, and did not say anything else, and made a note of it with her eyes, the way she did.

* * *

The black iron was in a fallen trunk, near the far gate.

Stark found it. He was looking at the ground, because he had decided that was safer than looking at anything else. He saw where a trunk had fallen, long ago, and split, and lay on its side in the leaf-bed. Inside the split, where the rust had not reached, the iron was not rust-coloured at all. It was black. Deep, dense, faintly shining, like wet stone.

"That's not ordinary iron," said Stark. He crouched beside it. He did not touch it. "I've seen iron. This is something else."

The notice came up, small, at the edge of Mau's vision.

*Material: black iron (raw). Rare.*

"Rare," Mau said.

Stark looked at the split trunk. At the dense black core.

"Can I take some?"

"Don't touch the tree."

"It's fallen. It can't ring." Stark considered it. "Probably."

Frieren tapped the fallen trunk with the end of her staff. Lightly.

It did not ring. It made a dull, dead sound, like a stone.

"It's dead," she said. "It's fallen. The ringing's gone out of it."

So Stark took the axe and very carefully cut out a piece of the black core, about the size of two fists, and wrapped it in his scarf. It was much heavier than it looked. He had to carry it in both arms.

"It's not armour," said Mau. "It's just a rock."

"It's a rock that's going to be armour," said Stark. "Eventually. Wakana and Senku can work out how." He hefted the scarf. "It's a start."

The far gate was just beyond. A tall dark arch between two iron trunks, wider apart than the others. Beyond it, a stair going up.

*Floor 11 cleared.*

*Level 13. Thirty points unspent.*

Mau did not open the window. He would look later. At a table. With pencils.

They went home through the return frame. On the other side, Anko looked at the watch.

"Two hours and fifty minutes," she said. "From ten. For *one* floor." She looked at Stark, carrying a scarf in both arms as if it were a baby. "What's that?"

"A rock," said Stark, "that's going to be armour."

Anko wrote it down. *Rock (armour, eventually). Black iron. Stark.*

Then, after a moment, in the margin: *Don't touch the trees.*
