## Chapter 13 — Not Tonight, Seiko

*When: Day 340, after dinner. The seventh night. Upstairs, the roof hatch has squeaked five times, which means Taihei went up too. Down here, Seiko has put out the crackers.*

The crackers were the same ones they always were.

Plain, from Momo's tin, a little dry, on a chipped plate in the middle of Seiko's table. Nobody ate many of them. That was not what they were for. They were there so that there was something on the table, and so that someone could pick one up and turn it over in her fingers instead of saying the thing she had come to say.

Frieren picked one up.

Seiko, in her chair, watched her do it.

Maki sat on the end of the bed with her back against the wall and her spear leaning in the corner. Mai sat beside her on the floor, knees up, as close as she could get without touching, which was how Mai sat near her sister. Turbo Granny was in her lucky cat on the windowsill, facing the wall, because she had announced at the beginning that she was not participating and would like that noted.

"Noted," Seiko had said, and turned the cat to face the wall herself.

Now Seiko folded her hands on the head of her stick.

"Well," she said. "Who's carrying something?"

* * *

Mai went first. She usually didn't. But tonight she said, quite suddenly, before anyone else could:

"The lights."

Everyone looked at her.

"The floating ones," said Mai. "At the festival. The girl's. Coco's." She was looking at her knees. "I watched them all night. I couldn't stop." She paused. "I make things too. With my technique. One a day, and it costs me. Every time." She turned her hand over on her knee. "And she just—drew them. Dozens. And they floated. And she looked so happy doing it."

She stopped.

Nobody said anything. That was the rule too. You let someone finish.

"I don't know what I'm carrying," said Mai at last. "I think I'm jealous. Of a little girl with a pen." She almost laughed. "That's stupid."

"It isn't," said Maki.

Mai looked at her sister. Maki was not looking at her. She was looking at the wall above Seiko's head.

"You make one thing a day," said Maki, "and it costs you, and you make it anyway. Every day." She paused. "That's not less than dozens. That's different."

Mai did not say anything.

But she put down the cracker she had been turning over, and she did not pick it up again.

Seiko nodded, very slightly, as if something had been put down on the table and could stay there.

* * *

Then Seiko looked at Frieren.

"And you," she said. "You've been holding that cracker for ten minutes."

Frieren looked down at the cracker. It was true.

She put it down.

"The festival," she said. "The last festival. At the inn. A long time ago." She folded her hands on the table. "I said, *we should do it again next year.* And Mau said yes. And he didn't think there would be one."

Maki, on the bed, went very still. She had been there. At the inn. She had collected cups with Frieren afterwards and said *that's information.*

"I found out later," said Frieren. "What he meant. That he said yes so I wouldn't have to know." She looked at the cracker. "And it took much longer than next year. And a great deal happened in between. And then, three nights ago, there was a festival. And we sat under the tree until the last lantern went out." She paused. "And I said it again. Next year. And this time he said yes, and he meant yes."

She stopped.

The room was quiet.

"So what are you carrying?" said Seiko gently.

Frieren thought about it. She thought about it properly, the way she thought about a spell she did not understand yet.

"I think," she said slowly, "I'm carrying how good it was."

Seiko's eyebrows went up.

"I'm not used to it," said Frieren. "Happy things. For me, they go past very fast. A festival. A summer. Ten years. I look up and they're gone, and I didn't notice until afterwards how good they were." She looked at her hands. "This one I noticed. While it was happening. All of it. Ori falling asleep. Bocchi looking at him once. The lanterns going out, one by one. I noticed every single one." Her voice went very quiet. "I'm carrying it because I don't know where to put it. I've never had to put one down before. They've always gone past on their own."

* * *

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

Then Turbo Granny, from the windowsill, facing the wall, said:

"Put it on the shelf with the flowers."

Everyone turned.

"I'm not participating," said Turbo Granny, to the wall. "I want that noted. But I heard about the flowers. The whole house heard about the flowers. Six now, the girl said. One of them's a weed." The cat did not turn round. "Put the festival on the shelf with the weed. That's where you put things you don't know where to put. Everyone knows that."

Seiko looked at the cat for a long moment.

"That," she said, "is the most sensible thing you've said since I put you in there."

"I've said many sensible things," said the cat. "You weren't listening. You were too busy stuffing me into a ceramic animal."

"Noted," said Seiko.

Frieren was looking at the cat.

"With the weed," she said.

"With the weed," said Turbo Granny. "It's got room. It's a very small weed."

Frieren almost smiled.

"Thank you," she said.

"I'm not participating," said the cat.

* * *

Seiko turned back to Frieren. And Maki, on the bed, saw her face change, and sat up a little.

Seiko had a look. Everyone in the house knew the look. It was the look she had worn at the high seat on the night of the festival, watching two people under a lit tree holding hands. The look of a woman who had lived a long time and seen a great many young people. She had decided, very firmly, a long time ago, that she was going to live to see a particular thing, and she could feel it getting close.

"You know," said Seiko, very comfortably, "when the two of you finally—"

"Not tonight, Seiko," said Maki.

Seiko stopped.

She looked at Maki. Maki looked back at her, very level, from the end of the bed.

"Not tonight," said Maki again. Quieter. "She came in carrying something good. Let her put it down good."

Seiko looked at Maki for a long time.

Then she looked at Frieren, who was looking at her with an expression of polite and total incomprehension, as if Seiko had started a sentence in a language Frieren did not quite speak.

Seiko sighed. She picked up a cracker. She bit it, which nobody ever did, and chewed, slowly, and swallowed.

"Fine," she said. "Not tonight."

"Not tonight *what*?" said Frieren.

"Nothing, dear," said Seiko. "An old woman's thought. It'll keep." She brushed crumbs off her lap. "Good things keep. That's the whole point of them."

* * *

They broke up a little after the lamps went down.

Mai went out first, with Maki a step behind her. In the corridor, Maki put her hand on her sister's shoulder for a moment, just a moment, and took it away again. Mai did not look round. But she walked a little slower, so that they went down the stair side by side.

Frieren stayed behind to help Seiko to bed. She did not need to. Seiko could manage. But Frieren stayed, and held the stick while Seiko got her shoes off, and turned the lamp down.

At the door, she stopped.

"Seiko."

"Mm."

"What were you going to say?"

Seiko, in bed, with the lamp down low and her hands folded on the blanket, looked at her for a long time.

"I'll tell you," she said, "at the long table. In front of everybody. When you're both eating something." Her eyes glinted. "That's the only way to say it properly. Ask any grandmother."

Frieren looked at her suspiciously.

"That sounds dangerous."

"It's very dangerous," said Seiko comfortably, and closed her eyes. "Good night, dear. Put it on the shelf. With the weed."

* * *

Frieren went up the stair to the east room.

Above her, through the ceiling, she could hear the roof. Faintly. Five voices, not four. Rimuru complaining about something. Gojo laughing. Taihei saying something she couldn't make out, and then everyone laughing at once, Mau's laugh in the middle of it, the one from the first roof night, long ago, that had been the first laugh on the roof.

She went into the east room. It was dark. The connecting door to Ori's room was open a crack, the way it always was. Ori was asleep. Milim was floating over Ori's bed, held by the red ribbon.

Frieren went to the shelf.

The grimoire, with the four old flowers pressed inside it. The fifth, from the date, beside it. And the sixth. Very small, a little crushed, white, from the north field, got in with the yarrow by accident.

She stood in front of the shelf for a long time.

Then she put her hand flat on the shelf beside the sixth flower. Just for a moment. As if setting something down.

She took her hand away.

It was still there. The festival. How good it had been. She could still feel all of it. But it was on the shelf now, with the weed, where she could look at it whenever she wanted, and she did not have to carry it in her hands.

That was the rule. Nobody leaves the table carrying what they came in with.

She got into bed and lay in the dark and listened to the roof until the five of them came down.
