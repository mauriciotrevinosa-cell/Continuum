# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 17 — The Order of Things

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 17
**Continuity state:** A Fern/Frieren afternoon. Frieren asks Fern for time (the reverse of Fern's "ask for me sometimes", answered on the Sukuna night in V28); they take the unreadable green book from floor 15 to the river bend; Fern's method (order, repetition) finds the first repeated mark; Frieren tells Fern about "next year"; Fern speaks about sharing the ledger's weight without giving it up; no translation achieved, no magic unlocked

*When: Day 344, afternoon. Jinshi has left only two questions in the ledger today. Fern answered them before lunch and has nothing she must do until dinner, which has not happened to her in some weeks.*

Frieren came to find her.

Fern was at the long table with the ledger closed in front of her, which was unusual, and her hands folded on top of it, which was more unusual. She was looking out of the window at the yard. She was not doing anything at all.

She heard Frieren come down the stair. She knew the step. She had known it for most of her life.

"Fern."

Fern turned round.

Frieren was standing at the bottom of the stair with the small green book in one hand. She looked, Fern thought, slightly uncomfortable. As if she had walked into a room and forgotten what she came for, and was trying to look as if she had meant to stop.

"Do you have the afternoon?" said Frieren.

Fern looked at her.

"I have the afternoon," she said slowly. "Why?"

Frieren held up the green book.

"I want to go to the river," she said. "With this. And you." She paused. "Not because I need help. I can't read it, but I don't need help not reading it. I'm very good at not reading things." She looked at the book. "I'd like you to come. For me."

Fern did not say anything for a moment.

It was the same thing, she realised. The same thing she had said to Frieren, a long time ago, in a corridor, when Frieren carried everything alone and would not ask for anything. *Ask for me sometimes. Not for a task. For you.* And Frieren had come to her room, on the night of the truce, and sat on the end of her bed, and asked.

And now here she was again. Asking. In the middle of an ordinary afternoon, with nothing wrong.

"Yes," said Fern. She stood up. "Let me get my hat."

* * *

They went to the river bend on the inside path.

The place Gojo had taken Shoko, the first time. Past the workshop, past the bench Ori had drawn three faces for, past the fork where the path went down to the water. Along the bank under the trees to the bend where the river widened and slowed and went clear over pale stones.

Nobody was there. It was very quiet. The bird that had forgotten the words was trying its call upstream, and giving up halfway, the way it always did.

Frieren sat down on the bank with her back against a tree. Fern sat beside her. Not close. Close enough.

Frieren opened the green book on her knees.

"I've been looking at it for two days," she said. "I've turned every page. Three times." She turned one now. Close writing, small, in rows. A drawing in the margin: a key. "I don't know any of it. Not the script. Not the language. Not what kind of book it is. A history. A diary. A list of groceries." She turned another page. "It could be a list of groceries."

"It isn't," said Fern.

"How do you know?"

"Nobody draws a key in the margin of a list of groceries," said Fern. "Unless they've lost it."

Frieren looked at her.

Then she looked back at the book.

"That's true," she said.

* * *

"In the library," said Fern, "I found the reading room by the order."

"Gojo told me. At dinner."

"The marks at the start of each row," said Fern. "They repeated. Every row began with one, and the next row began with the next one, and the next. I couldn't read them. I didn't need to read them. I just needed to know they were in order." She held out her hand. "May I?"

Frieren gave her the book.

Fern did not turn the pages. She opened it at the very first page and looked at it for a long time. Not reading. Looking at the shapes. Then she turned to the second page and looked at that. Then the third.

"There," she said.

Frieren leaned over.

Fern's finger was resting under a mark at the top of the first page. A small shape, like a hook with a bar across it. Then on the second page, at the top, the same mark. And on the third.

"It's at the top of every page," said Fern. "The same mark. And then, after it—" she moved her finger "—a different mark. A different one on every page. In order." She turned the pages. First, second, third, fourth. "That one. Then that one. Then that one."

Frieren looked at the marks.

"Numbers," she said slowly. "Page numbers. Or—"

"Or dates," said Fern. "The hook is *day*. Or *entry*. Or something. And the mark after it is which one." She turned back to the first page. "If it's dates, it's a diary. If it's a diary, the same words will come up over and over. *I. Today. Went. Ate.*" She looked at the close rows of writing. "Find the ones that come up most often. They'll be the small words. And once you have the small words—"

"The big words have something to hang on," said Frieren.

Fern nodded.

Frieren took the book back. She looked at the hook-with-a-bar at the top of the first page. Then she turned to the second page and found it again. Then she went back to the first page and looked at the first row of writing, very slowly, as if she had never seen it before.

"I've been reading it wrong," she said.

"You've been reading it," said Fern. "That's the wrong way to start a book you can't read. You have to count it first."

Frieren looked at her for a long moment.

"Where did you learn that?"

"From you," said Fern. "You taught me to count the repetitions in a spell before I tried to read it. When I was small. With the rock." She paused. "You don't remember."

"I remember the rock," said Frieren.

"Then you remember."

Frieren looked at the book in her hands.

"I forgot I knew that," she said quietly.

"That's what I'm for," said Fern. And then, because that had come out sounding more like a ledger than she meant: "I mean—that's one of the things. Not what I'm *for*. One of the things I do. Because I want to."

* * *

They sat for a long time.

Frieren went through the book page by page, slowly, counting. Fern sat beside her and watched the river. Every so often Frieren made a small sound and showed Fern a mark, and Fern looked, and said *yes* or *no* or *maybe*. Then Frieren went back to counting.

The bird upstream tried its call and gave up.

"Fern," said Frieren, without looking up.

"Mm."

"At the festival. Under the tree. After the lanterns went out." Frieren turned a page. "I told Mau, *next year*. And he said yes. And this time he meant it."

Fern did not say anything.

"I told the crackers table," said Frieren. "Seiko and Maki and Mai. And the cat." She turned another page. "I wanted to tell you too. Not because you need to know. Because you're—" She stopped. She looked at the river. "Because when something good happens, I think about who I want to tell. And you were the first one I thought of. Before the table. I just didn't know how to start."

Fern looked at her.

At the elf sitting on the river bank with a book she couldn't read open on her knees, telling her about next year as if it were a mark she had just found at the top of a page.

"Mistress Frieren," said Fern.

"Mm."

"I'm very glad."

Frieren nodded. She did not look up from the book. But Fern saw her ears go faintly pink at the tips, and pretended not to.

* * *

"Can I tell you something too?" said Fern, after a while.

"Always."

Fern looked at the water.

"I've been sharing the ledger," she said. "With Jinshi. And Nano, for the sums. Since the trial week. The way the house agreed." She folded her hands in her lap. "I didn't want to. I said I would and I did, but I didn't want to. I thought, if I'm not the one holding all of it, then what am I holding?"

Frieren put the book down on her knees.

"And?"

"And today Jinshi left two questions," said Fern. "Two. Instead of nine. Because he's learning." She paused. "And I answered them before lunch. And then I had nothing to do until dinner. And I sat at the long table with my hands on the closed ledger and I didn't know what to do with an afternoon." She looked at Frieren. "And then you came down the stair and asked me to go to the river."

Frieren looked at her.

"I didn't know," she said. "That you had nothing to do."

"I know you didn't," said Fern. "That's why it was nice."

The river went on over its pale stones.

"I'm still holding the ledger," said Fern. "It's still mine. The budget. The list. Jinshi asks me. Nano checks with me. That didn't change." She looked at her hands. "I'm just not holding *all* of it. And there's room now. For an afternoon at the river. With someone who asked."

Frieren did not say anything for a long time.

Then she picked up the book again and opened it to the first page and put it on her knees between them, half on hers and half on Fern's, so they could both see.

"Count with me," she said. "The hook at the top. I've found it on every page up to forty-one. You do forty-two onward."

Fern looked at the book. Then at Frieren.

Then she leaned in, a little, so that their shoulders touched, and found page forty-two, and the hook at the top of it, and started to count.

They stayed until the light went gold on the water and the bird upstream gave up for the day. They went back for dinner with the book between them. They had not translated a single word. They had found, between them, which marks came up most often on every page, and written them down on the back of one of Fern's ledger slips, in two columns, in two hands.

Small words, probably. Something for the big words to hang on.
