# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 20 — One a Day

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 20
**Continuity state:** Mai and Maki, an afternoon of their own. Mai has used her one construction a day on a bullet every day since she arrived (it costs her); after the crackers table she uses one day's construction on something that is not a bullet, a small flower, and gives it to Maki, who ties it to her spear; Maki wears the pale terrace flower from floor 5 already, and now has two. Mai's private-history arc (DH-057) stays untouched; nothing about Maki's lived world is spoken; no power growth

*When: Day 346, afternoon. Hot. The yard is empty because everyone sensible is inside. Mai is not inside. She has been sitting on the woodpile behind the workshop since lunch with her hands in her lap.*

Mai made one thing a day.

That was the rule of her technique, and it had been the rule since long before she came here. One thing, made out of nothing, out of her own cursed energy. And it cost her. Not a little. Every time. It left her tired in a way sleep did not quite fix, as if she had given away something she would not get back.

Every day since she came, she had made a bullet.

One bullet. For the revolver she kept in her room, in the drawer by the bed. Every morning after breakfast she sat on the edge of the bed and closed her eyes and made it, and opened her eyes, and it was in her palm, small and heavy and warm. She put it in the box with the others. There were a great many in the box now. More than she would ever need. She had never fired one here.

She made them anyway. Because that was what you made. When your technique let you make one thing a day, and you had grown up where she had grown up, you made the thing that might keep you alive tomorrow.

Today she had not made one yet.

She sat on the woodpile behind the workshop with her hands in her lap and thought about Coco's lights.

* * *

Maki found her there at three.

Maki was not looking for her. Maki was going to the timber yard to find a straight length of ash for a new spear shaft, because the old one had a crack in it she did not like. She came round the corner of the workshop with her sleeves rolled up and saw her sister sitting on the woodpile in the full sun, not moving, and stopped.

"You'll burn," said Maki.

"I know."

"Then go inside."

"I'm thinking."

Maki looked at her. Then she came and sat down on the woodpile beside her. Not close. Not far. The way they sat now, after everything. Close enough that if one of them wanted to, she could reach.

"What about?" said Maki.

Mai did not answer for a while.

"I haven't made today's yet," she said at last.

Maki did not say anything. She knew about the bullets. Everyone did, a little. Mai did not hide them. She just did not talk about them.

"I was going to," said Mai. "After breakfast. Like always. And I sat on the bed, and I closed my eyes, and I started." She looked at her hands. "And I thought about the lights. At the festival. Dozens of them. Just floating. And I stopped."

"The crackers table."

"The crackers table," said Mai. "I said I was jealous. Of a little girl with a pen." She almost laughed. "And you said, one a day isn't less than dozens. It's different."

"I did say that."

"I've been thinking about it for six days," said Mai. "What *different* means." She turned her hands over. "I make one thing a day. And every day, for as long as I've been here, I've made the same thing. Because that's what you make." She was quiet. "And I've got a whole box of them now. In the drawer. More than I could ever fire. And I've never fired one. Not here."

Maki looked at the timber yard.

"So I thought," said Mai, "what if, one day, I made something else."

* * *

Maki did not say anything for a long time.

Mai had expected her to. Had expected something. *Don't be stupid. You need the bullets. You never know.* That was what Maki would have said, once. That was what their whole family would have said. You do not waste the one thing. You do not spend your one thing on something that won't keep you alive.

Maki looked at the timber yard for a long time and did not say any of it.

"What would you make?" she said at last.

Mai looked at her.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "That's why I'm sitting on a woodpile." She looked down at her hands. "I've only ever made things that are for something. Bullets. Things you need." She paused. "I don't know how to make something that isn't for anything."

Maki considered this.

Then she reached up and touched the side of her own head, behind her ear. A flower was tucked there. Pale, small, five petals, a little faded now. From the tree on the terrace on the fifth floor of the tower, the day of the first transfer point. Yuka had picked it and brought it home in both hands, and Maki had put it behind her ear and not taken it out since, and it had dried where it was.

"Something like that," said Maki. "Something that's not for anything."

Mai looked at the flower behind her sister's ear.

She had seen it every day for six weeks. She had never asked about it. You did not ask Maki about things behind her ear.

"Yuka gave you that," said Mai.

"Yuka gave me that," said Maki. "On five. It's not for anything." She took her hand down. "I wear it anyway."

* * *

Mai closed her eyes.

She did not decide to. Her hands decided, in her lap. They turned over, palms up. And the thing inside her that made one thing a day, that had made a bullet every morning without asking her, waited, the way it always waited. For her to tell it what.

She did not think *bullet*.

She thought about the flower behind Maki's ear. Five petals. Small. Pale. Something Yuka had picked off a tree on a strange floor of a strange tower, and given to her grandmother. Her grandmother had worn it every day since, though it was not for anything.

She thought: *that. But one that won't fade.*

It cost her. It always cost her. She felt it go out of her, the thing she gave away every morning. She felt the tiredness come in behind it, the way it always came. It was not worse than a bullet. It was not better. It was the same price.

She opened her eyes.

In her palm was a flower.

Small. Five petals. The same shape as the one behind Maki's ear, but not pale. Silver-grey, like the metal of the bullets, but thinner, much thinner, so thin the light came through the petals at the edges. It had a short stem with a tiny loop at the end. It weighed almost nothing.

Mai looked at it.

She had never made anything like it in her life.

* * *

Maki was very still beside her.

Mai held out her hand. She did not say anything. She did not know what to say. She held it out, with the small silver flower in her palm, toward her sister.

Maki looked at it for a long time.

Then she took it. Carefully. Between her finger and thumb, the way you pick up something you are afraid of crushing. She turned it over. The light came through the edges of the petals.

"It won't fade," said Mai. Her voice came out strange. "That one. Yuka's. It's fading. This one won't."

Maki did not say anything.

She got up off the woodpile. She went round the corner of the workshop to where her spear leaned against the wall, the old one with the crack in the shaft. She took a thread from her pocket, the kind she used for binding the grip, and tied the small silver flower to the shaft, just below the blade, by its little loop. She tied it tight. She tested the knot. She tied it again.

Then she picked up the spear and turned it, once, so the flower caught the sun.

She came back and sat down on the woodpile.

"I'll put it on the new shaft," she said. "When I've cut it. Same place."

"It's not for anything," said Mai.

"I know," said Maki. "That's the point." She looked at the flower on the spear. "Now I've got two."

Mai looked at her sister. At Yuka's pale flower behind her ear, fading, and the silver one on her spear, which wouldn't.

She did not say anything else. Neither did Maki.

They sat on the woodpile in the sun until Mai's face started to go pink. Then Maki said *inside*, in the voice that meant it, and Mai went. Maki came with her. She did not go to the timber yard for the ash after all. That could wait until tomorrow.

* * *

That night, in her room, Mai opened the drawer by the bed and looked at the box of bullets.

There were a great many of them. She did not count them. She looked at them for a while, and then closed the drawer.

Tomorrow, she thought, she would probably make a bullet. Probably. You never knew.

But maybe not every day. Not any more.
