# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 28 — It Won't Happen Here

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 28
**Continuity state:** R56, in parallel with R51. In the cleared garden terrace on floor 5, Mau sits under the pale-flowered tree with Gojo, Rimuru and Yuta: no combat, no techniques, no healing power. Yuta and Rimuru already heard the wedding in S4 "The Last Two"; Gojo had not, and Mau chooses now to tell him the shape of it (not every word). The friends say it plainly: that was the Trial, she's alive, everyone is here, and if something like it ever comes, he won't be in it alone, because he already lived it and told them. "It won't happen here" is their conviction, not a guarantee. Mau laughs before they leave

*When: Day 348, early afternoon outside. The garden on five, which has no time of day, only a soft grey light and mist beyond the broken walls. Two hours agreed.*

The garden was exactly as they had left it.

The wide flat terrace, open on all sides, with its low broken walls and nothing beyond them but grey mist. The long grass, waist-high, pale green. The tangles of briar, dark, with long thorns, but quiet now. Nothing moved in them. The thorn-dogs had been gone since the day of the first transfer point. Cleared floors stayed cleared.

And in the middle of it all, the tree. Twisted and old, with dark bark and branches that went out sideways instead of up. Covered, still, in small pale flowers.

The seal in the stone by the stair. The small frame beside it, with home faintly visible.

Nothing else. No notice came up. There was nothing to do here. That was the point.

Mau walked out into the long grass toward the tree. The others came behind him. Nobody said anything.

He sat down under the tree with his back against the trunk.

The bark was rough. The grass came up round him. Above him, through the twisted branches, the grey light came down soft and even. A pale flower let go of a branch and drifted down and landed on his bandaged arm.

He looked at it.

He did not brush it off.

* * *

They sat down too.

Not in a row. Not facing him, like a doctor. Around. Yuta on his left, close, with his knees up and his arms round them. Rimuru on his right, a little further, cross-legged in the grass. Gojo in front of him, but not facing him. Sideways, lying back on his elbows in the long grass with his blindfold off and his bare eyes on the branches overhead, as if he had come here to look at the tree and Mau happened to be under it.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

That was the first thing. Nobody asked. Nobody said *are you all right* or *do you want to talk about it* or *what happened back there*. They sat in the long grass under the twisted tree in the mist, and Mau breathed.

In. Out. The way Frieren had counted.

The frost was not here. He noticed that after a while. There had been frost on the edges of everything in the yard, white at the corners of his eyes, even after the step. Here there was none. The grass was green. The flowers were pale, not white, and there were no blue ones. The light was grey and soft. The sky was mist, not sky. Nothing could open a seam in it.

It was a different place.

He had known it would be. That was why he had asked for it.

His heart came down.

* * *

Gojo pulled up a stem of grass and put it in his mouth.

"This is very boring," he said, to the branches.

Yuta looked at him.

"Satoru."

"I'm just saying. It's very boring. Nothing's happening. No dogs. No knights. No notices." He chewed the grass. "I came here once to fight thorn-dogs and it was very exciting. Now it's just grass." He looked at the tree. "I think it's the most boring place I've ever been."

"You said that about the river," said Rimuru.

"The river had fish," said Gojo. "There's nothing here. Not even a bird that forgets its words." He took the grass out of his mouth and looked at it. "It's perfect."

Mau almost laughed.

Not quite. But almost. Something moved in his chest that was not the closed door. He felt it move and was surprised.

Gojo turned his head, very slightly, and looked at him sideways out of his bare eyes.

Then looked back at the tree, satisfied, as if he had tested something and it had worked.

* * *

"There was a wedding," said Mau.

He did not plan to say it. It came out.

Nobody moved. Yuta, on his left, went still. Rimuru, on his right, went still. Gojo, in the grass, did not move at all. His eyes stayed on the branches.

"In the Trial," said Mau. "The last one. The last scenario." He looked at the pale flower on his bandaged arm. "Yuta knows. Rimuru knows. I told the house, the night I came back. All of it." He paused. "You weren't there, Gojo. You came later. You know the number. You know there were loops. I never told you this one."

"You don't have to," said Gojo, to the branches.

"I know."

"I mean it. You don't have to. I don't need to know what it was to sit in the grass with you."

"I know," said Mau. "I want to."

Gojo was quiet for a moment.

Then he said, "Okay," very simply, and lay back in the grass, and waited.

* * *

Mau told him.

Not all of it. Not every word. He did not tell the end, what the false one had said. That was Frieren's, now. He had given it to her, the night of The Last Two, and it was hers to carry or put down. He told the shape of it.

The clearing. The first one, where he woke. Frost. Flowers everywhere, white and blue, and he had made them all, on the elf route, after years. Everyone coming up through the trees. Because he asked.

The seam in the sky.

The slashes, cutting the air and the trees and the flowers. Moving to get in front of them, the way he always did. And Infinity opening on its own, and not letting him move toward anyone, however hard he tore at it. Watching from inside it. One at a time.

And Frieren. Fighting him, not the sky. Spell after spell into the space around him. Her hand a finger's width from his chest. Going down on one knee in the flowers with her hand still up.

And then it let go.

"And then the voice," said Mau. "From outside. Her real voice. Saying my name." He looked at the flower on his arm. "And then it was over."

He stopped.

The garden was very quiet.

"When Seiko said *marry*," said Mau, "it all came back. The frost. The flowers. I couldn't feel Frieren's hand on my back. I could see it. I couldn't feel it. Like it was a finger's width away." He let out a breath. "I thought it was better. I thought I'd—it *was* better. For months. I've walked through doors and red gates and yesterday I fought a knight. And Seiko said one word at lunch, a happy word, a *joke*, and I couldn't breathe."

* * *

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Yuta, on his left, unwound one arm from round his knees and put his hand on Mau's shoulder. Not heavily. Just there.

"That was the Trial," said Yuta.

Mau looked at him.

"That was the Trial," said Yuta again. Very quietly. "Four hundred and thirty-seven times. And that was the last one. It's over. It's been over for nearly a year." He did not take his hand away. "She's alive. She's at the gate right now, talking to Himmel. Because you told her to. Her hands were cold on yours on the step. I saw. That wasn't a finger's width. That was skin."

Mau did not say anything.

"Everyone's here," said Rimuru, on his right. He was not looking at Mau. He was looking at the mist beyond the broken wall. "All of them. The ones who came up through the trees in the Trial. They're at the long table right now, being loud. Fern's probably made a list about it. Milim's probably asking what *marry* means again." He paused. "Seiko's probably feeling terrible."

"She shouldn't."

"She will anyway," said Rimuru. "She's Seiko." He looked at Mau at last. "Everyone you watched go, in there. Every single one. They're here. They're having lunch."

Mau closed his eyes.

"I know," he said. "I know that. I know it the way you know a sum is right. And then someone says a word and the sum goes away and there's only frost."

"That's not because you haven't healed," said Yuta. "That's because it was real. To you. Four hundred and thirty-seven times real." He tightened his hand on Mau's shoulder. "You don't stop being able to remember a thing because you got better."

* * *

Gojo sat up.

He did it slowly, in the long grass. He sat cross-legged and turned to face Mau properly for the first time since they sat down, with his bare eyes. Mau saw them looking at him, all of him, the way Gojo's eyes always looked. Too clearly.

"Listen," said Gojo.

Mau looked at him.

"I'm going to say something," said Gojo, "and you're going to say I can't know it. And you're going to be right. And I'm going to say it anyway." He held up one hand, flat, between them, the way Mau had held up his hand in The Last Two to show where Frieren's had been. "It won't happen here."

Mau looked at the hand.

"You can't know that," he said.

"No," said Gojo. "I can't. I can't see the future. I can see everything in this garden down to the veins in the leaves and I can't see one minute ahead." He did not lower his hand. "I'm saying it anyway. Because it's true the way things are true when enough people decide they're going to make it true." He paused. "That's what you say. To someone you love. When they're scared of the thing they already lived. You say *it won't happen here*. And then you spend the rest of your life making it not happen."

Mau did not say anything.

"And if something like it comes," said Gojo, "something in the sky, something with slashes, something that wants to take everyone at once, then this time you won't be standing in it alone. You lived it. Four hundred and thirty-seven times. You told the house what it looks like. Every one of us knows what to watch for now, because you told us." He lowered his hand at last. "You won't be inside a space that won't let you move, watching. You'll be standing next to me. And Yuta. And Rimuru. And her." He looked at Mau. "And we'll have already seen it coming. Because you showed us."

"We're saying it too," said Yuta, beside him. "It won't happen here."

"It won't happen here," said Rimuru.

* * *

Mau sat under the tree and looked at the three of them.

At Gojo, cross-legged in the grass with his bare eyes on him. At Yuta, with his hand on his shoulder. At Rimuru, looking back at him now from the other side.

He did not believe it. Not the way you believe a sum. Gojo was right. Nobody could know it.

But he believed *them*. That they meant it. That they would spend whatever came next making it not happen.

That was different. That was something you could lean on.

He breathed in. It went all the way in.

He breathed out.

"Seiko," he said, "is going to feel so bad."

Gojo looked at him.

"She wanted the front row," said Mau. "That's all. She's waited all summer to say it. She saved it from the crackers table. Maki stopped her. *Not tonight, Seiko.* And she waited for us both to be eating, because that's how grandmothers do it." He felt it come up in his chest. The thing that was not the door. "And I choked on cold soup and then fell apart in front of the whole long table."

Gojo's mouth twitched.

"Frieren went *red*," said Mau. "Did you see? All the way up. I've never seen her go that colour. She looked at me like it was my fault."

"It was a little your fault," said Gojo.

"How was it my fault?"

"You were sitting next to her," said Gojo, "looking like a person someone would want to marry. That's very irresponsible at lunch."

And Mau laughed.

It came out of him all at once, in the long grass under the twisted tree, in the mist. Startled out of him, the way it had been startled out of Frieren under the lanterns. Not much. Not long. But real, and all the way from the bottom. Yuta, beside him, started laughing too, very quietly, into his knees. Rimuru put his face in his hands. Gojo lay back in the grass and looked at the branches with an expression of enormous satisfaction, as if he had planned the whole thing.

* * *

They stayed until Ciel said an hour and fifty.

Then Yuta stood up and held out his hand, and Mau took it, and stood. The pale flower slid off his bandaged arm. He caught it before it reached the grass.

He looked at it.

"Can I take one?" he said, to nobody.

"Yuka took one," said Yuta. "For Maki. It's fine."

Mau put the pale flower in his pocket.

They walked back through the long grass to the seal and the small frame, the four of them, not saying very much. At the frame, Mau stopped and looked back once at the twisted tree in the mist.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?" said Gojo. "We sat in some grass."

"For sitting in some grass."

Gojo looked at him for a moment. Then he put his hand on Mau's head and messed up his hair, and went through the frame first.
