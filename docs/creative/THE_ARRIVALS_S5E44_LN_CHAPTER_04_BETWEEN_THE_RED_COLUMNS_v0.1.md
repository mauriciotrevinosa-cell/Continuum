# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 29 — Between the Red Columns

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 29
**Continuity state:** R51, in parallel with R56. Frieren goes to Himmel at the front gate between his vermilion columns, as Mau asked. Himmel's first question is about Mau; he admits Seiko's word reached a grief he thought was folded away; Frieren does not apologise for loving Mau and does not ask Himmel to bless anything; Himmel chooses a little distance, not a departure, and something of his own (it points toward the north-end hour). There is no new confession, no choice between them, no farewell. It echoes S4 "Something Wet" (dawn, the columns) without repeating it

*When: Day 348, early afternoon. The front gate. The vermilion columns throw two short red shadows on the dry road. Himmel is standing between them, facing the road, as he does at dawn, though it is not dawn.*

Himmel heard her coming.

He always heard her coming. Not because she was loud. Frieren was never loud. Because he had spent ten years on a road listening for her step behind him. She wandered off to look at grimoires nobody was selling, and came back, and wandered off again. A man learns the sound of a step he is always waiting for.

He did not turn round.

He stood between his two red columns with his hands behind his back and his cape hanging still in the hot air. He looked at the road going away from the house, white and dry, toward the hills. It was empty. It was nearly always empty. He looked at it anyway.

She stopped beside him.

Not in front. Beside. Between the columns, where there was just room for two.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Is he all right?" said Himmel.

* * *

Frieren looked at the road.

"No," she said. "Not yet. Better than a minute ago. He said to say that one, because it's true." She paused. "He's gone to the tower. To the garden on five. Where nothing is. With Gojo and Rimuru and Yuta. He asked for it."

Himmel nodded slowly.

"Good," he said. "That's good. That he asked."

"He told me to come here."

Himmel was quiet for a moment.

"Did he," he said.

"He saw your face," said Frieren. "At the table. When Seiko said it." She did not look at him. "He was falling apart, and he could barely breathe, and he saw your face anyway. He sees everything. It's one of the things that's hard about him." She paused. "He said, *go to him.*"

Himmel did not say anything for a long time.

The road went on being white and empty.

"That's very like him," he said at last.

"Yes."

"I didn't want him to see." Himmel looked down at his hands, behind his back. He brought them round in front of him and looked at them. "I'm sorry. I didn't laugh. When Seiko said it. Everyone laughed and I couldn't. And then he—and I couldn't do anything. I sat there with a spoon."

"You don't have to be sorry."

"I know I don't have to." He almost smiled. "I'm being sorry anyway. It's a hero's prerogative."

* * *

"I thought I'd put it away," said Himmel.

Frieren did not say anything. She let him finish. That was something she had learned. From Mau. From the crackers table. From the long years on the road with this man, when she did not let anyone finish, and did not know she was not doing it.

"A long time ago," said Himmel. "When I told you. And you said what you said. That you didn't think you loved me like that. That you'd wanted more time with me." He was looking at the road. "And I said that was all right. And I meant it. I still mean it. And then later, here, when it was Mau, I meant it again. I meant it at dawn, here, the morning you leaned on me." He paused. "I folded it up. Very carefully. And put it on a shelf. I thought that was what you did with a thing you weren't going to wear again."

"And?"

"And then Seiko said one word at lunch," said Himmel, "and you went red."

Frieren did not move.

"I've never seen you that colour," said Himmel. Very quietly. Not bitter. Something else. Something that was almost wonder. "In ten years. On the whole road. I did everything. Every terrible joke. Every pose. I stopped at every festival. I made you laugh, sometimes, a little, if I was patient and very silly." He looked at the road. "I never once made you go red. Not like that. Not all the way up."

"Himmel—"

"I'm not asking for anything," said Himmel. "I want to be very clear. I'm not asking you to be sorry. I'm not asking you to choose again. I'm not asking you to stop going red at lunch." He almost laughed. "Please go red at lunch. It suits you. I'm only telling you what happened. The shelf was lower than I thought. That's all."

* * *

Frieren was quiet for a long time.

Then she said, "I'm not going to apologise."

Himmel turned his head and looked at her for the first time.

"For Mau," said Frieren. "For loving him. For going red. For any of it." She was looking at the road, not at him. "I thought about it on the way here. Whether I should. Whether that's what you came out to the gate to wait for, without knowing." She paused. "I'm not going to. It would be a lie. And you'd know it was a lie. You always knew."

Himmel looked at her.

"Good," he said.

She looked at him then.

"I mean it," said Himmel. "Good. If you'd come out here and apologised, I'd have had to stand here and say *no, no, it's fine*, and pretend, and you'd have pretended back, and we'd both have gone in feeling worse." He looked at the road again. "I don't want you to be sorry you love him. I'd think less of you."

"I'm sorry it hurts," said Frieren.

Himmel was quiet.

"That," he said slowly, "I'll take."

* * *

They stood between the columns.

A small wind came along the road from the hills, warm, and moved Himmel's cape a little, and moved Frieren's hair. Then it went on past them toward the house.

"What will you do?" said Frieren.

Himmel thought about it.

"I don't know yet," he said honestly. "I don't want to go anywhere. I want to be clear about that too. I'm not going to put on my cape and stride off down the road into the sunset." He looked at her sideways. "It's a terrible ending. Everyone who does it in a play looks very noble and then has nowhere to sleep."

Frieren made a small sound that was almost a laugh.

"But I think," said Himmel, "I'd like a little distance. Not from the house. From—" He stopped. He looked at the road. "From standing here. Every dawn. Facing the road. Waiting for something to come along it."

"You face the road because that's where heroes face."

"I face the road," said Himmel, "because I spent ten years walking down one, waiting for you to catch up. And then I came here." He was quiet for a moment. "And I've been facing the road ever since. As if something might still come along it. Something I was waiting for." He looked at the empty road. "It already came, Frieren. It came a long time ago. It came, and it went past me, and it went to someone else. And it's happy. And I'm still standing here facing the road every morning like a man waiting for a carriage that left years ago."

Frieren did not say anything.

"I think I'd like to face something else," said Himmel. "For a while. Something that's mine. Not watching for someone. Doing something."

"What?"

"I don't know." He almost smiled. "I'll find something. I'm very resourceful. I'm a hero."

Then he paused.

"Mikasa said something," he said slowly. "At the festival. About her hour. At the north end. Every afternoon. *Someone has to stand there.*" He looked at the road. "She's stood there every day. And she has someone now. Who'd like her to sit in a field with him and not stand anywhere at all."

Frieren looked at him.

"I'll think about it," said Himmel. "Not today. Today I'm going to go and brush my hair. It's been a very long lunch."

* * *

Frieren put her hand on his arm.

Not leaning, this time. She had leaned on him once, at dawn, between these columns, on the morning after the bad night, and that had been its own thing, and she was not going to do it again for a different reason. She put her hand on his arm, on the sleeve, just above the wrist, and left it there.

"Thank you," she said.

"For what?"

"For going in front," said Frieren. "For ten years. On the road. And here." She looked at the road. "I told you that once. You said I'd just done it."

"You had," said Himmel. "You just did it again. Coming out here." He looked down at her hand on his sleeve. "That was going in front, Frieren. Coming to a man with a spoon at the end of a table, because the person you love told you to, when he was the one falling apart." He paused. "You went in front."

Frieren did not say anything.

She took her hand back.

"He'll be home in two hours," she said. "I have to be there. He tells me first. Before anyone."

"Then go," said Himmel. "Be there." He turned back to the road. "I'll be at dinner. I'll pose. Badly. Milim will fall over."

Frieren went.

She went back across the dry ground toward the house, toward the screen, where Anko's board was. She did not look back. She did not need to. She had looked at him, between the columns, every part, and she knew exactly what she was leaving. It was a man who had decided to face something else.

* * *

Himmel stood between his red columns for a long time after she had gone.

He looked at the road. White. Dry. Empty. Going away toward the hills.

Then, very slowly, he turned round.

He stood with his back to the road and looked at the house instead. The long roofs. The ink tree in the yard, with its dark burned-out paper lanterns still hanging in the branches. The fort, with MINISTRY OF CURRY (OFFICIAL) still up on its post. Smoke from the kitchen chimney. Somebody's laundry on a line. Milim, on the roof of the fort, floating, held down by a red ribbon that went down through a window into a room he could not see.

And beyond the house, to the north, at the far end of the settlement, past the timber yard, the place where the white line of lime ran across the field. The north end. Where someone stood every afternoon for an hour, because someone had to.

He looked at that for a long time.

Then he took the brush out of his coat and brushed his hair. Once. Not because it needed it.

Because his hands wanted something to do while he decided.
