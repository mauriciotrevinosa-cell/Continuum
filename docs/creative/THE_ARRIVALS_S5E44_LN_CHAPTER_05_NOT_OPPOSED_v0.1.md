# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 30 — Not Opposed

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 30
**Continuity state:** R57. Mau comes back and tells Frieren first. Seiko apologises before dinner, and Mau tells her to keep the front row. In the east room at night, Frieren tells Mau what she chooses of the Himmel talk, then asks whether he has thought about a wedding. Mau's answer, as the creator directed: he is not opposed, but he has to finish digesting the memory he kept putting away; telling it once (S4 "The Last Two") was not the same as being finished. There is no proposal, no engagement and no refusal. It is a future they want, with pain still being worked through

*When: Day 348, from late afternoon into the night. Two hours and two minutes by Anko's watch. Frieren has been standing at the screen for the last forty of them.*

Frieren was at the screen when they came through.

She was standing exactly where she had stood the day before, in front of Anko's board, with her staff against her shoulder and the stool Anko had brought her standing empty beside her. Mau came through the frame first.

He walked straight to her.

"I'm telling you first," he said.

"Tell me."

"I'm better," said Mau. "Not better than a minute ago. Better." He thought about it, honestly. "My hands are warm. My heart's normal. There's no frost on the edges of anything." He paused. "I told Gojo. About the wedding. The shape of it. Not the end. The end is yours." He paused again. "We sat under the tree. Gojo said it was boring. He said it was perfect. Yuta said that was the Trial. Rimuru said everyone's here. Gojo said it won't happen here, and I said he couldn't know that, and he said he was saying it anyway." He looked at her. "And then I laughed. Because you went red."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment.

"I did not go *red*," she said.

"You went red all the way up."

"I went—" Frieren stopped. Behind Mau, Gojo had come through the frame and was standing very still with an expression of tremendous innocence. "Who told you I went red?"

"I saw you," said Mau. "I was sitting next to you. Choking."

Frieren looked at him. Then at Gojo. Then back at Mau.

Then, very slowly, the tips of her ears went pink.

"That," said Mau, "is the start of it."

Frieren turned round and walked back toward the house without saying anything. But she walked slowly, so he could catch up. When he did, she took his hand without looking at him, and did not let go all the way to the kitchen door.

* * *

Seiko found him before dinner.

She did not send for him. She came herself, slowly, on her stick, along the east corridor to the door of the east room. That was a long way for Seiko. She knocked, which Seiko never did, because Seiko usually announced herself by simply arriving.

Mau opened the door.

Seiko looked up at him. She looked older than she had at lunch. Smaller. The look she had worn in the high seat, enormously pleased with herself, was gone.

"Dear," she said.

"Seiko—"

"No. Let an old woman finish." She held up one hand. "I said it at the table. In front of everyone. When you were eating. Because that's how it's done. That's how it's always been done." She paused. "And I didn't think. I didn't think about what a word like that might mean to someone who'd been where you've been. Who'd seen what you've seen. In that place." She looked at the floor. "I'm sorry, dear. I'm very sorry. I'll never say it again."

Mau looked at her.

At the old woman on her stick in the corridor, who had walked all the way from the high seat to knock on his door.

"Don't," he said.

Seiko looked up.

"Don't never say it again," said Mau. "Keep the front row." He crouched down a little, so his face was level with hers. "You didn't do anything wrong. You said a happy thing, at lunch, the way grandmothers do. It's a good thing to say. It's the best thing anyone's said to me all summer." He paused. "The bad part wasn't yours. The bad part was something that happened to me a long time ago, in a place you never saw. And a good word went in and found it." He looked at her. "That's not your fault. That's just where it was."

Seiko looked at him for a long time.

"You're sure?" she said.

"I'm sure. Keep the front row."

Seiko's mouth worked for a moment.

Then she reached up and patted his cheek, twice, the way she patted Ori's.

"Silly boy," she said. "Of course I'm keeping the front row. I was only being polite." She turned round on her stick. "Dinner. Momo's made something with the last of the pears, and if you don't eat it she'll take it personally."

She went back down the corridor, slowly. At the corner she stopped and called back, without turning round:

"And I'm sitting in the *middle* of the front row. Tell Turbo Granny."

* * *

He did not see Himmel until dinner.

Himmel came to the long table late. His hair had been brushed. Very well brushed. He sat down at his usual place at the far end and helped himself to the pears. He said something to Stark that made Stark laugh, and then he looked down the length of the long table at Mau.

Just for a moment. Across all the noise.

He nodded. Once. The way he nodded at people he had known a long time.

Mau nodded back.

Then Milim asked Himmel to do the pose. The one from the festival. Himmel stood up on the bench and did it, very badly, with his cape held out and his chin up. Milim fell off the bench laughing, and Ori said *Milim* in a voice exactly like Fern's, and Himmel sat back down, satisfied.

That was all. Nothing else. It was enough.

* * *

The east room was dark except for one lamp.

Ori was asleep through the connecting door. Milim was floating over her bed on the red ribbon. Above them, in the loft, Rimuru was a slime on a pillow, and very carefully not making any noise, which meant he was awake.

Frieren sat on the edge of the bed with her hands in her lap.

Mau sat beside her.

"I'll tell you about Himmel," she said. "What I want to. Not all of it. Some of it's his."

"Okay."

"He's all right," said Frieren. "Not *all right*. The kind of all right you are. Better than a minute ago." She looked at her hands. "He said Seiko's word went into him. That he thought he'd put it away and the shelf was lower than he thought. He said he'd never seen me go that colour in ten years." She paused. "He didn't ask for anything. He said if I'd apologised he'd have thought less of me."

"Did you?"

"No," said Frieren. "I said I was sorry it hurts. He said he'd take that." She was quiet for a moment. "He wants a little distance. Not from the house. From standing at the gate every morning facing the road. He said he's been waiting for a carriage that left years ago." She turned her hands over. "He wants to face something else. Something that's his."

Mau did not say anything for a while.

"That's a good thing," he said.

"I think so too."

"I'm glad you went."

"You told me to."

"I know," said Mau. "I'm glad you went anyway."

* * *

The lamp burned low.

Frieren did not move from the edge of the bed. She sat with her hands in her lap and looked at the lamp. Mau could see she was deciding something. The way she decided things. Slowly. Looking at every part.

"Mau," she said.

"Mm."

"Have you thought about it?"

He did not ask what. He knew what.

She was not looking at him. She was looking at the lamp.

"Not because of Seiko," she said. "Not because of the front row. Not because of lunch." She paused. "I've been thinking about it. Since the festival. Since *next year*. I keep putting you in things that haven't happened yet. I told you that, under the tree. And one of the things I keep putting you in is—" She stopped. "That."

She did not say the word. Mau understood why she didn't. He loved her for it.

He thought about it.

Properly. The way she would have. Every part.

The frost. The white and blue flowers he had made. Everyone coming up through the trees. The seam in the sky. Her hand a finger's width from his chest. Going down on one knee in the flowers.

And then the step today. Her cold hands round his. *Here. Not a finger's width. Here.*

"I'm not opposed," he said.

Frieren turned her head and looked at him.

"I'm not," said Mau. "I want to say that first. So you know. I'm not opposed to it. I'd—" He stopped. He tried again. "When I think about it, the part of me that isn't frost wants it. Very much. It's the thing I wanted most in there. In the last one. It's why I made all those flowers." He looked at his hands. "That part's still true. It didn't stop being true because of what happened after."

Frieren did not say anything.

"But," said Mau.

"But," said Frieren.

* * *

"I told it once," said Mau. "The night I came back. The whole house. The Last Two. I told it all. Even the end. You made me." He was quiet for a moment. "And I thought—after that—I thought that was it. That telling it was finishing it. Like when you finally say a thing out loud and it stops being so heavy."

"It wasn't."

"It wasn't," said Mau. "It was lighter. For a long time. It was so much lighter I forgot it was there." He looked at the lamp. "But I didn't put it down. I just—kept it. Folded it up. Put it somewhere in the back and didn't look at it. Every time it came near, I put it further back. And it stayed there, and stayed there, and I thought that meant I'd dealt with it." He let out a breath. "Like Himmel's shelf. I folded it up and put it away and thought that was the same as being done with it."

"And then Seiko said one word."

"And then Seiko said one word." Mau almost laughed. "And it all came out of the back at once, because it had been there the whole time. I'd been keeping it, not processing it. Saving it. The way Shoko saves her cigarette." He looked at Frieren. "Except hers is a good thing to save."

Frieren looked at him for a long time.

"So I'm not opposed," said Mau. "I want you to know that. I'm not saying no. I'm saying not *yet*. Because if we ever—if there's ever a day with flowers, I want to be standing in it. Here. Not inside a space that won't let me move. Not watching." He looked at his hands. "And right now, I think if I stood in a clearing with frost and flowers and everyone coming up through the trees, I'd see the sky. I'd be waiting for the seam." He was quiet. "I don't want to marry you waiting for the sky to open. You deserve somebody who's looking at you."

* * *

Frieren did not say anything for a very long time.

Then she reached over and took the small green book off the shelf, the one from the drowned library. She opened it on her knees, in the low lamplight, to the first page. There was a slip of Fern's ledger paper tucked inside the cover, with two columns of marks on it in two hands. Small words, probably.

"Fern taught me something," she said. "At the river. Except it turned out I taught it to her first, a long time ago, and forgot." She touched the hook at the top of the page. "You don't start a book you can't read by reading it. You count it first. You find the parts that repeat. The small words. And once you have the small words, the big ones have something to hang on."

Mau looked at the book.

"I think it's the same," said Frieren. "With the thing you kept." She did not look at him. "You tried to read it all at once. The night you came back. The whole thing, start to finish, in front of everyone. And you got through it, and it was very brave, and then you shut the book and put it on a shelf because it was too big to read again." She turned a page. "Maybe it needs counting first. Slowly. A little at a time. The frost. The flowers. The trees. Find the parts that keep coming back. Look at them one at a time, in the daylight, with someone sitting next to you." She paused. "Until they're just small words. Things you can hang the rest on."

Mau did not say anything.

"I'll count with you," said Frieren. "If you want. Not tonight. Not all at once. When you're ready." She closed the book. "And when you can stand in a clearing and look at me and not at the sky, then you can ask me. Or I'll ask you. I haven't decided which. I'm very old. I've never done this. I may need to think about it for a hundred years."

Mau laughed.

He could not help it. It came out of him the way it had in the garden. Startled. Real.

"A hundred years?"

"At least," said Frieren. "It's a big decision. Seiko will have to wait."

"Seiko will be furious."

"Seiko," said Frieren, "will be in the front row. In the middle. Whenever it is." She put the green book back on the shelf. "She told me. On her way to dinner. She said to tell Turbo Granny."

* * *

He took the pale flower out of his pocket.

The one from the garden on five. From the twisted tree, that had landed on his bandaged arm while he sat under it and breathed. It was a little crushed. Still pale. Still there.

He held it out.

Frieren looked at it.

"Not on the shelf," she said. "The shelf is for the ones you made. And the weed. We agreed on the weed."

"I know."

"What's this one?"

Mau thought about it.

"It's from the day I couldn't breathe," he said. "And then could."

Frieren took it.

She looked at it for a long time in the low light. Then she reached for the green book again, and opened it to the first page, to the hook at the top. She laid the pale flower there, flat, and closed the book on it, very gently, so it would press.

"Counting," she said.

She put the book back on the shelf. Then she turned the lamp down until it went out, and lay down in the dark, and put her cold foot against his shin. Not because it was the reset hour. It wasn't. Just because.

"One," she said, into the pillow. "Still here."

"Still here," said Mau.

Above them, in the loft, very quietly, a slime that was supposed to be asleep let out a long breath. It had been holding it for some time.
