# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 35 — A Curve That Goes Two Ways

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 35
**Continuity state:** The atelier shows a result and changes an idea (the V30 slot in the old map). Coco's mother has practised the two-way curve she could not do on Day 303 (V29 "Straight Lines"), and draws it. Agott turns the mother's sewing principle ("look at where the seam is going, not at the needle") into a new warm-up: the girls sew a seam before they draw. Plain pencil and thread only; the ink stays private; no new glyph, no rescue. Mau is invited to witness

*When: Day 351, late afternoon. The Witch Hat room, window open. Forty-eight days since Coco's mother drew her first straight line. Agott has asked Mau to come, which Agott has never done before.*

Agott found him in the yard after the second wall.

She did not ask him. Agott did not ask. She stood in front of him with her arms folded and her hat pulled down and said, "Five o'clock. The atelier. Coco's mother has something to show." She paused. "Qifrey said you should see it. Because you were there for the first one."

Then she walked away before he could answer, because Agott did not wait for answers either.

Mau looked at Umaru, who had come out of the long room behind him with her empty milk glass.

"Agott invited me," he said.

Umaru looked at Agott's retreating back for a long moment.

"The Ministry," she said, "will note that in the record."

* * *

The Witch Hat room was the way it had been on the day of the straight lines.

The window open. The smell of paper, and faintly, under it, of ink, though there was no ink on the table. Agott's alcove. Tetia's bed, with Tetia on it. Richeh asleep against the wall, which was still normal. Qifrey by the door, the way he always stood by doors.

And at the table, Coco and her mother, side by side.

There was a stack of rough paper in front of them. Pencils, sharpened. The small wooden ruler pushed to one side, because nobody got the ruler. And between them, on the table, a piece of cloth. Plain, pale, from Marin's offcuts. A needle stuck through one corner of it, threaded.

Coco looked up when Mau came in. Her face did the quick pleased thing, and then went serious.

"You came," she said.

"Agott said five."

"Agott never invites anyone," said Coco. "Ever. She invited you." She looked at her mother. "Mama's been practising. Every evening. Since the day you drew the worm."

"It was a very honest worm," said Mau.

"It was," Coco agreed. "It was honestly not a curve."

Her mother laughed, softly, and Mau smiled at her, and she smiled back.

* * *

"The curve that goes two ways," said Coco.

She drew one herself, first, to show him. A long smooth line that started in one corner of the page, swept up, turned, and swept down again the other way toward the far corner, in one stroke, without stopping. Like a river bending. Like the shape of a sleeve from the shoulder to the wrist.

"That one," said Coco. "On the first day, Mama could do straight. Perfectly. And a curve that went one way, nearly as good as mine." She looked at the line she had drawn. "But not this one. Hers kept going only one way. Like it didn't know how to turn round."

Her mother looked at the line.

"It's the turn," she said. Quietly. "The middle. Where it stops going one way and starts going the other. My hand didn't know where to look." She picked up a pencil. "In a seam, you look at where it's going. That's all. You don't look at the needle. You look at the end." She turned the pencil in her fingers. "But this one has two ends. And you can't look at both."

"So what did you do?" said Mau.

Coco's mother looked at the piece of cloth on the table.

"I sewed it," she said.

* * *

She picked up the cloth.

She took the needle out of the corner. She held the cloth flat on the table with one hand and the needle with the other, between her fingertips, lightly, not gripping. And she sewed. A running stitch, small and even, starting at one corner of the cloth.

She did not look at the needle.

She looked at where the seam was going. Up, along the cloth. Then, halfway, she did something Mau did not quite see. A small movement of her eyes. Not her hand. Her eyes. They went from where the seam was going to where it was *going to go next*, the other way, before her hand got there.

The seam turned.

It went up, and turned, and came down the other way toward the far corner. A long smooth line of tiny even stitches, like a river bending. Exactly the shape of the curve Coco had drawn.

She stopped. She held the cloth up.

"You look at the turn before you get there," she said. "Not when you're in it. Before. While you're still going the first way. You look ahead to where it's going to change." She put the cloth down. "Then your hand already knows when it gets there."

Nobody said anything.

Then she picked up the pencil, and pulled a sheet of paper toward her, and drew.

* * *

One stroke.

From the corner, up. Her pencil moving lightly, held like a needle. Her eyes not on the pencil. On the page ahead of it. And then, halfway, before the line got there, Mau saw her eyes move to the turn. To where it was going to change.

The line turned.

It swept up, and turned, and swept down the other way toward the far corner, smooth and even and whole. A curve that went two ways.

She lifted the pencil.

It was not as beautiful as Coco's. Mau could see that. It was a little stiffer at the turn. A little careful. Like a seam sewn by someone who has sewn ten thousand seams and is doing this one slowly, on purpose, so it will be right.

But it turned.

"Mama," said Coco. Very quietly.

Her mother looked at the line.

"It took forty-eight days," she said. "I counted." She almost laughed. "Every evening. On the end of your bed. With the lamp. Tetia watched me fail."

"Every evening," said Tetia from her bed, cheerfully. "Some of them were very bad."

"Some of them were very bad," Coco's mother agreed.

* * *

Agott came out of her alcove.

She did not say anything. She walked to the table and stood behind Coco's mother's chair and looked at the line over her shoulder, with her arms folded and her face severe. The way she had looked at the first straight line, forty-eight days ago.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she looked at the cloth. At the seam that went two ways.

"Do it again," said Agott. "The cloth. Slowly."

Coco's mother looked up at her. Then she picked up the cloth and the needle and sewed again, slowly, along the first seam, a second row of tiny stitches beside it. Up, and the eyes going ahead to the turn, and the turn, and down.

Agott watched every stitch.

When it was done, she unfolded her arms.

"We're doing that," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Before the lines," said Agott. She was looking at Qifrey, by the door. "Every morning. Before we draw. We sew a seam first. A curve that goes two ways. On cloth, with thread. So we learn to look at the turn before we get there." She pointed at the cloth. "Not with the pencil. The pencil lets you cheat. You can go back and fix it. The thread doesn't. Once the stitch is in, it's in." She folded her arms again. "That's the same as ink."

The room was very quiet.

Qifrey, by the door, did not say anything for a long moment.

Then he came to the table. He looked at the cloth. At the line on the paper. At Coco's mother, in the chair, with the needle still between her fingertips.

"Will you teach them?" he said. "The seam. Every morning."

Coco's mother looked at him.

Then at her daughter beside her. At Agott, standing behind her chair with her arms folded. At Tetia on her bed, and Richeh, who had woken up at some point and was watching with her chin on her knees.

"I can teach a seam," she said slowly. "That's all I know how to teach. I don't know any magic."

"It isn't magic," said Qifrey. "That's why it's important." He paused. "The most important thing, and the most boring. Coco told you."

"Both of those are true," said Coco, very quietly, in exactly his voice.

Qifrey looked at her. Then at the line her mother had drawn.

And then, for the first time Mau had ever seen, Qifrey smiled the whole way.

* * *

Afterwards, when the girls had gone down to dinner, Coco stayed behind.

She sat at the table beside her mother with the cloth between them, the seam that went two ways. She traced it with her fingertip, the whole curve, up and round and down.

"Mama," she said.

"Mm."

"When I was little," said Coco. "Before. Before the circle I shouldn't have drawn. You used to sew in the evenings. By the lamp. And I used to watch." She traced the seam again. "I didn't know you were drawing."

Her mother did not say anything for a moment.

Then she put her hand over Coco's, on the cloth, over the curve.

"I didn't know either," she said.

Mau went out quietly. He did not think they noticed. At the door he passed Qifrey. Qifrey was standing there the way he always stood by doors, looking back into the room at the two of them at the table, with the evening light gold through the open window.

He did not say anything to Mau. He did not need to.

On the stair down, Mau met Agott coming back up. She had forgotten something. She stopped two steps above him and looked down at him with her arms folded.

"You came," she said.

"You invited me."

"Don't tell anyone," said Agott, and went on up.
