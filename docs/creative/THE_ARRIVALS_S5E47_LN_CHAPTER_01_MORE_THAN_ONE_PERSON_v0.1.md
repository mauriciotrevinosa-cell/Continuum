*When: Day 354, afternoon into dusk. The seventh night. Mau's reserve has refilled enough to feel, not enough to use. He has his coat on, though it is warm, because of what is in the inside pocket on the left.*

The coin was where it had always been.

In the inside pocket of his coat, on the left, against his ribs. He had put it there on the tenth night, on the bridge, when every stayer felt one appear in their palm at sunset. It had stayed there through the winter and the ugly days and the snow, and the night of the bell. Through the door, and the red gate, and nineteen floors of tower, and a red knight on twenty. Small and warm. He felt it every day, the way you feel a stone in your shoe.

Beside it, since the long table in the winter, a bullet. Mai's. Cold.

Warm coin. Cold bullet. He had stopped noticing them as two things a long time ago. They were just what was in the pocket.

He took the coin out on the afternoon of the seventh night, sitting on the back step, and looked at it.

* * *

He had not decided anything.

That was what he told himself, sitting on the step with the coin warm in his palm. He had not decided. He was looking at it. He looked at it sometimes. That was all.

He was lying, and he knew he was lying.

He had decided on twenty. Or before. On the night of the honeysuckle. When Kaneki put his hand in Kusuri's basket at the festival and drew two little bundles, stuck together where the stems had caught, that meant *the bond of love*. He had put them both in his breast pocket and walked off to the kilns without looking back. Mau had watched him go and felt the coin in his own pocket, warm against his ribs, and thought: *two*.

Or before that. A long time before that. Under the ink tree in the winter, the night he found out there was nobody before the clearing. Frieren had closed his fingers over the coin with her own and said *to keep, or to give*.

*Someday, maybe, to someone who has more than one person on the other side of the clearing, and only one coin.*

She had not said a name. Everyone knew. Nobody said.

He had carried it for two hundred and fifty days since then. Waiting for someday.

* * *

Frieren found him on the step.

She did not ask what he was doing. She saw the coin in his palm. She sat down beside him, close, her shoulder against his. She looked at it for a long time.

"You've decided," she said.

"I'm telling you first."

"I know you are." She did not look away from the coin. "Tell me."

"Kaneki," said Mau.

Frieren did not say anything.

"He has one," said Mau. "He needs two. Everyone knows. Nobody says." He turned the coin over. "He's had his for nearly a year. And he's never used it. Because he won't choose. Because he shouldn't have to choose." He looked at the coin. "And I've had this one for nearly a year. And I've never used it. Because there's nobody on the other side of my clearing."

"Everyone you'd call is already here," said Frieren quietly. Her own words. Back to him.

"Everyone I'd call is already here." He closed his hand round the coin. "So it's been sitting in my pocket. Waiting. And there's a man at the kilns every night making two cups of coffee and only drinking one."

Frieren was quiet for a long time.

"You said *to someone who has more than one person on the other side,*" said Mau. "Under the tree. In the winter."

"I remember."

"You were thinking of him."

"I was thinking of him," said Frieren. "Everyone was thinking of him. Nobody said it, because it wasn't anyone's to say. It was yours." She turned her head and looked at him. "It's still yours. Even now. Even giving it."

"I know."

"Are you sure?"

He looked at his closed hand.

"Yes," he said.

Frieren put her hand over his closed fist. Cold, the way her hands always were.

"Then give it," she said. "Before you start thinking of reasons not to. You're very good at reasons not to."

* * *

He went at dusk.

Kaneki slept in the day. He would be awake now, getting up, before the night shift. Mau did not go to his room. He went to the kilns.

The kilns were at the far edge of the timber yard. Three low humps of earth and turf with smoke coming out of the vents, and the bark shelter beside them, and the stack of Wakana's useless twisted oak behind it, half of it gone now into the middle kiln, burning slow and dense for Senku's furnace. The small pot on the flat stone at the edge of the fire. Two cups on the shelf in the shelter. Upside down.

Kaneki was there already. Early. He was raking the embers in the mouth of the middle kiln with his sleeves pushed up.

He heard Mau coming across the timber yard. He did not turn round. He reached up and took down one of the cups and turned it over and poured. Black. Thick. Bitter. Steaming. He held it out behind him.

Mau took it.

He did not drink it. He sat down on the bench in the bark shelter with the cup in both hands, the way Eren sat there, and looked at the fire.

Kaneki raked the embers.

They did not say anything for a long time. That was how it was, at the kilns. Mau had sat here before. Not often. A few nights in the winter, after the bell, when he could not sleep and could not go up on the roof. Kaneki had poured him a cup each time and not said anything, and that had been enough.

He took the coin out of his pocket.

He held it in his open palm, in the red light of the kilns.

Kaneki stopped raking.

* * *

He did not turn round for a long moment.

Then he put the rake down against the kiln, very carefully, the way you put down something you might need again. He turned round and looked at Mau sitting on the bench. At the cup in one of his hands. At the small warm coin in the other, open, in the red light.

Kaneki did not move.

"No," he said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You don't have to," said Kaneki. "No."

Mau looked at him.

"It's yours," said Kaneki. His voice was very even. Very quiet. "The Goddess gave it to you. Because you couldn't bring anyone. She gave you something for the long run. For *you*." He looked at the coin. "You don't give that away. Not to me. Not to anyone. You keep it. For when you need it."

"I don't need it."

"You don't know that. Nobody knows that. Something might happen. Someone might—" Kaneki stopped. "You might need it. Someday. And it'll be in my—it'll be gone."

"Kaneki."

"*No*."

* * *

Mau did not say anything for a while.

He drank some of the coffee. It was terrible. It was the worst coffee he had ever had, the way it always was. Kaneki made it like that on purpose, so there was one thing his hands did that he knew. One thing in the whole day that was his.

He put the cup down on the bench.

"You've got two cups," he said.

Kaneki did not answer.

"On the shelf. Every night. You pour two and drink one." Mau looked at the shelf. At the second cup, upside down, next to where the first one had been. "I don't know whose the second one is. You never said, and nobody asked. But everyone knows there's a second cup."

"That's—"

"And you've got one coin," said Mau. "And you need two. Everyone knows that too. Frieren said it, under the ink tree, in the winter, without saying your name. *Someone who has more than one person on the other side, and only one coin.*" He looked at Kaneki. "You've never used yours. You'd have to choose. And you won't."

Kaneki was very still.

"So I'm here," said Mau. "So you don't have to."

* * *

Kaneki sat down.

Not on the bench. On the ground, by the kiln, with his back against the warm earth of it and his knees up and his arms round them. He looked at the fire. He did not look at Mau.

"Why," he said.

Mau thought about it.

He had thought about it for two hundred and fifty days. He had thought about it on the step that afternoon with Frieren. He thought about it now, properly, the way she would have, every part.

"Because there's nobody on the other side of my clearing," he said. "I woke up next to Frieren with nothing. No name. No world. Nobody. And the Goddess gave me a coin anyway, because she couldn't give me a person." He looked at the coin in his palm. "And I carried it. All winter. And under the ink tree I found out there was nobody to call. And I thought it would hurt more. And it didn't. Because everyone I'd call is already here."

Kaneki did not say anything.

"Everyone except yours," said Mau.

The kilns breathed.

"I don't have a wife," said Mau. "I don't have a daughter. I don't have anyone on the other side. And you have two people. And one coin." He held the coin out, across the shelter, in the red light. "So I'm giving you mine. So you don't have to choose. Because nobody should have to choose which one of the two people they love gets to come."

* * *

Kaneki looked at the coin for a very long time.

He did not reach for it.

"What if they don't want to," he said.

His voice was very quiet. Not even. Something under it that Mau had never heard in Kaneki's voice before.

"The petition," said Kaneki. "It can't override what they want. The Goddess said. If I ask for Touka, and she doesn't want to come—" He stopped. "I left. In my world. I didn't mean to, but I left. One day I was there and the next I was here. And she doesn't know what happened. She doesn't know if I'm dead, or if I walked away, or—" His voice went. He waited. It came back. "She's been on her own with Ichika for however long it's been there. Raising her. Alone. Thinking I—"

He stopped.

"What if she doesn't want to," he said again. "What if she's angry. What if she's moved on. What if Ichika doesn't remember me." He looked at the fire. "She was so small. What if I ask, and the coin goes cold in my hand, and I find out."

Mau did not say anything for a long moment.

"Then you'll know," he said. "Instead of wondering every night at eleven, with two cups." He paused. "And if it goes cold, it goes cold. And you're still here. And the house is still here. And Eren will still come to the kilns. And I'll still sit on this bench and drink your terrible coffee."

Kaneki looked at him.

"But I don't think it'll go cold," said Mau. "Rem was afraid of that. With Ram. She knocked on my door after dark, the night before. She said she was afraid the coin would go cold." He looked at the coin. "I told her, *that's not someone who's going to make a coin go cold.*"

"You don't know Touka."

"No," said Mau. "But I know you. You've poured a second cup every night for nearly a year for someone who isn't here." He held out the coin. "Nobody does that for someone who'd make a coin go cold."

* * *

Kaneki did not move for a long time.

The fire burned low in the mouth of the middle kiln. The smoke went up out of the vents into the darkening sky. Somewhere behind them, across the timber yard, the roof hatch squeaked, and squeaked again. Taihei and Rimuru, going up. The seventh night.

Then Kaneki reached out.

He took the coin from Mau's palm. Slowly. The way you take something you are afraid you will drop. He held it in his own open palm, in the red light, and looked at it.

"It's warm," he said.

"It's always warm."

Kaneki closed his hand round it.

He did not say *thank you*. Mau had not thought he would. He sat on the ground with his back against the kiln and his fist closed round the coin, and looked at the fire, and did not say anything at all. That was the whole of it.

After a long time, he reached into his own breast pocket. He took out his own coin. The one he had carried since the tenth night, on the bridge. And the two little bundles of honeysuckle from the festival, still stuck together where the stems had caught, a little crushed now.

He looked at all of it in his open palm. Two coins. Two bundles.

"Fern first," he said. Very quietly. "That's the rule. You tell Fern first."

"That's the rule."

"Tomorrow," said Kaneki. "I'll tell her tomorrow. In the morning. Before I sleep." He closed his hand. "And then—"

He did not finish.

Mau got up off the bench. He picked up his cup. He finished the coffee, every drop, the way Eren always did. He rinsed the cup at the water barrel and put it back on the shelf, upside down, next to the other one.

Then he went across the timber yard toward the house, toward the ladder and the hatch and the roof. He did not look back. Kaneki stayed by the kiln with his back against the warm earth and his fist closed round two coins, looking at the fire.

On the roof that night, Mau did not say anything about it. Nobody asked. But when Gojo came up twenty minutes late and sat down, he looked at Mau for a long moment with his bare eyes. Then at the empty place on the left side of Mau's coat, where something small and warm had always been.

He did not say anything either. He handed Mau a cup.
