# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 40 — Fern First

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 40
**Continuity state:** Kaneki tells Fern first, the house custom for every coin. Fern's practical questions (room, food, the register, who waits); the next room at the end of the west wing (chalked Day 310, far wall by Taihei) becomes the Kaneki family's, and Wakana adds a small bed; Maomao confirms Kaneki's existing food arrangement can extend, "not elegant"; Momo bakes pear bread FOR TOMORROW (OR WHENEVER); Rimuru chalks a new empty room. No fuss, as with every coin

*When: Day 355, early morning, after Kaneki's shift and before he sleeps. Fern is at the long table with the ledger, alone, the way she is every morning before Jinshi's questions. Kaneki has not slept.*

Fern heard him come in from the yard.

She knew the step. Not as well as Frieren's. She had known Frieren's all her life. But she knew everyone's, more or less, by now. Kaneki walked quietly, for a man his size, the way he did everything. He came in through the kitchen door at the end of his shift with lime on his boots and smoke in his clothes, the way he always did.

He did not go up to bed.

He came to the long table and stood at the end of it, across from her, and did not sit down.

Fern looked up from the ledger.

She looked at his face. Then at his right hand, closed, at his side.

She put her pen down.

"Sit down," she said.

* * *

He sat down.

He put his closed hand on the table between them. He opened it.

Two coins. Small, warm, no named metal. Side by side on his palm. And beside them, crushed a little, two dried bundles of honeysuckle tied with Kusuri's thread, still stuck together where the stems had caught.

Fern looked at the coins for a long time.

"Two," she said.

"Mau gave me his," said Kaneki. "Last night. At the kilns." His voice was very even. "I said no. He gave it to me anyway."

Fern did not say anything for a moment.

"I know," she said. "Frieren told me. Last night, after the roof. She came to my room." She looked at the coins. "She said he decided on the step, and told her first, and went to you before he could think of reasons not to."

Kaneki almost smiled.

"He's very good at reasons not to."

"He's very good at reasons not to," Fern agreed. She picked up her pen again. She did not write anything yet. "Tell me."

Kaneki looked at the two coins on his palm.

"Touka," he said. "My wife." He said the name the way you set down something fragile. "And Ichika. Our daughter." He paused. "I'd like to bring them. Both. Together. Tonight. At eleven. At the kilns."

Fern wrote it down.

*Day 355. Kaneki. Two coins (one his, one Mau's, given). Touka (wife). Ichika (daughter). Tonight, at eleven. The kilns.*

She looked at what she had written for a long moment. Then she looked up.

"Thank you for telling me first," she said.

"That's the rule."

"That's the rule," said Fern. "Thank you anyway."

* * *

Then she asked the questions.

She always asked the questions. Nijika had told her first, and Rem, and Yuta, and Maki, and Umaru. Every one of them had sat across from her at this table and she had asked the questions. Not to stop anyone. To be ready.

"How old is Ichika?"

Kaneki thought about it.

"I don't know," he said. "Not exactly. She was very small when I—" He stopped. "When I came here. She'll be older now. I don't know how much. Every world keeps its own clock." He looked at the coins. "She might be walking. She might be talking. She might—" He stopped again. "She might not remember me."

Fern wrote: *Ichika: young. Age unknown. Small.*

"Food," she said.

Kaneki was quiet for a moment.

"Touka is what I am," he said. "Ichika is—half. Partly." He looked at his hands. "Maomao found an arrangement for me. When I came. It isn't elegant. It works." He paused. "I don't know if it'll work for Ichika. She's different. She's both."

Fern wrote: *Food: Maomao. Ask today. Before tonight.*

"The room," she said.

Kaneki looked at her.

"I have a room," he said. "Past Rem's turn. It's—" He stopped. "It's one room. With one bed. And a shelf. For one person."

Fern looked at him.

Then she turned the ledger back several pages. To Day 310. She turned it round so he could see.

*Day 310. Wakana chalks the next empty room. End of the west wing, past Taihei's. Window toward the hills. Taihei on the far wall. (For whoever comes.)*

"It's finished," said Fern. "Wakana finished the door on Day 349. It's been empty since. That's the rule. Rimuru's. There's always one empty room ready." She turned the ledger back round. "It's ready. For whoever comes."

Kaneki did not say anything for a long time.

"It's bigger than yours," said Fern. "It has two windows. Wakana put in a second one, facing the yard, because he said a room at the end of a wing gets lonely without something to look at." She wrote. "It has one bed. It'll need another. A small one."

* * *

She asked the rest.

Who would wait. Where. What Touka would need to know first, and who would tell her. Whether the register girl should be asked to open the hall, the way she had for Taihei, or whether they would come straight to the kilns. Whether Kaneki wanted the house on the step, the way the house had stood on the step for every coin since Nijika's, or whether he wanted it quiet.

Kaneki answered every one. Slowly. Carefully. The way he did everything.

"Quiet," he said, to the last one. "Not nobody. But quiet." He looked at the coins. "Eren. At the kilns. I'd like Eren there. He's the only one who knows her name." He paused. "Knew her name. Until this morning."

Fern wrote it.

"And on the step," said Kaneki. "Whoever wants. But not—" He stopped. "Touka doesn't like crowds. She ran a coffee shop. She liked people one at a time. Across a counter." He almost smiled. "She'll hit someone if there's a crowd. Probably me."

Fern wrote: *Step: whoever wants. Not a crowd. (She'll hit someone. Probably Kaneki.)*

She looked at what she had written.

Then she put down the pen.

"Go to sleep," she said. "You haven't slept. You'll need to be awake at eleven." She closed the ledger. "I'll talk to Maomao. And Wakana. And Momo. You don't have to do anything until tonight."

Kaneki looked at her.

"Fern."

"Go to sleep, Kaneki."

He did not move for a moment. Then he closed his hand round the two coins and the honeysuckle and stood up.

At the bottom of the stair he stopped.

"She'll like you," he said, without turning round. "Touka. She'll like you. You're the kind of person she'd keep the good cups for."

He went up.

Fern sat at the long table with the closed ledger for a long time. Then she opened it again, to the day's page, and wrote one more line under the others, very small:

*He said she'd keep the good cups for me. (Don't tell Stark. He'll be jealous.)*

* * *

The house did not fuss.

It had learned not to. Nijika had asked for no fuss, the first time, and the house had done it, and it had become the way. Nobody said anything at breakfast. Nobody gathered. Nobody made a speech.

But Momo, at the long counter, took down the big bowl at nine o'clock and started a pear bread. When it was in the oven, she wrote a card in her round hand and propped it against the bowl:

*FOR TOMORROW (OR WHENEVER).*

Maomao came out of the clinic at ten and went to Kaneki's door and knocked. When there was no answer, because he was asleep, she sat down outside it on the floor with her notebook and waited. When he woke at two, she was still there. They talked for an hour with the door half open. When she came out, she went straight to the clinic and started measuring things into small jars, very fast, with an expression Mau had only seen on her face when something interested her more than it worried her.

"It'll work," she said to Fern, in the corridor. "For the mother. Same as his." She paused. "For the child—I'll need to see her. But I think it'll work. Better, maybe. She's young. Young bodies adapt." She looked at her jars. "It won't be elegant."

"It never is," said Fern.

"It never is," Maomao agreed, and went back to her measuring.

Wakana spent the afternoon at the end of the west wing with Taihei. When Mau went past at four, he heard sawing, and then hammering, and then Taihei's voice saying, "Smaller. She's small. Kaneki said she's small." And Wakana's voice saying, "She'll grow. It needs to be a bed she can grow into." Then sawing again.

At five, Marin went down the west wing with something folded over her arm. A quilt. Small. Patchwork, from offcuts, in colours that did not match and somehow did. She went into the empty room at the end and came out without it.

At six, Rem went down the west wing with a basket of kindling and lit the small stove in the empty room. She had lit the stove in the Muslin for Seika, and in the new rooms for everyone since. She did not say anything. She lit it and checked it and came back.

And at seven, after dinner, Rimuru went out into the yard with a piece of chalk.

He went to the very end of the west wing, past Taihei's room, past the room with two windows that was not empty any more. He found the place in the dirt where the next wall would go. He knelt down and drew a long white line on the ground, and then another, at right angles. The corner of a room that did not exist yet.

He stood up and looked at it.

"For whoever comes," he said, to nobody.

Then he went back inside. Wakana, watching from the window of the room with two windows, nodded once and went back to sanding the edges of a very small bed.

* * *

Mau did not go to the kilns.

He sat on the back step after dinner with Frieren and watched the sun go down over the hills. His coat was on. The inside pocket on the left was lighter than it had been for nearly a year. Mai's bullet was still there, cold. The warm thing beside it was gone.

He kept putting his hand on the pocket. He did not mean to.

"You keep doing that," said Frieren.

"I know."

"Does it feel empty?"

He thought about it.

"It feels like it's somewhere else," he said. "Not empty. Somewhere else. Being warm in someone else's hand." He took his hand away from the pocket. "That's different from empty."

Frieren leaned against him.

"Eleven," she said.

"Eleven."

They sat on the step and watched the light go. Across the timber yard, at the far edge, the smoke from the kilns went up into the darkening sky. And a little after nine, a figure walked across the yard toward the kilns from the house. Not fast. Not slow. With his hands in his pockets and his head down.

Eren.

Kaneki had asked him. Nobody else.

Mau watched him go. Then he did not watch any more. Some things were not yours to watch.
