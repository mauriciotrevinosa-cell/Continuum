*When: Day 355, eleven at night. The kilns at the far edge of the timber yard. The middle kiln is burning Wakana's twisted oak slow and dense for Senku's furnace. Three cups on the shelf in the bark shelter, not two. The third is new.*

Kaneki made the coffee the same way he always made it.

Same measure. Same water. Same pot. On the flat stone at the edge of the fire, where it always sat. He did not do anything differently. That was the point of the coffee. It was the one thing his hands did that he knew. The one thing in the whole day that was his, and that he had chosen, and that never surprised him.

Tonight, his hands were shaking.

He made it anyway. Same measure. Same water. He watched it heat. He did not pour it yet.

On the shelf in the bark shelter there were three cups. The two that had always been there, upside down. And a third, beside them. Small. Wooden. Turned on the lathe that afternoon, very smooth, the inside polished. Wakana had brought it to his door at six, without a word, and put it in his hands, and gone back down the corridor.

Kaneki looked at the three cups.

Then he heard Eren coming across the timber yard, and did not turn round.

* * *

Eren sat down on the bench in the shelter.

He did not say anything. That was how it was between them. It had always been how it was. Two people who did not sleep, sitting by a fire. Eren sat on the bench with his elbows on his knees and looked at the fire, and Kaneki raked the embers, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

Then Kaneki put the rake down.

He took the two coins out of his breast pocket. And the honeysuckle, still stuck together. He put the honeysuckle back. He held the two coins in his open palm in the red light.

Eren looked at them.

"Mau's," he said. It was not a question.

"Mau's," said Kaneki. "And mine."

Eren was quiet.

"He gave it to you."

"I said no."

"And he gave it to you anyway."

"He gave it to me anyway," said Kaneki. "He said nobody should have to choose which of the two people they love gets to come." He looked at the coins. "He said there's nobody on the other side of his clearing. Everyone he'd call is already here." He paused. "Except mine."

Eren did not say anything for a long time.

"That's a very Mau thing to say," he said at last.

"Yes."

"Are you going to do it?"

Kaneki looked at the coins.

"That's why you're here," he said.

* * *

He did not sit down. He stood at the mouth of the middle kiln, where he always stood, with the red light on his face. He held the two coins in his open palm.

He closed his hand round them.

You held it. You thought of the person. That was all. Ciel had said it was the simplest thing the Goddess had ever made. Nijika had done it on the floor of the music room, thinking of her sister. Rem had done it in her pencil room at midnight. Yuta had done it on the roof with his eyes open. Every one of them had sat across from Fern first and told her, and then gone somewhere that was theirs, and closed their hand, and thought of the person.

He closed his hand, and thought of Touka.

Not the way she had been at the end. Not worried. Not the face he imagined, some nights, at the kilns, at eleven: her alone, with Ichika, in a world where he had simply stopped being. He thought of her the way she had been in the coffee shop. Behind the counter. Annoyed with him. Pouring him a cup without asking, because she knew how he took it, and pretending she didn't care whether he drank it.

He thought of her hitting him first. And then saying yes.

The coins went warm.

Warmer.

And a voice, from nowhere, from everywhere, from the inside of his own closed hand:

*You are sure? Touka.*

Kaneki did not breathe.

"Yes," he said.

His voice came out very quiet. Very even. The way it came out when something mattered too much to let it shake.

"Yes. I'm sure."

* * *

The coins stayed warm.

One of them. He could feel it. One was warmer than the other now, much warmer, almost too warm to hold. The other was waiting.

He thought of Ichika.

He did not know what she looked like now. That was the hard part. That was the part he had thought about for nearly a year, at eleven, with two cups. She had been so small. She had fitted in the crook of one arm. She had held on to his finger with her whole hand and looked up at him with eyes that were, sometimes, when she was hungry or frightened, the same as his. The same as Touka's. And he had thought, *I'll protect this. Whatever it costs. I'll protect this.*

And then he was gone.

He did not know what she looked like now. He thought of her anyway. The small hand round his finger. The eyes.

The second coin went warm.

Warmer.

*You are sure? Ichika.*

Kaneki closed his eyes.

"Yes," he said. "I've been sure since she was born."

* * *

The coins went warmer still.

Both of them. All at once. Warmer than anything he had ever held, in his closed hand. He held on, the way everyone before him had held on, Rem and Yuta and Maki and Umaru, because you held on until it was done.

And then they were gone.

Not cold. Gone. One moment they were burning in his closed fist, and the next there was nothing in it at all, and his palm, when he opened it, was empty and pink. As if he had been holding something warm for a very long time and had just put it down.

He looked at his empty palm in the red light of the kilns.

Behind him, on the bench, Eren let out a breath. A long one. As if he had been holding it the whole time.

"It carried," said Eren.

"It carried," said Kaneki.

His voice was not even any more.

* * *

He turned round.

He did not look at Eren. He went to the shelf in the bark shelter. He reached up and took down the first cup, the one he always used, and turned it over and set it on the bench. Then the second.

The second cup. The one he had never poured. The one that had sat upside down on the shelf beside his every night, at the kilns, at eleven, for longer than anyone in the house could remember.

He turned it over. He set it beside the first.

Then the third. The small one. Wooden, turned on the lathe that afternoon, smooth, the inside polished. He turned it over and set it beside the other two.

He picked up the pot from the flat stone.

He poured the first cup. Black. Thick. Bitter. Steaming. The way he always made it. On purpose.

He poured the second.

Black. The same. From the same pot. He looked at it.

"She'd hate this," he said. To nobody. "She taught me how to make it properly. Behind the counter. Every morning for a month. I could never get it right, and she always drank it anyway and told me it was terrible." He looked at the cup. "I'll make it properly when she comes. The way she taught me. I'll try."

Eren did not say anything.

Then Kaneki picked up the small wooden cup.

He did not pour coffee into it. He looked into the box under the bench, where he kept the things for the coffee. There was a little covered jug in there that had not been there yesterday. Milk. Momo had brought it at nine, without a word, and put it in the box, and gone back across the timber yard.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he closed the box. Maomao had said *wait*. She had said she would need to see the child before anyone knew what was safe. He did not know what Ichika could drink now. He did not know anything about her now.

He filled the small cup from the water barrel instead. Not to the top. Exactly to the line. The way you fill a cup for someone small, so they will not spill it.

Three cups on the bench in the bark shelter. Two black. One water.

Kaneki looked at them for a long time.

* * *

"They'll come at the house," said Eren quietly. "Not here. At the hall. Everyone does."

"I know."

"Fern said before dawn. Probably. That's when the others came. Seika. Ram. Yuka." Eren looked at the three cups. "The coffee'll be cold."

"I know," said Kaneki.

He picked up the first cup, his own, and drank some of it. It was terrible. It was the worst coffee he had ever had, the way it always was.

He did not touch the other two.

"I'll make more," he said. "When they come. Fresh. Properly." He looked at the second cup. "But I wanted to pour them. Tonight. Here." He paused. "I've been pouring one cup and putting the other back on the shelf for nearly a year. I wanted to pour both. Once. Before they came. So I'd know what it was like."

Eren looked at him.

"What's it like?" he said.

Kaneki looked at the three cups on the bench in the red light of the kilns.

He did not answer for a long time.

"Like a tomorrow," he said at last. "With a tomorrow in it."

* * *

They sat by the kilns until three.

Eren on the bench. Kaneki on the ground with his back against the warm earth of the middle kiln. The three cups on the bench between them, going cold. Neither of them said anything. The fire burned low. The smoke went up out of the vents into the dark.

At three, Kaneki stood up.

He banked the fires in all three kilns, the way he did at the end of every shift. He took the three cups off the bench. He did not pour them away. He covered them, one at a time, with a clean cloth, and left them on the shelf in the bark shelter, side by side. The small one in the middle.

Then he walked across the timber yard toward the house.

Eren walked beside him.

Halfway across the yard, Eren said, without looking at him, "You told me once. That it's never over. That's the good news."

"I remember."

"You were right," said Eren. "This isn't over. This is the start of something." He put his hands in his pockets. "That's the good news."

Kaneki did not say anything.

But he walked a little faster. Toward the hall at the front of the house. Toward the step where the house had stood, in the dark before dawn, for every coin that had ever carried.

There were already people on it.
