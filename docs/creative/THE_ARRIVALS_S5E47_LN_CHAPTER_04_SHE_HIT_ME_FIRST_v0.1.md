## Chapter 42 — She Hit Me First

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)

*When: Day 356, the fifth bell, before dawn. The intake hall of Arrival House. The register girl lit the stove at four. A few people are in the plaza outside, pretending not to wait: Fern, Eren, Maomao, Rem, Momo with a cloth-covered basket, Mau and Frieren at the back. Not a crowd.*

The register girl had the stove lit by four, and the hall was still cold.

It was always cold. It was the stone floor. She knelt in front of the stove and fed it, piece by piece, and watched the light come up on the clay walls. Then she went to the desk. The book was open on it, to the page after Taihei's. A fresh page. A sharpened pen.

Kaneki stood in the middle of the hall.

He had been standing there since half past three. He had walked across the timber yard with Eren and through the plaza, past the few people on the step. He had not looked at them. He came in through the door and stood in the middle of the stone floor, where everyone who came by coin had appeared, and did not move.

He had washed. He had changed his shirt. The smoke from the kilns was still in his hair.

He had both hands at his sides. They were closed.

The register girl did not say anything to him. She had been doing this a long time. She knew when not to say anything.

She sat behind her desk with the pen in her hand and waited too.

* * *

The bell rang five.

It came from the bell tower on the far side of the settlement, faint through the clay walls, one stroke at a time. One. Two. Kaneki did not move. Three. Four.

Five.

The air in the middle of the hall moved.

Not a light. Not a sound. The air, the way air moves over a road in summer. A shimmer, an arm's length in front of Kaneki, about the height of a woman. And lower, beside it, a smaller one.

Kaneki did not breathe.

And then there was a woman standing on the stone floor.

She had a cloth in one hand, the kind you wipe down a counter with. Her other arm was round a small child, held against her hip, a little girl in a sleeping shirt with her face pressed into her mother's shoulder. The woman's hair was dark and fell across one side of her face, the way it always had. She was wearing an apron.

She was looking at the cloth in her hand.

Then she looked up.

* * *

She saw the hall first. The high windows, still dark. The clay walls. The stove in the corner. The register girl behind her desk with her pen.

Then she saw him.

Touka did not move for a long moment.

Neither did Kaneki.

He stood an arm's length from her on the stone floor with his hands closed at his sides and the smoke of the kilns in his hair and looked at her. At her face. At the dark hair falling across it. At the apron, and the cloth, and the small girl on her hip with her face hidden.

She looked back at him.

Her face did not change. Not at first. It was very still. The way Touka's face went very still when she was deciding how angry to be.

Then she put the child down.

She did it carefully. She set her on her feet on the stone floor beside her, and held her hand for a moment until she was steady. The child stood there in her sleeping shirt, small, barefoot on the cold stone, holding on to her mother's apron with one fist and looking at the hall with enormous eyes.

Then Touka let go of the child's hand.

She took two steps across the stone floor.

And hit him.

* * *

It was not a slap. Touka did not slap. It was her fist, closed, square in the middle of his chest, with all of her weight behind it. It made a sound in the cold hall like someone dropping a sack of flour.

Kaneki did not move.

He did not step back. He did not lift his hands. He stood there and took it, the way he had taken everything else for longer than she knew. When she pulled her fist back, he was still standing exactly where he had been, an arm's length away, looking at her.

"*Where*," said Touka, "*were you.*"

Her voice was very low. Very flat. Shaking underneath.

"Here," said Kaneki.

She hit him again. The same place.

"*Where were you.*"

"Here," said Kaneki again. He did not move. "I was here. I didn't leave. I'd never—" His voice went. He waited. It came back. "One day I was there and the next I was here. In a field. With strangers. There was a voice. I couldn't get back. There wasn't a way back that brought you." He looked at her. "I was here. The whole time. Making two cups of coffee every night and drinking one."

Touka's fist stayed up.

She looked at him. At his face. At the smoke in his hair.

"I know you didn't leave," she said.

Her voice broke in the middle.

"I know you didn't leave," said Touka. "You'd never leave. You're too stupid to leave. That's why I was so *angry*." Her fist came down. She grabbed the front of his clean shirt in both hands. "Because I knew you didn't leave. So something took you. And I didn't know what. And I didn't know where. And Ichika kept asking. Fifteen days she kept asking. And I kept the shop open. Every night. Until closing. In case you walked in. In case you walked in and wanted a cup and I wasn't there—"

She stopped.

She was holding his shirt so hard her knuckles were white.

Kaneki put his arms round her.

* * *

He did it very slowly. As if she might hit him again. She did not. She stood with her fists in his shirt and her face against his chest, in the place she had hit him. He held her. He did not say anything. Neither did she.

They stood like that in the middle of the stone floor of the cold hall for a long time.

The register girl, behind her desk, looked down at the book very carefully. She did not look up until it was over.

* * *

"Something asked me," said Touka, eventually. Into his chest. Muffled. "At closing. I was wiping down the counter. And something—" She stopped. "A voice. From nowhere. It said your name. It said you were asking." She was quiet. "It said, *you are sure?*"

"What did you say?"

Touka lifted her head and looked at him.

"I said yes," she said, "before it finished asking."

Kaneki looked at her.

Then he laughed. Not much. A small sound, broken, startled out of him, the first time anyone in the house had heard Kaneki laugh like that.

"Of course you did," he said.

"Of course I did," said Touka. "You idiot."

* * *

The child had not moved.

She stood on the cold stone floor in her sleeping shirt, barefoot, holding on to nothing now, because her mother had let go of her hand to hit a strange man. She looked up at the two of them with enormous eyes.

Touka saw her looking.

She let go of Kaneki's shirt. Not all at once. One hand, then the other. She stepped back, half a step, and turned, and crouched down beside the child, so their faces were level.

"Ichika," she said.

The child looked at her mother. Then up at the man.

Kaneki had not moved. He stood where he had been standing, with his arms empty now. He looked down at the small girl on the stone floor. She was exactly as small as the morning he left. Exactly. Standing, barely, the way she had only just learned to. The same dark hair falling across one side of her face. His eyes. Or Touka's. Both. Fifteen days, for her. Nothing had changed in her at all.

It had changed in him. More than a year of another world, the kilns and the house and the smoke in his hair, his face thinner and older and quieter than the face she knew.

She was looking at him the way you look at a stranger who reminds you of someone.

"Ichika," said Touka again. Gently. "Do you know who that is?"

Ichika looked at Kaneki for a long time.

She did not say anything. She held on to the edge of her mother's apron and looked at the tall man in the clean shirt with smoke in his hair. Her face was very serious. The way Touka's face went serious when she was deciding something.

Kaneki crouched down.

Slowly. On the cold stone. Until he was the same height as his daughter. He did not reach for her. He did not say *it's Papa* or *do you remember me* or any of the things he had said in his head at the kilns, at eleven, for more than a year. He crouched there, an arm's length away, and held out his hand. Open. Palm up. The way you hold out your hand when you are not reaching, just offering.

The palm was still pink.

Ichika looked at the hand.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she let go of her mother's apron. She took one step across the cold stone floor, barefoot. And another. She reached out with one small hand and took hold of his finger. Not his hand. One finger. With her whole fist. The way she had held it fifteen days ago, the last morning, before he went out and did not come back.

She looked up at him.

"Papa?" she said.

Kaneki could not say anything at all.

* * *

The register girl wrote it down.

She did it while they were still on the floor. Kaneki on his knees with his daughter's fist round his finger, and Touka beside them with her hand over her mouth. The register girl picked up the pen and found the fresh page in the book and wrote the date at the top in her best handwriting, which was still not very good.

*Day 356. The fifth bell. By coin (two).*

*Touka. Wife of Kaneki.*

*Ichika. Their daughter.*

She looked at it.

Then, because Fern had told her the coins, and where they came from, she added underneath, small:

*(The second coin was Mau's. Given.)*

She did not say anything about it. She put the pen down.

* * *

Outside, in the plaza, in the grey before dawn, the few people on the step heard the hall go quiet. Then a sound like a sack of flour dropped. Then another. Then a long silence. Then, very faintly, through the clay walls, a man laughing. Broken, startled. A sound none of them had ever heard Kaneki make.

Eren, on the step, put his face in his hands.

Fern wrote something in the ledger. She did not say what.

Rem looked at Momo. Momo looked at the cloth-covered basket on her arm, the pear bread, FOR TOMORROW (OR WHENEVER), and did not take the cloth off yet.

Maomao looked at the door with an expression of great professional interest. She was very carefully not crying.

At the back of the plaza, by the corner of the hall, Mau stood with Frieren.

He had his hand on the inside pocket of his coat, on the left. He did not mean to. It was lighter. Mai's bullet was still there, cold. The warm thing was gone. It was somewhere else, being warm in somebody else's life.

Frieren took his hand off the pocket.

She held it in hers. Cold, the way her hands always were.

"It carried," she said.

"It carried," said Mau.

They did not go in. Some things were not yours to watch. They stood at the corner of the hall in the grey light and waited. The door opened. A tall man came out with smoke in his hair and a small girl on his hip, holding on to his finger, and a woman beside him in an apron with a counter cloth still in her hand, looking at everything with narrowed eyes.

Touka looked at the plaza. At the few people on the step. At Fern with her ledger, and Eren with his face in his hands, and Rem, and Maomao, and Momo with her basket.

"This is a lot of people," she said to Kaneki, very low.

"It's six," said Kaneki.

"It's a lot of people at five in the morning."

"Fern said not a crowd," said Kaneki. "This is not a crowd. You should see a crowd here." He paused. "They wanted you to know someone was waiting."

Touka looked at the step for a long moment.

Then she looked at Momo, and the basket, and the smell coming out from under the cloth. Pears. Warm bread.

Her face changed. Very slightly. The way the face of someone changes when they smell something they cannot have.

"We can't eat that," she said. Quietly. Not unkind. Just true.

"I know," said Momo. "Maomao told me. Yesterday." She took the cloth off anyway. The pear bread steamed in the grey air, golden, with the card still propped against the side of the basket in her round hand. *FOR TOMORROW (OR WHENEVER).* "It's not for eating. Not for you. It's what we do. Whenever someone comes by coin, there's pear bread on the step. So the first thing they smell here is something somebody made because they were coming." She looked at Touka. "Maomao's making the other thing. The thing you can have. It'll be ready by breakfast. It won't be elegant." She paused. "This is just so you know somebody baked."

Touka looked at the bread for a long time.

Then at the round handwriting on the card.

"It smells like the bakery," she said, very low, to Kaneki. "Near the shop. The one on the corner. I used to walk past it every morning and wish." She stopped.

"I know," said Kaneki.

Ichika, on his hip, leaned toward the basket with her whole body and breathed in, very seriously, with her eyes closed.

Momo laughed. She could not help it. And Touka, after a moment, very nearly did too.
