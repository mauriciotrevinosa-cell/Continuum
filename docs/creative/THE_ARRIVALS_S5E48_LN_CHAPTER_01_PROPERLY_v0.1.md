# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 43 — Properly

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 43
**Continuity state:** Touka's first night, from her point of view. She goes with Kaneki to the kilns on his shift while Ichika sleeps (Rem sits in), tastes his deliberately terrible coffee and makes it properly the way she taught him; Eren comes by and tastes both; she learns about the night shift, the second cup, Eren and the house in pieces; she keeps her own judgement and is not overwhelmed into gratitude. The coffee is ghoul-safe black. No powers

*When: Day 356, eleven at night. Touka has been in this world for eighteen hours. Ichika is asleep in a small bed in a room with two windows, under a patchwork quilt, with Rem sitting in the chair by the door because Touka would not leave her alone and Rem said "Rem will sit. Rem is very good at sitting."*

The kilns were not what she expected.

She did not know what she had expected. Kaneki had said *the night shift* and *charcoal* and *the timber yard*, in pieces, all day, between other things. Between Maomao's questions in the clinic, and the room with two windows, and the girl with the pink hair who floated. Between a woman called Fern who wrote everything down, and a long table with more people at it than Touka had seen at one table in years. She had built a picture from the pieces. Something grim. Something lonely. A man alone in the dark doing a hard job because nobody else would.

It was three humps of earth with smoke coming out of them, and a bark shelter, and a bench.

And it was warm. That surprised her. The night was cool, the first cool night, Kaneki said, since the summer started. The kilns breathed out warmth into the dark like sleeping animals.

Kaneki picked up a long iron rake and went to the mouth of the middle one and raked the embers.

Touka stood in the bark shelter and watched him.

He did it the way he did everything. Slowly. Carefully. The red light on his face. His sleeves pushed up. She had seen him do a thousand things that way, in her world. Fight. Read. Hold Ichika. Pour coffee, badly, behind her counter, while she told him it was terrible.

She had not seen him do this. She had not seen him look like this. Like a man who was standing in a place that was his.

* * *

There was a pot on a flat stone at the edge of the fire.

There were three cups on the shelf.

Two of them were covered with a clean cloth. She lifted the cloth. Two cups underneath, full, cold. Black. And a small wooden one, also full. Water.

She looked at them for a long time.

"Last night," said Kaneki, behind her, without turning round from the kiln. "After the coins. I poured them. Before you came." He raked the embers. "I wanted to know what it was like. Pouring both."

Touka did not say anything.

She looked at the second cup. Black. Cold. Poured for her before she was here.

"What was it like?" she said.

Kaneki was quiet for a moment.

"Like a tomorrow," he said.

Touka put the cloth back over the cups, very carefully.

Then she picked up the pot from the flat stone and smelled it.

Her face did something.

"*What*," she said, "is this."

* * *

Kaneki stopped raking.

"Coffee," he said.

"This is not coffee." Touka looked into the pot. "This is—" She smelled it again. "Did you burn it? On purpose? Is that—what did you do to it?"

"I make it like that on purpose."

"*Why?*"

Kaneki put the rake down. He came over to the shelter. He did not look at her. He looked at the pot in her hands.

"So there was one thing my hands did that I knew," he said. "When I first came. When everything was strange, and my body was strange, and the food was strange. I made coffee. Every night. The same way. Awful. So it would never surprise me." He paused. "It's not supposed to be good. It's supposed to be the same."

Touka looked at him for a long time.

Then she looked at the pot.

"For a year," she said.

"Nearly."

"You've been drinking this. Every night. For nearly a year. On purpose."

"Yes."

"In front of people."

"Eren drinks it."

"Who's Eren?"

"You met him. On the step. With his face in his hands."

Touka thought about it. The young man on the step at five in the morning with his face in his hands, who had looked up when they came out of the hall with an expression she had not understood. He had looked as if someone had given him back something of his own.

"He drinks *this*?"

"Every drop," said Kaneki. "Every time. He says it's the worst coffee he's ever had." He almost smiled. "Then he drinks all of it."

Touka put the pot down on the flat stone.

"Show me what you've got," she said.

* * *

He had more than she expected.

A sack of beans that Hollis brought from the southern towns, whole, dark. A grinder, small, wooden, that Wakana had made him in the winter. A cloth for straining. The pot. A small jar of something she did not recognise and did not trust. Water from the barrel.

She looked at all of it in the red light.

"You've had real beans," she said. "This whole time. Whole beans. And a grinder."

"Yes."

"And you've been doing *that* to them."

"On purpose."

Touka closed her eyes for a moment. Then she opened them, and pushed up her sleeves, and knelt down in front of the flat stone.

"Watch," she said. "I'm only showing you once more. I showed you every morning for a month, behind the counter, and you never got it."

"I remember."

"You *never got it*."

"I remember," said Kaneki. He sat down on the bench and watched.

* * *

She did it the way she had done it every morning, behind her counter, in the shop, for years.

She did not hurry. She measured the beans by eye, by the weight in her palm. She ground them, not too fine. She heated the water and did not let it boil. She took it off the stone and let it stand while she counted, under her breath, the way the old manager had counted when he taught her, a long time ago, in another shop. She poured it slowly, in a thin stream, through the cloth, over the grounds. She let it drip. She did not touch it.

The smell came up.

Kaneki, on the bench, went very still.

She knew why. She had seen it on his face a thousand times, behind her counter. The first smell of a cup made properly, in the morning, before the shop opened, when it was just the two of them.

She poured it into the cup that was his. The one he always used. She handed it to him.

He took it.

He did not drink it at once. He held it in both hands and breathed it, with his eyes closed.

Then he drank.

Touka watched him.

He did not say anything for a long time. He sat on the bench in the bark shelter with the cup in both hands and his eyes closed, in the red light of the kilns.

"Well?" said Touka.

He opened his eyes.

"It's terrible," he said.

Touka hit him on the arm.

He laughed. The broken one, the startled one. Twice in one day. She had not heard it once in the whole time before he went.

"It's perfect," he said. "It's exactly how you make it. Exactly." He looked into the cup. "I'd forgotten. I'd made the other kind for so long I'd forgotten what it was supposed to taste like."

Touka looked at him.

"That's why you make it badly," she said slowly. "Isn't it. Not so it's the same. So it's not *this*."

Kaneki did not answer.

He did not have to.

* * *

Eren came at half past eleven.

Touka heard him from the other side of the timber yard. She did not know how she knew it was him. She heard the step, and it was a young man's step, quick and heavy and a little uneven. She thought: the one on the step with his face in his hands.

He stopped at the edge of the shelter when he saw her.

"Sorry," he said. "I didn't—I'll go."

"Sit down," said Touka.

Eren looked at Kaneki.

Kaneki, on the bench, moved over, so there was room.

Eren sat. He looked at Touka. Then at the pot on the flat stone, which smelled wrong. Wrong in the opposite way from usual.

Kaneki reached up without looking and took down a cup. He did not pour from the old pot. He held it out to Touka.

Touka filled it from the new one and handed it to Eren.

He took it. He smelled it. His face changed.

"That's not—" he said.

"That's coffee," said Touka. "Real coffee. The way it's supposed to be." She looked at him. "He says you drink his. Every drop."

"Every drop," said Eren.

"Why?"

Eren looked at the cup in his hands for a long time.

"Because he made it," he said. "Every night. The same. When I couldn't sleep. When I'd walked the line twice and didn't know what to do. He made it terrible on purpose and gave it to me and didn't say anything." He paused. "It was the worst coffee in the world. It was the only thing that was the same every night." He looked at Kaneki. "That's why."

Touka looked at him.

Then at Kaneki, on the bench beside him, not saying anything, the way he never said anything.

"Drink it," she said to Eren. "Mine. Tell me what you think."

Eren drank it.

He was quiet for a long time.

"It's very good," he said finally. "It's much better than his." He looked into the cup. "I think I like his better."

Touka stared at him.

Kaneki put his face in his hands.

And Touka laughed.

She did not mean to. It came out of her all at once, in the bark shelter, in the red light, at the young man who had just told her that her husband's terrible coffee was better than hers. It was the first time she had laughed in this world. It was the first time she had laughed since before he went.

"You're both idiots," she said, when she could.

"Yes," said Kaneki, through his hands.

"Yes," said Eren, and drank the rest of hers anyway. Every drop.

* * *

She stayed until two.

She sat on the bench between them, in the bark shelter, with her own cup, and listened. Kaneki told her things. Not all at once. In pieces, the way he had told her all day. The timber yard. The night shift. The night against the wall in the winter, when he had stood all night so his blood would not hurt anyone. A girl called Mikasa who was Eren's. A boy called Mau who had given away the only thing the Goddess ever gave him.

Touka listened to all of it.

She did not say she was grateful. She did not say she was overwhelmed. She was not, really. She was a woman who had run a coffee shop through the worst years her world had ever had, and then for fifteen nights raised a child alone after he went, and kept the doors open every night until closing in case he walked in. She did not get overwhelmed. She got angry, and then she got on with it.

She was angry, a little. At the voice that had taken him. At the year.

She was getting on with it.

At two, she stood up.

"I'm going back," she said. "To Ichika. The girl who sits."

"Rem," said Kaneki.

"Rem." Touka looked at the pot on the flat stone. The new one, mostly empty now. "I'll come back tomorrow night. And show you again." She paused. "And the night after. Until you get it."

"I'll never get it," said Kaneki.

"I know," said Touka. "That's not the point."

She went back across the timber yard in the dark, toward the house, toward a room with two windows at the end of a wing, where her daughter was asleep under a quilt that didn't match.

Behind her, she heard Eren say, very quietly, "She's terrifying."

And Kaneki say, "Yes."

And Eren: "I like her."

And Kaneki, after a long moment: "So do I."
