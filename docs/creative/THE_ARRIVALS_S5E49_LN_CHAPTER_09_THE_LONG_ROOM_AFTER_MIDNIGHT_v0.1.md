# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 54 — The Long Room After Midnight

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18, DH-125)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 54
**Continuity state:** Day 361, after midnight. Mau is asleep under Shoko's wet cloth, with Ori against the connecting-door frame and Frieren in the reading chair. In the long room, by one lamp, without anyone calling it, the people who were there or who have stood watch gather: Shoko, Gojo, Yuta (bound, grey), Maki, Rimuru, Fern, Stark, Kaneki, Himmel, Anko, Mikasa and Eren, Rem, Seiko. It is a possible intervention. Should they stop him? Lock the screen? Take the key? Each speaks from their own history. Shoko: the boy who got so strong nobody could stand next to him. Mikasa and Eren: they know what it is to watch someone walk toward a thing that will take everything. Rem remembers the bay night. Kaneki: going off alone was the worst thing he did. Himmel: you can't make a hero stop by forbidding it; you can only make him want to come home. Rimuru brings Ciel's word: he will not stop, and taking the key would make him go the way he went on the north road, without anyone. They decide not to cage him. They decide that nobody lets him go alone again, that Shoko's check stays, and that the house will give him reasons to come back. Fern opens no page yet. Near the end Frieren comes down, having heard some of it from the stair, and says the one thing that settles it. No vote, no rule. Nobody tells Mau there was a meeting

*When: Day 361, after midnight. The house should be asleep. One lamp is lit at the end of the long table nearest the stairs. Nobody called anyone. They came.*

Shoko was there first.

She had not meant to be. She had come out of the clinic at midnight, after Yuta finally went to sleep in his own bed, meaning to go to hers. She had stopped at the bottom of the stairs instead and looked at the long room, dark, with the chairs pushed in, and sat down at the end of the table nearest the stairs and lit the lamp, and not known why.

Gojo came down ten minutes later. He did not say anything. He sat down across from her.

Then Rimuru, with Diablo a pace behind. Then Fern and Stark, together, Fern still in her day coat. Then Maki, who said only "Yuta's asleep" and sat. Then Kaneki, from the kilns, smelling of smoke. Then Himmel, with his hair not brushed at all, which nobody in the house had ever seen.

Anko came down the stairs with her pencil behind her ear and her coat on over her nightclothes. Mikasa and Eren came in from the north end, where Mikasa had been standing a watch nobody had asked her to stand. Rem came out of the kitchen with a pot of tea she had already made, because she had known. Seiko came last, on her stick, and lowered herself into the high chair at the end without asking anyone to help.

Nobody had called them.

They had all seen the same thing come through the frame that afternoon.

* * *

"Well," said Seiko, at last. "Somebody say it."

Nobody did, for a while.

Then Gojo said it.

"Do we stop him?"

* * *

The words sat on the long table in the lamplight.

"Stop him how?" said Stark.

"The key," said Gojo. "It's in his coat. Inside left. With Ori's list and the pencil and the bullet." He did not look up. "I could take it while he sleeps. He wouldn't feel it. He can't see."

Nobody said anything.

"Or the screen," said Gojo. "Someone sits at the screen. All day. All night. In shifts. He doesn't go through without two people, and the two people are us, and we say no." He turned his cup in his hands. "Or we tell him. All of us. At breakfast. *No more floors until the end of summer.*"

"Would he listen?" said Himmel.

Gojo did not answer.

* * *

Shoko put her coffee down.

"I knew someone," she said. "Where I come from." She did not look at Gojo. Everyone at the table knew she was not looking at Gojo. "A boy. Sixteen. He got strong. Stronger than anyone. He got there by never waiting, and never being stopped, and never letting anyone stand in front of anything for him." She was quiet. "He got so strong that nobody could stand next to him. And then nobody did."

Gojo did not move.

"I patched him up," said Shoko. "Every time. And said *fine*. And he went back out." She looked at the lamp. "I watched it happen from an infirmary for years. I've watched it happen again from a clinic for a month." She picked up her coffee. "So no. I don't think he'd listen. I don't think that boy would have listened either. I wish somebody had tried anyway."

* * *

"I watched Eren," said Mikasa.

Everyone looked at her. Mikasa did not often speak at the long table.

She did not look at Eren. Eren, beside her, did not look at her. He was looking at his hands.

"Walk toward something," said Mikasa. "For years. Something that was going to take everything. I knew. I could see him going." She was quiet. "I stood beside him. Every step. I thought if I stood beside him close enough, I could stop it." She looked at the table. "I couldn't stop it. I could only be there when it happened."

"Mikasa," said Eren. Very quietly.

"I'm not saying it's the same," said Mikasa. "It's not the same. He's not you." She looked up. "I'm saying I know what it looks like from beside. And standing beside him isn't nothing. It's not enough. But it isn't nothing."

Eren put his hand on the table, next to hers. Not on it. Next to it.

* * *

"I held a bowl of his blood once," said Rem.

She had her hands round the teapot. She was looking at it.

"The bay night," she said. "When the hole opened again. Sister and I. We held the bowls while Maomao worked and Senku did the transfusion." She did not look up. "I thought that was the worst I would ever see him." She was quiet. "Today he came through the frame, and he was walking, and he was talking, and it was worse."

Nobody said anything.

"On the bay night he didn't choose it," said Rem. "Today he did."

* * *

Kaneki had not said anything.

He sat at the end of the bench nearest the door, still smelling of the kilns, with his hands in his pockets.

"I went off alone," he said at last. "In my world. To find out what was in me. To be strong enough that nobody would have to protect me." He was quiet. "It was the worst thing I ever did. I hurt everyone I was trying to protect. And I got strong. I did. It didn't help."

He looked up.

"I told him that," he said. "At the kilns. And he listened." He was quiet. "And then he went up twenty floors on his own." He looked at the table. "So telling him isn't enough either."

* * *

Himmel had been listening with his chin on his hands and his unbrushed hair falling in his face.

"You can't stop a hero by forbidding it," he said.

Everyone looked at him.

"I was one," said Himmel. "A long time ago. People tried. Kings. Priests. My own party, sometimes." He almost smiled. "Eisen used to stand in doorways. Heiter used to pretend to be too drunk to walk so I'd have to stay and carry him." The smile went away. "It never worked. When I'd decided a thing had to be done, I did it. Forbidding it just meant I did it without them."

"Then what does work?" said Fern.

Himmel looked at the lamp for a long time.

"Wanting to come home," he said. "More than wanting to win." He was quiet. "I came back from every fight because there was always a village. A dance. Someone who'd made a crown of flowers and wanted to see me wear it." He looked down the dark length of the long room. "I didn't stop fighting. I just always had somewhere I wanted to be afterwards. More than I wanted to be in the fight."

He looked at them.

"Don't take his key," said Himmel. "Give him more reasons to put it down."

* * *

Rimuru had been quiet the whole time. His face had been turned inward, now and then, the way it turned when Ciel spoke.

"Ciel's been listening," he said.

Everyone looked at him.

"Not to us. To him. All evening. Asleep." He was quiet. "She says he won't stop. Not because he doesn't care what it does to us. Because of what he thinks happens if he does." He looked at the table. "She says, in the Trial, every time he waited, or stopped, or let someone else go first, that was the time Frieren died. Not every time. Enough times that he can't count them." His voice went very quiet. "And on the north road, he waited one second."

Nobody moved.

"She says if we take his key," said Rimuru, "he won't stop. He'll find another way. A worse one. Alone. The way he went down the road with nobody." He looked at Gojo. "She says the screen's the only door in this house he still comes back through. Don't lock it."

* * *

Anko had her pencil in her hand. She had not written anything. She put it down on the table.

"I tried to write a rule," she said. "On the first morning he went alone. I sat up two nights. Every rule I wrote down said *you may not go into your own tower.*" She looked at the pencil. "Every one of them sounded like a cage. I crossed them all out."

"So no rule," said Stark.

"No rule," said Anko. "I can't write one. I tried."

* * *

"Then what?" said Gojo.

He said it to all of them. His voice was not steady.

"No key. No lock. No rule. He comes back through the frame looking like that, and we say *what*? *Welcome home*? *Here's your soup*?" He put both hands flat on the table. "I stood there today, Shoko. I stood on forty and watched him look at me with my own eyes and bleed out of them. And I couldn't do anything. The notice said *may not carry*. So I stood there." He looked up. "I'm not doing that again."

"Then don't," said Maki.

Everyone looked at her.

"Don't stand there," said Maki. "Go with him. Every floor. Every time. You, or me, or Yuta, or Stark. Two of us. Always." She looked round the table. "The notice says we can't carry. It doesn't say we can't be there. It doesn't say we can't stand next to him while he does it." She looked at Mikasa. "Standing beside isn't nothing."

"And Shoko looks at him," said Fern. "Every morning. Before he goes. Every single morning."

"He'll stop coming," said Shoko. "If I say *not today* often enough."

"Then we'll make him come," said Fern. Very precisely. "That's ours. The ledger's mine. I'll write it down that he agreed."

"And the rest of the time," said Himmel, "we give him somewhere to come back to." He looked down the long room again. "Not a rule. A village."

* * *

Nobody had heard her come down the stairs.

Frieren was standing on the bottom step, in the dark beyond the lamp, with her arms wrapped round herself. Nobody knew how long she had been there. Her face was dry now. It did not look like the face from the yard. It did not look like her usual face either.

Everyone stopped.

She came down the last step and stood at the end of the long table, in the edge of the lamplight, and looked at them. All of them. Every face.

"Thank you," she said.

Nobody said anything.

"For not taking his key," said Frieren. "I thought someone would. I came down to stop you." She looked at Gojo. Gojo looked away. "I'd have taken it myself, this afternoon. On the floor of that hive. If I'd thought it would work." She was quiet. "It wouldn't. He'd have gone through the screen with his bare hands."

She looked down at the table.

"He's not doing it because he wants to be the strongest," she said. "He doesn't care about that. He's doing it because he thinks if he's strong enough, nobody will ever have to stand in front of anything for him. And nobody will ever die in front of him again." She was quiet for a long time. "He's doing it for us. That's the worst part. You can't take that away from someone. It's the best thing in him."

She looked up.

"So we go with him," she said. "And we let him come home. And we're here when he does." Her voice did something. "And when the ant comes for its *later*—"

She stopped.

Nobody asked her to finish.

She turned round and went back up the stairs, to the east room, to the reading chair, and the long room heard the door close softly behind her.

* * *

They sat for a while longer.

Then Seiko got up out of the high chair, on her stick, slowly.

"Well," she said. "That's decided, then." She looked down the table. "Nobody tells the boy. He's got enough to carry."

She went to bed.

One by one, the others went too. Until only Shoko was left, with the lamp and her cold coffee, and Gojo across from her, not moving.

"You didn't say anything," said Shoko. "At the end."

"No," said Gojo.

"The boy," said Shoko. "Sixteen. Very annoying." She did not look at him. "If somebody had sat at a table like this one, back then. For him."

Gojo did not answer for a long time.

"He wouldn't have listened," he said at last.

"I know," said Shoko. She blew out the lamp. "I'd have liked them to try."
