# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 53 — One Free Shot

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 53
**Continuity state:** The creator's Beru/Frieren sequence, part 1. North field at dusk, well inside the lime, under Umaru's Ministry rules, with the house watching and Gojo on his stool. Beru challenges Frieren for the place at his King's side (closeness to Mau as guard and champion, not romance). Frieren accepts; Beru, confident, grants her one shot and promises not to move; she fires Zoltraak, angled upward so it goes into the sky and hits nothing else, and Beru is destroyed. Igris stands as witness. Part 2 (the remaking and the apology) follows

*When: Day 364, dusk. The north field. The long grass is pale and dry. The ditch is dry. The fireflies have been gone for four weeks. Half the house is sitting on the slope below the red stake. Nazuna is on top of the stake, because Rule 3 says nothing gets hit that isn't the challenger or the challenged, and she wants to see it from up there.*

Everyone came.

Mau had known everyone would come. He had not known *everyone*. He came up the slope from the house at dusk with Frieren beside him and stopped, and looked at the north field, and did not say anything for a moment.

They were on the slope below the red stake. All of them. The fort in a row at the front, with Ichika in the middle in her too-long sash, holding on to Ori's sleeve. Kessoku, with Bocchi's guitar, which nobody had asked her to bring. The five, with Kusuri's slate, which Umaru had un-confiscated for the occasion. The witches. Touka, with Kaneki, with a cup. Seiko on a bench somebody had carried up, with Turbo Granny in her cat beside her. Eren and Mikasa, a little apart. Himmel, a little further apart, with his arms folded and an expression Mau could not read. Fern, at the front, with the ledger. Stark behind her.

And Gojo.

In the middle of the slope, on a three-legged stool with a back and no armrests, holding a cup of Touka's coffee in both hands. Wakana was beside him, looking at the stool with the faint, critical air of a man who has noticed a leg is very slightly shorter than the other two and is never going to say so.

"This is a lot of people," said Mau.

"It's a challenge," said Umaru, at the front, with her slate. "Everyone wants to see a challenge."

"Rule 1," said Yuka, beside her, reading off the copy book. "Not inside the lime. Too many windows. Rule 2. North field. Well inside the line. Dusk."

"It's dusk," said Shoko, from the bench beside Seiko. She had her watch. "Rule 2 is satisfied. And I'm adding one. Nothing comes out of you after it's dark. That's an hour. Use it."

"An hour," said Mau.

"An hour," said Shoko.

* * *

He walked out into the long grass.

Thirty paces from the slope. Well inside the lime. He stopped where the grass was flattest and the ground was level, with the slope behind him and the open north field in front of him going away toward the hills, darkening.

Frieren walked out with him.

She stopped ten paces to his left. She stood in the long pale grass with her staff in her hand, loosely, the way she held it when she was reading. She had not brought the green book. She did not look at the crowd on the slope. She looked at Mau.

"Ready?" he said.

"I'm always ready," said Frieren. "I've been ready for a thousand years. Call him."

Mau looked down at his shadow, long and thin in the low gold light, stretching away across the grass toward the hills.

"Igris," he said first. "Witness."

The knight came up out of the grass beside him, tall and dark, with the long sword sheathed at his hip. He looked at the crowd on the slope. At Frieren, ten paces away. He looked at her for a long moment.

Then he crossed the ten paces and stood a pace behind her and to her left. Exactly where a knight stands beside someone he serves.

Rule 7. *My liege's knight requests a place to stand. (Near the queen.)*

On the slope, Rimuru put his face in his hands.

Frieren did not look round at the knight behind her. But the tips of her ears went faintly pink in the dusk.

"Beru," said Mau.

* * *

Beru came up all at once.

Not the way Igris came, smooth and unhurried. All at once, like something released, out of the grass in front of Mau. Tall, thin, long-limbed, dark, with the two small cold blue lights burning in his ant-man's head.

He went straight down on one knee.

"*My King!*" said Beru. The voice went out across the north field, high and fierce and trembling, and the crowd on the slope went perfectly silent. "My King has rested! My King has called! I, Beru, rise at my King's word—"

"Beru."

"—to serve, to guard, to *devour* whatever my King commands—"

"*Beru*."

Beru stopped.

He looked up at Mau with his cold blue eyes.

"You said you'd speak with Frieren," said Mau. "Later. About the place at my side." He looked across at Frieren, ten paces away, with Igris behind her. "It's later."

Beru turned his head.

He looked at Frieren for a long time. At her standing in the long grass with her staff held loosely. At the knight a pace behind her and to her left, where a knight stands.

His head tilted. The insect tilt.

"Igris," he said. "You stand behind the elf."

"I stand where I choose to stand," said Igris. Dry. Level. "Near the one I choose to stand near."

Beru looked at him.

Then he stood up.

* * *

He walked out into the long grass.

Ten paces toward Frieren. Then five more. He stopped in front of her, close. Tall. Thin. Dark. Looking down at her from his height with his cold blue eyes, the way he had looked at her across the heart of the hive.

Frieren looked up at him.

She did not move.

"Elf," said Beru.

"Frieren," said Frieren.

"Frieren," said Beru, as if filing it. "You stand at my King's side. Close. Closer than a guard. In the place that should belong to his champion. His strongest. His most devoted." He drew himself up. "I am his strongest. I am his most devoted. I was the King of a hive and I kneel to him alone." He paused. "I challenge you. For the place."

On the slope, someone made a very small sound. It might have been Kusuri. It might have been Gojo.

Frieren looked up at the Ant King.

"For the place at his side," she said.

"For the place at his side."

"All right," said Frieren.

Beru stopped. As if he had expected an argument.

"You accept?"

"I accept."

* * *

Beru looked at her for a long moment.

Then something happened to his posture. Mau saw it. A kind of loosening. A settling. The way a very large animal settles when it has decided the thing in front of it is not a threat.

"I am generous," said Beru. "My King is generous, and so I am generous." He spread his long arms wide. "I will grant you one strike."

The crowd on the slope went silent again.

"One strike," said Beru. "With everything you have. Your strongest spell. The white light that took my arm in the hive." He planted his feet in the long grass. "I will not move. I will not dodge. I will not defend. I will stand here and receive it." He lifted his ant-man's head proudly. "And when I am still standing afterward—as I will be, for I am Beru, who was King, and I have grown since the hive—you will yield the place at my King's side."

Frieren looked at him.

"One strike," she said.

"One."

"You won't move."

"I will not move."

"Everything I have."

"Everything you have," said Beru magnificently. "I have felt your white light once. It surprised me. It will not surprise me twice."

On the slope, Gojo had put his cup down very carefully on the grass beside his stool. He was leaning forward with his elbows on his knees and his bare eyes on Frieren. He had the expression of a man watching the best thing he has ever seen and trying very hard not to laugh before it happens.

Mau looked at Frieren.

Frieren did not look at him. She looked at Beru.

"Mau," she said. "Is that allowed?"

"It's his offer," said Mau. "He made it. In front of witnesses."

"Rule 3," said Umaru, from the slope, very fast. "Nothing gets hit that isn't the challenger or the challenged. Not trees. Not the stake. Not Nazuna."

"I heard," said Frieren.

She looked at Beru. At him standing in the long grass with his arms spread wide and his head held high. Behind him, the open north field went away toward the hills, and above the hills, the sky. Darkening. Empty. Very large.

She took three steps to her left.

Beru turned his head to follow her.

"I said I would not move," he said. "I will not move. You may stand wherever you like."

"I know," said Frieren. "I'm moving the angle."

She took one more step. And then she knelt down in the long grass, on one knee, very low. From down there, Beru stood above her against the sky. Nothing behind him but the dark empty sky above the hills.

Up. Not across.

Rule 3.

She raised her staff.

* * *

"Wait," said Beru.

Frieren waited.

"You're kneeling," said Beru. "Why are you kneeling?"

"So that it goes up," said Frieren. "Into the sky. Instead of across the field. So it doesn't hit the stake. Or Nazuna. Or the hills." She did not lower her staff. "Rule 3."

Beru looked down at her. At the angle. At the sky behind him.

"You are very careful," he said. "For a spell that will not move me."

"I'm always careful," said Frieren. "I've been careful for a thousand years. That's why I'm still here." She looked up at him along the length of her staff. "Ready?"

Beru spread his arms wider.

"I am Beru," he said. "I am always ready."

"Good," said Frieren.

And fired.

* * *

It was not the thin line she used on the soldier-ants. It was not even the white light she had put through his shoulder in the hive.

It was Zoltraak.

The killing magic. A demon's spell once, until humans studied it and made it theirs; the first spell every mage in her world learned to fear and then to cast. She had been casting it longer than most of them had been alive. It came out of the end of her staff with no sound at all. It was white, and then it was brighter than white, and it went up.

Through Beru.

Not through his shoulder. Through all of him. The whole tall thin dark shape. He was standing in the long grass with his arms spread wide and his head held high, and then he was standing in the middle of the light, and then he was not standing anywhere.

The light went on up.

Into the sky. Above the hills. A straight white line from the long grass of the north field to somewhere very high, where the first stars were coming out. It lit up the whole field, the whole slope, every face on it, white. And then it was gone.

Where Beru had been, there was nothing.

A few dark pieces, falling. Like ash. Like leaves. They fell slowly through the dusk and sank into the long grass, into the ground. Into the long thin shadow that stretched across the field from where Mau was standing.

And were gone.

* * *

Nobody said anything.

The whole slope was silent. The fort. Kessoku. The five. The witches. Touka and Kaneki. Seiko on her bench. Fern with the ledger. Eren and Mikasa. Himmel with his arms folded.

Igris, standing a pace behind Frieren and to her left, had not moved at all.

Frieren stood up. She brushed the grass off her knee. She lowered her staff.

She looked at the place where Beru had been.

Then she looked at Mau.

"One strike," she said.

Mau could not say anything.

On the slope, very slowly, Gojo fell off his stool.

He did not fall far. It was a stool. He went sideways off it into the long grass and lay there on his back with his arms over his face, and made a sound. It took everyone a moment to understand it was laughing. Silent at first. Then not silent. Then so loud it went out across the north field the way Beru's voice had. Wakana looked at the empty stool with a betrayed expression. Gojo lay in the grass beside it, laughing until he could not breathe, with his blindfold half off and tears running down his face.

"*One*," he managed. "*One strike*. He *gave* it to her. He said *everything you have*. He said *I will not move*—"

Shoko, on the bench, had her hand over her mouth.

Seiko was cackling.

Kusuri was writing on her slate so fast the chalk broke.

And the fort, at the front of the slope, all of it, Ori and Umaru and Yuka and Milim, and Ichika in her too-long sash, stared at the empty place in the long grass with their mouths open.

Then Milim said, very quietly, in a voice of total awe:

"*She one-shot him.*"
