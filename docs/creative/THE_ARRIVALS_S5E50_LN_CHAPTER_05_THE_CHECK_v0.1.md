# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 30
## Chapter 57 — The Check

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18, DH-124)
**Volume:** 30
**Reading order:** 57
**Continuity state:** Day 366, evening, the clinic. For two mornings (365 and 366) Mau has gone to the tower without Shoko's check, the rule since the beginning. He went with a party each time (the house no longer lets him go alone) but asked nobody's leave and told Shoko nothing. Shoko finds out from the board at lunch and waits for him in the clinic. It is not a shouting match. She is his doctor, not his permission, and he skipped her because he knew she might say not today. Mau says what he has never said: in the Trial, the times he waited for someone to say it was all right were the times she died; on the north road he waited one second. Shoko answers as a doctor and as someone who once watched a friend become the strongest alone. She will not stop him, because she can't. She will not pretend it's medicine. Then she asks Frieren for one thing: take him somewhere that isn't the tower. This is the seed of the house's chosen days with him through V31. Fern adds a line to the ledger. No powers

*When: Day 366, after dinner. The clinic lamp is lit. The board in the yard has two new lines under FLOOR on the third plank, and beside each, in Anko's hand, small: (No check.)*

Shoko did not go and find him.

That was how he knew.

On any other day, if he had done something stupid, Shoko would have come across the yard with her coffee and told him so in a sentence, and gone back. She did not come across the yard. She did not say anything at lunch, when Anko wrote the second *(No check.)* on the board in front of everybody. She did not say anything at dinner. She ate her dinner and drank her coffee and went back to the clinic and left the door open and the lamp lit.

He went.

* * *

She was at the table, writing. The ledger she kept for him, the one with the numbers in it. His pulse. His eyes. The cup, in Ciel's words, copied through Rimuru. The cuts and the dates. She did not look up when he came in.

"Sit," she said.

He sat on the clinic bed. The same bed. He had sat on it so many times this summer it had a shape.

She finished the line she was writing. She put the pen down. She turned round on her stool and looked at him.

"Two mornings," she said.

"I know."

"Since the start," said Shoko. "Since floor one. Before every visit, you come here. I look at you. I say *fine*, or I say *not today*." She did not raise her voice. "That's the rule. It's the only rule I asked for. Everything else, I let the house decide."

"I know."

"You didn't come."

"No."

"Why?"

* * *

He looked at the floor.

He knew why. He had known on the first morning, standing at the back door at dawn with the bag over his shoulder, looking across the dark yard at the clinic with no lamp in it yet. He had known she would be up in twenty minutes with the first pot. He had known what she would see when she looked at his eyes. Still not quite right, at the edges, since forty. Still red, a little, when he was tired.

She might have said *not today*.

"Because you might have said no," said Mau.

Shoko did not say anything.

"My eyes," said Mau. "Since forty. They're not right yet. You'd have seen. You'd have said wait a day." He looked at his hands. "I didn't want to wait a day."

"Why not?"

"Because—"

He stopped.

* * *

He had never said it. Not to Frieren. Not on the roof. Not to anyone. It had been in him since the north road, and before that, much longer, since the Trial, folded small, the way the Noise folded things, and kept in the dark.

Shoko was looking at him. Not the clinic look. Not the doctor's look. Just looking.

"In the Trial," said Mau.

She did not move.

"Four hundred and thirty-seven times," said Mau. "The house knows. I told everyone. Every time, she—" He stopped. "Every time."

"I know," said Shoko quietly.

"There's a thing I didn't tell anyone," said Mau. "I didn't know it until the road. I worked it out afterward. In the chair. While she was asleep." He looked at his hands. "The times I waited. The times I stopped. For one second. To be sure. To check. To let someone else go first, because they were stronger, or because I thought she had it, or because somebody said *wait*." He swallowed. "Those were the times she died. Not all of them. A lot of them. More than I can count. Because I can't count them. I tried."

The clinic was very quiet.

"And on the road," said Mau, "I saw her shield go up, and I thought, *she's got it*, and I breathed. One second." He looked up. "It's the same second. It's the same one. It followed me out."

* * *

Shoko did not say anything for a long time.

She picked up her coffee. She did not drink it. She held it in both hands and looked into it.

"I knew someone," she said at last. "Where I come from. A boy. Sixteen. Very loud. Very annoying." She almost smiled. "He was the strongest person in the world. Everyone said so. He said so. It was true."

Mau did not say anything. He knew who she meant. Everyone in the house knew who Shoko meant when she said *a boy, very annoying*.

"He got there by never waiting," said Shoko. "Not for anyone. Not one second. He got so strong that nobody could stand next to him, and then nobody did." She turned the cup in her hands. "I watched it happen. From the infirmary. Every time he came in with something broken, a little further away. And I patched him up and said *fine* and he went back out." She was quiet. "I've thought about that a lot. Whether I should have said *not today* more often."

"Would he have listened?"

"No," said Shoko. "Never. Not once." She looked at Mau. "That's not the point. The point is I'd have said it."

* * *

"I'm not your permission," said Shoko.

Mau looked at her.

"I'm not. I can't be. It's your tower. Your door. Anko looked for a rule and there isn't one, and there shouldn't be." She put the cup down. "I'm your doctor. That's all. Before you go up those stairs, I look at you, and I tell you the truth. *Fine*, or *not today*. And then you decide." She looked at him. "You can go anyway. You've always been able to go anyway. I can't stop you, and I'm not going to try."

"Then why—"

"Because you didn't skip me because you didn't need me," said Shoko. "You skipped me because you were afraid of what I'd say. And that means you already knew." She leaned forward. "The check isn't for me, Mau. It's so that every time you go up there, you've heard someone tell you the truth first. Even if you don't like it. *Especially* if you don't like it."

He did not answer.

"So come," said Shoko. "Every morning. Let me look. Let me say *not today* if it's not today. And then go anyway, if you have to." Her voice did not change. "But don't go without hearing it. Don't make me find out from a board."

* * *

He sat on the clinic bed for a long time.

"Okay," he said at last.

"Okay what?"

"Okay. I'll come." He looked at her. "Every morning."

"And if I say *not today*?"

He was quiet.

"Then I'll hear it," he said.

It was not *I'll stay*. They both knew it. Shoko looked at him for a long moment, and then she nodded, once, as if it were more than she had expected and less than she wanted, and picked up her coffee again.

"Your eyes," she said. "Come here. Into the light."

He went. She looked. For a long time, with the lamp.

"Tomorrow," she said, "is *not today*."

He breathed out.

"Okay," he said.

* * *

Frieren was on the back step when he came out. She had not been listening. She had been sitting on the step in the dark with her knees up, not listening, for exactly as long as he had been in the clinic.

He sat down beside her.

They did not say anything for a while.

Behind them, the clinic door opened. Shoko stood in it with the lamp behind her. She looked at the two of them on the step.

"Frieren," she said.

Frieren turned her head.

"Take him somewhere," said Shoko. "Tomorrow. And the next day, if you can. Not the tower. Not the north field with a shadow out. Somewhere." She looked at Mau, and then back at Frieren. "Anywhere he can't climb."

Frieren looked at her for a long moment.

"I can do that," she said.

Shoko nodded. She went back in. The door stayed open. The lamp stayed lit.

* * *

Fern found out at breakfast, the way the house found out everything.

She did not say anything about it. She opened the ledger, found Mau's column, crooked as ever, and wrote one line under the last entry for the tower, in her small exact hand:

*Day 366. The check. (Every morning. His word.)*

And under it, because Shoko had come to the long table with her coffee and said one sentence to her and gone away again:

*Day 367. Not today. (He heard it.)*

Then she turned to a fresh page in the ledger, a page she had never used before, and at the top of it she wrote a heading.

*Days.*

And under it, nothing yet. A list waiting to be started. Of days that would not be tower days. Days the house would choose, one person at a time, to spend with him, somewhere he could not climb.

She did not tell anyone what the page was for.

By the end of the week, there were six names on it.
