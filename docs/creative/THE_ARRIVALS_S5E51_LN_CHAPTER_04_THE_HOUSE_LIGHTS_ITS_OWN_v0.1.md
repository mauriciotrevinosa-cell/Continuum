*When: Day 368, night. The seventh night. The roof. The hatch has squeaked five times. Below, in Seiko's room, the crackers table is meeting. Above, the stars are out over the hills, and the small steady one is low and a little apart. Shoko is on the roof too, tonight, which she never is, with her watch.*

Shoko came up the ladder after Gojo.

Nobody had ever seen Shoko on the roof on a seventh night. The roof was the five of them. The crackers table was downstairs. That was how the seventh night worked, and had worked since before most of the house had arrived.

She came up through the hatch anyway, with her watch on its chain and her coat over her arm, and stood at the top and looked at the five of them.

"Three minutes," she said.

Nobody said anything.

"I've had a long talk with Ciel," said Shoko. "Through Rimuru. Who didn't want to be in the middle of it." She looked at Rimuru. Rimuru looked at the stars. "The cup's lower than I'd like, after raising the wyvern. Calling him costs once, and he's strong enough to carry you, Ciel says, but he's new, and a mount that's never carried anyone over people is a mount that might drop someone." She looked at Mau. "Three minutes. Low. Inside the lime. Over the house, not past it. And you come down when I say. Not when you feel like it. When I say."

Mau looked at her.

"Over the house," he said.

"Ori asked," said Shoko. "At dinner. She heard about the island. She came to the clinic afterwards and stood in the doorway with her list and asked me. Very politely." She looked at the hatch. "I said I'd think about it. I thought about it. Three minutes."

Below them, the hatch squeaked again.

Ori came up through it. Then Frieren, behind her. Then, a moment later, Milim's head appeared in the hatch, and Yuka's hand on the ribbon round her ankle, holding her back.

"Not you," said Shoko to Milim.

"*Why not?*"

"Because you'd fly round the house fifteen times," said Shoko, "and then into the hills, and then I'd have to explain to Yuka why her ribbon's empty." She pointed. "Sit. With the five. Watch."

Milim looked at Yuka. Yuka looked back, very firmly.

Milim sat.

* * *

Mau stood at the edge of the roof, by the chimney.

He looked down at his shadow. It lay across the roof boards in the light from the hatch, long and thin.

"Kaisel," he said.

The wyvern came up out of the roof.

Not all at once. Carefully. The roof was not a floating island. The dark came up out of the boards beside the chimney and drew itself out: the long neck, the narrow head, the two dark wings folded tight along its sides. It stood on the roof on two legs with its long tail curled round the chimney stack, and looked at Mau with its cold blue eyes.

Taihei, by the draught, made a small sound and held on to the roof.

"That's very big," he said.

"It's smaller than Lantern," said Gojo.

"*Lantern* is very big," said Taihei.

Kaisel looked at Taihei. Then lowered itself, slowly, folding its legs, until its body was low against the roof boards, the way a horse kneels.

Mau climbed on. At the base of the neck, where the wings met the body.

Then he looked at Ori.

* * *

Ori came across the roof.

She did not run. She did not hesitate. She walked across the boards the way she had walked across the wet grass toward Lantern at dawn, the way she had walked across the dry ground toward Igris at dusk. She stopped beside the kneeling wyvern and looked up at it.

Kaisel turned its head and looked down at her.

"Hello," said Ori. "I'm Ori. I wrote you on the list this afternoon. Seven." She paused. "Milim says you fly because you like it."

Kaisel breathed out, very slowly. The air stirred Ori's hair.

"I'd like to fly too," said Ori. "Just once. Over the house. Shoko said three minutes." She looked at Mau. "Is that all right?"

Mau held out his hand.

Ori took it. He pulled her up. She sat in front of him, at the base of the wyvern's neck, small, with his arms round her on either side holding on, the way he had held on over the island.

Frieren came to the edge of the roof.

She did not climb on. She stood beside Kaisel's wing. She looked at Ori, sitting in front of Mau, and at Mau behind her.

"I'll be beside you," she said. "The whole time. On the left." She looked at Ori. "If anything happens, anything at all, you let go of the wyvern and you hold on to me. I'll be there."

"I know," said Ori.

"I know you know," said Frieren. "I'm saying it anyway."

Shoko opened her watch.

"Three minutes," she said. "Starting now."

Kaisel spread its wings.

* * *

It did not go up fast.

It went off the edge of the roof slowly. One beat of its great dark wings, then another. The roof dropped away. Gojo's upturned face. Rimuru's. Yuta's. Taihei holding on to the draught spot with both hands. Milim sitting on her own hands with her mouth open. Shoko with her watch.

Then they were above the house.

Not high. Shoko had said not high. A little higher than the roof. High enough.

Ori did not make a sound.

Mau felt her go very still in front of him, inside his arms. Then he felt her lean forward, a little, the way you lean toward something you want to see better.

And he looked down.

* * *

The house was lit.

All of it. Every window. Mau had never seen it like this. He had seen it from the yard, and from the roof, and from the north field, one side at a time. He had never seen all of it at once, from above, at night.

The long roofs, bark shingles, going off in every direction. The east wing, with the window of the east room lit gold, and the connecting door's window beside it, Ori's. Empty now, because Ori was here. The west wing, going away toward the hills. Window after window. Taihei's room, dark, because Taihei was on the roof. And past it, at the very end, the room with two windows. Both of them lit. One facing the hills and one facing the yard. Someone moving behind the yard one, small, and someone tall beside her.

And past that, at the very end of the west wing, in the dark, two white lines of chalk on the ground. A corner.

The ink tree in the yard. Dark lanterns still hanging in its branches, burned out since the festival, every one. But under it, the Summer Annexe, with a single small lamp left on the blanket. Somebody had forgotten it, or had not forgotten it.

The long room, with the long table, lit, with people still at it. Fern with the ledger. Stark beside her. Seika's sharp head bent over something. And Seiko's room, at the back, lit low, with four shapes round a table and one small one on the windowsill. The crackers table. *Nobody leaves this table carrying what they came in with.*

The workshop, dark. The clinic, with one lamp. Maomao, still awake, measuring something into jars.

The music room, lit, with the shutters open for the cool. And faintly, very faintly, up through the night air, a guitar. The slow part. Just Bocchi. A few notes far apart. *The Room's Full.*

And at the far edge of the timber yard, the three low humps of the kilns, breathing red into the dark. In the bark shelter beside them, three people on the bench. A tall one. A woman. A smaller one, between them, with a cup in both hands.

And beyond all of it, out past the end of the yard, past the north field where the fireflies had been, the white line of the lime. Faint in the starlight, running round everything like a chalk mark. And on top of the red stake, a small dark figure, looking up.

Nazuna waved.

* * *

Ori did not say anything for a long time.

Kaisel turned. Slowly. A long gentle curve over the yard. The ink tree went by below. The long roofs. The wing of the house with the room with two windows. The house went round beneath them, all of it, every lit window, like something turning on a wheel.

Frieren floated beside them on the left, a hand's breadth from the wyvern's wing. She was not looking down. She was looking at Ori.

"Two minutes," came Shoko's voice, from the roof, very faint.

Ori leaned forward a little more.

"The fireflies are gone," she said.

Very quietly. Mau barely heard it over the wind.

"The fireflies are gone," said Ori again. "And the lanterns burned out. All of them. In the tree." She looked down at the house. At every window, lit gold. "Frieren said. When the fireflies are gone, the house lights its own."

Mau did not say anything.

"I thought she meant the lanterns," said Ori. "The festival. I thought that was what she meant. The house lights its own lanterns." She looked at the windows. All of them. The east room. The room with two windows. The long room. The crackers table. The music room. The clinic. The kilns. "But the lanterns burned out." She was quiet. "And it's still lit."

Frieren, beside them, made a very small sound.

"That's what she meant," said Ori. "Isn't it." She looked up at Frieren, floating beside them in the dark. "Not the lanterns. The *house*. All the people in it. Every room." She looked back down. "That's the house lighting its own."

Frieren could not say anything.

Mau saw her face in the starlight. It was doing the thing it did very rarely. Not serene. Not calm. The thing that was a thousand years of meaning something and never quite saying it, and then a child saying it for her, from the back of a wyvern, over a house.

"Yes," said Frieren at last. Her voice was not steady. "That's what I meant."

* * *

"Three minutes," came Shoko's voice. "*Down.*"

Kaisel turned. A long slow curve. Back toward the roof, where five people and a girl on a ribbon and a doctor with a watch were standing up, all of them, watching.

They came down on the roof by the chimney. Kaisel folded its wings. It knelt.

Ori did not get off at once.

She sat at the base of the wyvern's neck with Mau's arms still round her and looked out at the house from the roof. At the lit windows going away along the wings. At the kilns. At the small dark figure on the red stake, still looking up.

Then she climbed down.

She went straight to Shoko.

"Thank you," she said. "For the three minutes."

Shoko closed her watch.

"Was it worth it?"

Ori thought about it. Seriously. The way she thought about everything.

"I saw the whole house," she said. "All of it. At once. Every room." She paused. "It was all lit."

Shoko looked at her for a long time.

"Yes," she said. "It usually is."

* * *

Mau sent Kaisel back.

The wyvern went down into the roof boards all at once, smoothly, the cold blue lights last. The cup was low, from raising him that afternoon and calling him tonight. Lower than he liked. Not empty. There was a margin left. There was always, now, a margin left.

He sat down in his place on the roof.

Yuta handed him a cup. Gojo lay back on his elbows and looked at the stars. Rimuru complained about the draught, which was not his any more, out of habit. Taihei held on to the roof with one hand and his cup with the other and looked at Mau as if he had just watched him do something impossible, which, Mau supposed, he had.

Frieren sat down beside him. Close. Her shoulder against his.

Ori sat down on his other side. Milim floated down to sit beside Ori, and Yuka sat beside Milim with the ribbon, and Shoko sat at the end, by the hatch, with her coat round her shoulders.

Nobody went down.

The roof was not built for ten people. It held. Nobody said anything about the seventh night, or who belonged on the roof and who belonged at the crackers table, or the rule. They sat in the warm dark, all of them, and looked out.

At the house. Every window lit. The kilns breathing red. The music room, and the slow notes far apart. The room with two windows. The chalked corner at the end of the west wing, white in the starlight, for whoever comes.

And beyond it all, low over the hills, a little apart from the others, a small steady star.

Still here.
