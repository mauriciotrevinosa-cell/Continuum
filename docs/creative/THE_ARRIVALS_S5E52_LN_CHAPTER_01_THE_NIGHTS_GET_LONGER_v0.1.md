*When: Day 368, past midnight, into Day 369. The north field. The red stake. The roof has gone quiet. The house is going dark one window at a time. Nazuna has been sitting on top of the stake since a little after dusk, with her coat round her knees.*

Nazuna had waved at the wyvern.

She did not usually wave at things. She sat on the stake and watched. That was what the stake was for. You sat on it, at night, in the north field, at the edge of the lime, and you watched the dark beyond the line for anything that came out of it. Nothing ever did. You watched anyway. That was the job, and she was good at it, and she liked it more than she had ever told anyone.

But the wyvern had come up off the roof of the house, slowly, with two small shapes on its back and an elf floating beside it. It had gone round over the lit windows in a long gentle curve. And Nazuna, sitting on top of the red stake at the edge of everything, had looked up. And before she could decide not to, she had lifted her hand and waved.

The smaller of the two shapes on the wyvern's back had looked down.

Nazuna was almost sure it had looked down.

She sat on the stake now, past midnight, with her coat round her knees, and thought about that.

* * *

The house was going dark.

She watched it go. One window at a time. The roof first, when the hatch squeaked ten times. Ten. She had counted. She did not know who the extra five were and did not need to. Then the long room, when Fern closed the ledger. Then the music room, when the slow notes stopped. Then the clinic, very late, when Maomao finally put down whatever she was measuring.

The room with two windows went dark at one.

The kilns did not go dark. The kilns never went dark. They breathed red at the far edge of the timber yard all night, every night. In the bark shelter beside them, there had been three people on the bench until past midnight. Then two. Then, a little while ago, the smaller one had gone. The woman had carried it, asleep, wrapped in something, back across the timber yard toward the house.

Now there was one person at the kilns. Raking. The way there always had been.

Except that he was not alone, Nazuna thought. Not the way he used to be. He was one person at the kilns because the other two had gone to bed in a room with two windows. That was a different kind of alone. A good kind.

She knew about kinds of alone.

* * *

Anko came on the round at two.

Nazuna heard her from the far end of the line. The particular crunch of Anko's boots on the dry ground, and the way she stopped every hundred paces and looked out past the lime for a count of ten, and then went on. Nazuna had heard it every night for a long time. Since the loft. Since before the loft. She could have told you where Anko was on the line at any moment with her eyes closed.

Anko stopped at the foot of the stake.

She did not say anything. She looked up at Nazuna on top of it. Then she looked out past the lime, at the north field, at the dark, for a count of ten.

Then she sat down at the foot of the stake with her back against it.

"You waved," she said.

"You saw?"

"I was at the bend by the river. I saw the wyvern. I saw you." Anko looked out at the dark. "You never wave."

"I waved," said Nazuna.

Anko did not say anything else for a while.

That was how it was between them. It had always been how it was. Two people who did their jobs at night and shared a room by day, and talked when there was something to say and did not when there wasn't. Nazuna slept in the loft over Anko's bed, and had since the Muslin. They were not a couple. Everyone in the house had wondered, at one point or another, and some had asked. They were not. They were something else, that did not have a word, and neither of them had ever needed one.

"The nights are getting longer," said Nazuna.

Anko looked up at her.

"Are they?"

"Since the fireflies," said Nazuna. "A little every night. The dark comes earlier. The light comes later." She looked at the hills. "Not much. A few minutes. But I notice. It's my time. I notice when there's more of it."

"Summer's turning," said Anko.

"Summer's turning," Nazuna agreed.

She was quiet for a moment.

"I like it," she said. "More night. More of the bit that's mine." She pulled her coat tighter round her knees. "In my world I used to love the end of summer. The nights got long and cool and everyone stayed out later. Everyone was awake when I was awake. For a few weeks, it was like the whole city was mine."

Anko did not say anything.

"It's not a city here," said Nazuna. "But tonight, everyone was up on the roof. Ten of them. Past midnight." She looked at the dark house. "For a bit, it was like that. Everyone awake when I was awake."

* * *

Anko took something out of her coat pocket.

A cup. Small. Clay. One of the chipped house ones from the kiln by the river. She held it up to Nazuna without a word.

Nazuna looked at it.

"What's that?"

"Coffee," said Anko. "Touka's. She made a pot at midnight because Kaneki's shift was starting. She gave me a cup for the round." She held it higher. "I saved half. For you."

Nazuna looked at the cup for a long time.

Then she climbed down off the stake.

She did not usually climb down off the stake until dawn. She climbed down now and sat at the foot of it beside Anko, with her back against it, and took the cup. It was still a little warm. She drank half of the half.

"That's very good," she said.

"Touka says she's not grateful," said Anko. "She says she's just open early."

"Open late," said Nazuna. "Tonight."

"Open late," Anko agreed.

* * *

They sat at the foot of the red stake and looked at the dark beyond the lime.

Nothing came out of it. Nothing ever did. They watched anyway.

"The box," said Nazuna, after a while.

"What about it?"

"Your box. Outside the door. With the thing in it." Nazuna drank the last of the coffee. "It's got four leaves now. I looked, when I came out. Mau watered it this morning. It's got four."

Anko was quiet.

"I know," she said. "I looked too."

"Do you know what it is yet?"

"No."

"Do you want to know?"

"No," said Anko. "I want to find out." She looked at the dark. "There's a difference. Like you and the fireflies. You didn't ask anyone where they were. You went out every night and found them. And then you kept them secret so somebody small could find them too." She paused. "I wrote that down. When Ori told the table."

"You write everything down."

"I write down things that matter," said Anko. "Most things don't. That one did."

Nazuna did not say anything for a long time.

Then she leaned her head back against the red stake and looked up at the stars. Too many of them, strange ones. Low over the hills, the small steady one that Mau had named. Above it, higher, the cluster Frieren had named, *The Party*. Further along, the bright one Senku had called *The Hundred and First*.

"I'm going to count something," she said.

Anko looked at her.

"The fireflies are gone," said Nazuna. "I counted them all summer. Down to the last one. Now there's nothing to count." She looked at the sky. "So I'm going to count the nights. How long they are. How many minutes of dark. Every night, till the end of summer." She paused. "Till it's all mine again."

"You'll need a watch."

"You've got a watch."

"I need my watch for the round."

"You can lend it to me," said Nazuna, "after the round. When you go to bed. I'll give it back at dusk."

Anko considered this.

Then she took the watch off its chain and held it out.

"Don't drop it off the stake," she said.

"I never drop anything off the stake."

"You dropped a shoe off the stake in the spring."

"That was on purpose," said Nazuna. "There was a fox."

Anko looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed. A short, dry sound in the dark. Nazuna had heard it perhaps five times in all the years she had known her.

"There was a fox," said Anko. "I remember. You hit it."

"I hit it," said Nazuna, with dignity.

* * *

Anko went on with the round at three.

She stood up. She looked out past the lime for a count of ten. Then she went off along the white line toward the river bend, her boots crunching on the dry ground, stopping every hundred paces.

Nazuna climbed back up the red stake.

She sat on top of it with her coat round her knees and Anko's watch in her hand. She opened it. In the starlight she could just see the hands. She noted the time. Three. She would note the time again at first light, when the dark began to go. The difference would be the night. Tomorrow it would be a little longer. And the night after that, a little longer still.

She did not write it down. She did not need to. She would remember. She remembered every number she had ever counted.

Thirty-three. Twenty-eight. Twenty. Fifteen. Eleven. Six. Two. One.

Now the nights.

Out past the lime, the dark lay over the north field, empty, the way it always was. Behind her, the house was dark, every window. Except one, at the far edge of the timber yard, where the kilns breathed red, and one man raked the embers, alone in the good way.

Nazuna watched the dark.

It was very quiet. It was hers.

The nights were getting longer.
