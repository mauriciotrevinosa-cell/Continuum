# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 31
## Chapter 2 — A Bead the Size of a Thumb

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 31
**Reading order:** 2
**Continuity state:** The first black-iron test (V30 "Hotter Than That", Day 341). Kaneki's dense charcoal, Wakana's crucible furnace with two bellows, Senku running it, Stark and Taihei pumping, Marin's quilted jacket finished first. The furnace is used once and broken open, and holds a thumb-sized bead of black iron that melted and separated: proof it can be worked, not armour yet. Ram offers her wind as a third bellows for the next furnace, once a week, within her limits. The bead goes to Stark

*When: Day 369, all day. The far end of the timber yard, beside the kilns, where Wakana built the furnace over five days. Fourteen days after Kaneki started the dense charcoal. It has cooled. It rings like a bell when you tap it.*

The furnace stood beside the kilns like a fat clay chimney.

Wakana had built it there on purpose. Close to the charcoal. Close to the heat the kilns already made. Clay and stone, taller than Stark, round, with a belly where the crucible sat and a narrow neck where the heat went up. Two holes at the bottom for two bellows. A door in the side, sealed with clay, that would be broken open at the end.

"Once," said Wakana, at dawn, standing in front of it with his hands black with clay. "We use it once. Then we break it."

"Five days to build it," said Stark. "Once."

"Five days to build it," said Wakana. "And everything we learn when we break it open." He looked at the furnace. "That's what it's for."

Senku had the black iron in a cloth.

Not all of it. A piece. About the size of an egg. Cut from Stark's rock with a chisel and two days of patience. He knelt by the furnace and set it in the crucible, through the door, very carefully. Then he packed charcoal round it. Kaneki's charcoal. The dense kind, from the twisted oak, burned slow for ten days and cooled slow for four. It rang when you tapped it.

Then Wakana sealed the door with wet clay.

"Fire," said Senku.

* * *

It took all day.

Kaneki lit it. He did not usually work in the day; he slept. He had come out at dawn anyway, because it was his charcoal, and he wanted to see it burn. He knelt at the bottom of the furnace and put a coal from the middle kiln into the first bellows hole. Then he blew on it, very gently, the way he blew on the embers at night.

It caught.

Then Stark and Taihei took the bellows.

Two of them. Great leather bags on wooden frames that Wakana had made, one on each side of the furnace, with long handles. You pushed the handle down and air went into the furnace. You pulled it up and the bag filled. Push. Pull. Push. Pull. All day. Stark on one side and Taihei on the other. Stark because he had carried the rock and would not let anyone else do it. Taihei because he had said, at breakfast, *I can't fight a boss with a rope, but I can pump a bellows*, and nobody had argued.

The furnace got hot.

Then hotter.

By noon you could not stand within three paces of it. By the middle of the afternoon the clay at the neck had gone red, and the air above it shimmered so hard the timber yard behind looked like it was underwater. Senku walked round it every quarter of an hour with a wet cloth over his face and his eyes narrowed, looking at the colour of the clay. He did not say *ten billion percent* once all day. Mau noticed that. When Senku stopped saying it, he was working.

At four, Marin came out with the jacket.

* * *

She had finished it the night before.

She held it up in the timber yard, away from the furnace, so everyone could see. A jacket. Thick. Quilted, in rows, the way Wakana had described from his grandfather's pictures. Rows of small even stitches running down it, holding layers of wool and linen between two layers of strong cloth. It had no sleeves. It would go under armour, over a shirt.

"It's for when the iron's ready," said Marin. "If it's ever ready." She looked at Stark at the bellows, sweating, red-faced, pumping. "It's for you. You carried the rock."

Stark looked at the jacket.

He did not stop pumping. Push. Pull.

"It's—" He stopped. "You made that. For me. Before there's even any armour."

"Armour's no good without padding," said Marin. "I told you." She folded the jacket over her arm. "And nobody else was going to make it. So I made it first. So when the iron's ready, the padding's already waiting." She smiled. "That's called planning."

Stark pumped the bellows. He did not say anything for a long time.

"Thank you," he said at last.

"Try it on later," said Marin. "When you're not covered in soot."

She went and stood beside Wakana and watched the furnace.

* * *

At six, Senku said, "Stop."

Stark and Taihei stopped pumping.

Both of them sat down on the ground beside their bellows. Taihei lay back on the dry earth with his arms out, breathing hard, staring at the sky. Stark sat with his head between his knees.

The furnace roared on its own for a while. Then quieter. Then quieter still.

"Now it cools," said Senku. "Slowly. If it cools fast, everything inside cracks." He looked at the red clay at the neck. "Two hours. Then we break it."

Nobody went in to dinner.

Momo brought dinner out. Rem helped her carry it. They set it on the woodpile behind the workshop, and everyone ate standing up in the timber yard, watching the furnace cool from red to dark. Touka brought coffee. Ichika came with her and sat on the woodpile with her small wooden cup, watching the furnace with the same expression she had used to watch her father rake the embers.

At eight, the clay was dark.

Senku touched it with the back of his hand. Then with his palm.

"Break it," he said.

* * *

Wakana broke it.

He did it with a hammer, carefully, at the sealed door in the side. The way you would open something you had made yourself and did not want to break any more than you had to. The clay cracked. He pulled the pieces away. Inside, the crucible. Inside the crucible, under a crust of grey ash where the charcoal had been, something.

Senku reached in with the tongs.

He lifted it out. He held it up in the lamplight that Rem had brought from the kitchen.

It was small.

A bead. Round. About the size of the top of a thumb. Black. Deep, dense, shining, like the black iron always shone. And it was round. Smooth. Not the rough broken shape of the piece he had put in. It had melted. It had pulled itself into a drop and cooled that way.

Behind it, in the crucible, the rest of the egg-sized piece. Still rough. Still black. Unmelted.

Senku looked at the bead for a long time.

"It melted," he said. Very quietly.

Nobody said anything.

"A bit of it," said Senku. "Only a bit. The outside of it. A thumb's worth." He turned the bead in the tongs. "The rest didn't. Not hot enough. Not for long enough." He looked at the unmelted piece in the crucible. Then back at the bead. "But it *can* melt. It *will*. It just needs—"

"Hotter than that," said Wakana.

"Hotter than that," said Senku. And then, for the first time all day, his face split into a grin so wide it nearly reached his ears. "Ten billion percent hotter than that. But it *can*. That's the step. That's the second step. We know it can."

* * *

Ram spoke from the edge of the lamplight.

Mau had not seen her come. She was standing beside Rem, with her arms folded, looking at the broken furnace and the bead in Senku's tongs with an expression of tremendous condescension.

"Wind," said Ram.

Everyone looked at her.

"You need hotter," said Ram. "Hotter means more air. You had two bellows and two sweating men." She looked at Stark and Taihei, still on the ground. "Ram can make wind."

Senku looked at her.

"How much?"

"Not much," said Ram. "Ram's horn is gone. Ram's mana is borrowed, every week, from the elf." She did not look at Frieren. "But Ram can make a steady wind. Into a hole. For an hour. Maybe two." She paused. "Once a week. Not more. Rem will scold Ram if Ram does more."

"Rem will scold Nee-sama," said Rem, very quietly, beside her.

"See," said Ram.

Senku looked at the broken furnace. At the two bellows holes at the bottom.

"A third hole," he said slowly. "At the top of the belly. For a steady wind. On top of the bellows." He looked at Wakana. "Can you build it in?"

"Five days," said Wakana. "For the next one."

"Five days," said Senku. He looked at Ram. "Once a week."

"Once," said Ram. "Ram is generous. Ram is not stupid."

* * *

Senku gave the bead to Stark.

He did not make a speech about it. He walked over to where Stark was still sitting on the ground beside his bellows, covered in soot. He held out the tongs with the bead in them.

"It's cool enough to hold," he said. "Just."

Stark looked at it.

"Why me?"

"You carried the rock," said Senku. "From the iron wood. In both arms. Like a baby." He held the tongs out further. "The first bit of it that ever melted should be yours."

Stark took the bead. It was still warm. He held it in his sooty palm and looked at it in the lamplight. Small. Round. Black. Shining.

"It's not armour," he said.

"It's a bead," said Senku. "That's going to be armour. Eventually." He grinned. "Ten billion percent."

Stark closed his hand round it.

Later, Mau saw Fern take it from him. She looked at it for a long time, and then gave it back. Later still, he saw Stark sitting on the woodpile in Marin's quilted jacket, which fitted perfectly. He was covered in soot and turning the black bead over and over in his fingers. Fern sat beside him with the ledger, writing something.

Mau did not look at what. But the next morning it was on the day's page, in Fern's small neat hand:

*Day 369. The first furnace. Broken open. A bead (Stark). It can melt. Hotter than that. Ram: once a week.*

And in the margin, smaller:

*He wore the jacket to bed. (Don't tell Marin.)*
