# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 31
## Chapter 8 — Three Who Came Late

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 31
**Reading order:** 8
**Continuity state:** Following CANON_TRAPS ("future arrivals still require Mau's permission before further disclosure") and the spirit of DH-099, Mau chooses to give the three newest arrivals (Taihei, Touka, Armin) the same chosen account the later arrivals got on Day 212: the Trial, 437 deaths/resets, the long subjective time, the reward and the coins. He does not give every loop and nobody owes a debt. Frieren is beside him and Ciel watches the guardrails at his request. Each reacts in their own way. No pity performance, no new power

*When: Day 372, afternoon. The long room, with the doors shut, which almost never happens. Frieren has asked the house to leave it for an hour. Fern has put a sign on the door in her small neat hand: AN HOUR. Ori has added underneath, in pencil: PLEASE.*

Mau had decided on the roof.

Not the seventh night. An ordinary night, after Armin came, when he went up alone because he could not sleep and sat in Rimuru's old spot by the draught and looked at the dark house. Three new people in it since the summer started. Taihei, at the end of the west wing, in the room that had said *Came*. Touka and Ichika, in the room with two windows. Armin, for six nights, in Yuta and Maki's spare room, until his own was finished.

None of them knew.

They knew there had been a Trial. Everyone knew that. They knew the coins came from it. Umaru had told Taihei, the first week, on a step. Kaneki had told Touka in pieces at the kilns. Mikasa would have told Armin something, by now.

But they did not know what it had cost. Not the way the others knew. Not the way he had told it on Day 212, in this room, to the eight who came later, when he decided they should hear it from him and not from rumour.

The rule was that later arrivals did not get the inside cost unless he chose.

He had sat on the roof by the draught and chosen.

* * *

The three of them sat at the long table.

Taihei at one end, with his hands folded on the wood the way he folded them at the left end of the counter while the bread rose. Touka in the middle, with a cup, because Touka always had a cup, and her arms folded round it. Armin at the other end, with Anko's pencil behind his ear and the folded map of the house in front of him, because he had not put it down since he finished it.

Mau sat across from them.

Frieren sat beside him. Close. Her shoulder against his.

*I'm watching,* said Ciel. *The guardrails. If anything starts to move that shouldn't, I'll tell you. Before. You can stop whenever you want.*

*I know.*

*I'm saying it so you know I'm here.*

"Thank you for coming," said Mau.

"Fern said it was important," said Taihei. "She said you wanted to tell us something. Yourself." He looked at his folded hands. "She said we didn't have to come."

"You didn't."

"I came," said Taihei.

Touka did not say anything. She drank her coffee.

Armin looked at Mau with his eyes going over Mau's face very fast, the way they went over everything. Reading it. Remembering it.

"It's about the coins," said Armin. "Isn't it."

Mau looked at him.

"Mikasa told me where they come from," said Armin. "Last night. A little. She said there was a test. Before. That you went into. And when you came out, everyone who stayed got a coin." He paused. "She said you'd tell me the rest. If you wanted. She said it was yours to tell."

"It is mine to tell," said Mau. "I want to."

* * *

He told them.

He told it the way he had told it on Day 212, to the eight. Not the way he had told it on the night of Day 5, at the very beginning, to the whole house, all of it, every death, until he could not breathe. Not that way. That way had been for the people who had stood at the wall and waited. This was for the people who came after.

The Trial. A white wall in a plaza. Everyone outside it, waiting. Him inside, alone. Inside it, over and over, he had tried to find a way where the people in this house stayed alive. Standing in front. Running. Staying. Leaving. Asking. It used places he knew and faces he knew, and he could not look out and check which was real.

Four hundred and thirty-seven.

That was the number. He said it plainly. Four hundred and thirty-seven times he had died, or failed, or been reset, and started again. Some of them had lasted seconds. Some had lasted years. One had lasted a very long time. He did not say how long, because he did not know exactly, and it did not matter now.

He did not tell them each one. He did not tell them the wedding. He did not tell them the street of white light with the man and the smile like a door left open. Those were his. Some of them were Frieren's now. He told them that there had been many, and that they had been hard, and that he had come out.

And that when he came out, the Goddess had said there was a reward. A window to go home, or stay. And for everyone who stayed, at sunset on the tenth night, on the bridge, a coin in their palm. Warm. To bring one person. Someone they loved.

"I wasn't doing it to get the coins," he said. "I didn't know there would be coins. I didn't go in to buy anyone." He looked at Frieren beside him. "After the return window closed, the Goddess gave one petition to each person who stayed. She said it had been purchased by the one who stood in the Trial. Four hundred and thirty-seven times." He looked back at the three of them. "That's when I found out."

Nobody said anything.

"And nobody owes me," said Mau. "For being brought. That's the other thing I wanted to say. The house decided that on Day 212. When I told the others. Nobody who came by coin owes me anything. Not thanks. Not work. Not being grateful." He looked at them. "You're here because someone loved you enough to hold a coin and say your name. That's all. That's the whole reason."

* * *

The long room was very quiet.

Through the shut doors, faintly, the house went on. Someone laughing in the yard. A door. Milim's voice saying something, and Yuka's voice saying *shh*, and then quiet again.

Taihei spoke first.

He did not look up from his folded hands on the table. He was looking at them as if they were bread dough he had been told to knead and had not started yet.

"Four hundred and thirty-seven," he said.

"Yes."

"Some of them years."

"Some of them."

Taihei was quiet for a long time.

"I made dinner," he said at last. "Every night. For a year. In case she came home." He looked at his hands. "I thought that was a long time. To keep doing something for someone who wasn't there." He was quiet again. "That's nothing. That's one year. One thing. Dinner." He looked up at Mau for the first time. "You did four hundred and thirty-seven. Some of them years. For people who were standing outside a wall."

"It's not a competition."

"I know it's not a competition," said Taihei. "I'm just—" He stopped. He looked at his hands again. "I'm just understanding why everyone here is so careful about the bread."

Mau did not say anything.

"Umaru told me," said Taihei. "After. In the booth. She said the coin was the twenty-third move. She'd moved it twenty-two times in the winter, in and out of a drawer marked LATER, and the twenty-third was me." He looked at the table. "I thought it was a game thing. Something from her slate. Moves." He was quiet. "I didn't know where the coin had come from. What it had cost before it ever got to her drawer." He was quiet again. "It wasn't a game thing."

"It was a little bit a game thing," said Mau. "She's Umaru."

Taihei almost laughed. He put his hand over his eyes.

* * *

Touka had not moved.

She sat in the middle of the long table with her arms folded round her cup, looking at Mau. Her face was very still. The way Touka's face went very still when she was deciding how angry to be.

"The Goddess," she said.

Mau looked at her.

"The Goddess set this," said Touka. "This test. Four hundred and thirty-seven. Some of them years. For you. Alone. While everyone you loved stood outside a wall." Her voice was very flat. "And when you came out, she said she'd *bought* the coins with what you paid. Like a shop." She put the cup down on the table. Hard. "Like you were the money."

Nobody said anything.

"I ran a shop," said Touka. "For years. I know what it is to sell something. You put a price on it. People pay." She looked at Mau. "You don't make someone pay with four hundred and thirty-seven of their own deaths and then tell them, *by the way, I've spent it on coins for everyone else.*" She stopped. "That's not a shop. That's a—"

She did not finish.

Frieren, beside Mau, was looking at Touka with an expression Mau had seen on her face only a few times. When someone said exactly the thing she had been thinking for a very long time, and had never said, because it had not been hers to say.

"Yes," said Frieren quietly.

Touka looked at her.

"That's what I think too," said Frieren. "I've thought it for nearly a year." She looked at the table. "I've never said it. In this house. Because the coins brought Seika and Ram and Gojo and Yuka and Shoko and Coco's mother and Jiji and Byakuya. And Taihei. And you, and Ichika. And Armin." She paused. "And I didn't want anyone to think I was sorry they came."

"I'm not sorry I came," said Touka.

"I know."

"I'm angry at the price," said Touka. "Not the coin. The price."

"I know," said Frieren. "So am I."

* * *

Touka picked up her cup again.

She looked at Mau over it for a long moment.

"You're as stupid as he is," she said.

"You've said that."

"I'm saying it again," said Touka. "Because it's still true. You went into a wall alone and died four hundred and thirty-seven times looking for a way to keep people alive. And when you came out and found out the price had been spent on coins, you gave yours away too." She drank. "To my husband. So he wouldn't have to choose."

"Yes."

"Stupid," said Touka. And then, much more quietly, not looking at him: "I'm not grateful. I'm still just open early." She looked into the cup. "But I'm going to make your coffee better than anyone else's. Every morning. For as long as I'm here." She paused. "That's not a thank you. That's a decision."

"That sounds like a thank you," said Frieren.

"It's a *decision*," said Touka.

* * *

Armin had not said anything.

He sat at the far end of the long table with the folded map in front of him and Anko's pencil behind his ear. He had listened to all of it. Every word. His eyes had gone over Mau's face the whole time, very fast. Reading. Remembering.

Now he spoke.

"Can I ask one question?" he said.

"Yes."

Armin looked at the map in front of him. At the house, drawn from above, every room. At the room at the end of the west wing, with a window facing south and west. *Toward the sea.*

"When you were in there," he said slowly. "Inside the wall. Alone. Did you think you'd come out?"

Mau did not answer for a long time.

Beside him, Frieren had gone very still.

"No," said Mau at last. Honestly. "Not for a long time." He looked at the table. "There was a festival. Before. Frieren said *next year*, and I said yes, and I didn't think there'd be one." He was quiet. "I kept going anyway. Every time. Looking for the way where they'd all be safe." He looked at Armin. "That's the honest answer."

Armin looked at him for a long time.

Then he looked down at the map. At the house. At every room.

"I know someone like that," he said. Very quietly. "Who went into something thinking he wouldn't come out. Because if he did, everyone else would be safe." He did not look up. "I know a lot of people like that."

Nobody said anything.

Mau did not ask who. He did not ask *what happened*, or *after*, or *in your world*. He saw Armin not say any more. He saw him fold it up and put it somewhere, the way he had folded it up in the intake hall, looking at Eren in the doorway.

"Thank you for telling me," said Armin. "Yourself." He looked up. "Mikasa said it was yours to tell. She was right." He picked up the map. "Can I put something on this?"

"On the map?"

"On the house," said Armin. He unfolded it on the table. He took Anko's pencil from behind his ear. He found the long room, where they were sitting. He wrote something very small beside it, in his neat careful hand.

He turned it round so Mau could see.

*Here, on Day 372, someone told three people the truth because he chose to.*

Mau looked at it for a long time.

"You don't have to put that on a map," he said.

"Maps are for knowing where things are," said Armin. "And what happened there. So the next person knows." He folded the map up again. "The next person who comes by coin. They'll need to know where the long room is. And what happened in it."

* * *

When the hour was up, Fern took the sign off the door.

The three of them went out into the yard. Taihei to the kitchen, because it was nearly time to start dinner and the left end of the counter was his. Touka to the room with two windows, where Ichika was waiting with Rem. Armin to find Mikasa and Eren, with the map under his arm.

Mau sat at the long table with Frieren for a while after.

"That was easier," he said. "Than Day 212."

"You've told it twice now," said Frieren. "The chosen way. Not the whole way." She leaned against his shoulder. "It gets easier to choose what to say. That's not the same as the thing getting smaller."

"No."

"But it's something."

"It's something," said Mau.

*The guardrails didn't move,* said Ciel. *Not once. The whole hour.* A pause. *I'm writing that down.*
