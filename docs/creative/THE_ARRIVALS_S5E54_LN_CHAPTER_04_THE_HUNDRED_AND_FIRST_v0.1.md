# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 31
## Chapter 13 — The Hundred and First

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 31
**Reading order:** 13
**Continuity state:** The seventh-night roof (Day 375 = 81 + 7x42). Senku and Byakuya bring the telescope up for Armin, as promised, so the five (Rimuru, Yuta, Gojo, Taihei, Mau) have guests. Senku explains *The Hundred and First* in his own words from V29 "First Light" ("Because it isn't finished. Like the story. We're still in it."). Armin sees "the ones behind the ones" and asks about the turning sky on nineteen. Taihei and Armin, both late arrivals, share the draught. The crackers table meets downstairs as usual. No threat

*When: Day 375, night. The seventh night. The hatch has squeaked eight times, which is three more than usual. Downstairs, in Seiko's room, the crackers table has put out the crackers.*

Senku came up the ladder with the telescope on his back.

He had wrapped it in a blanket and tied it with cord and slung it over his shoulder like a rifle. He came up through the hatch one rung at a time, very slowly, because the telescope was worth more to him than anything in the house except possibly Byakuya. Byakuya came up behind him carrying the three wooden legs.

And behind Byakuya, carefully, the way a person climbs a ladder onto a roof for the first time, Armin.

The five of them were already there. Rimuru, complaining about the draught that was not his any more. Yuta, with the cups, eight of them tonight on a bigger tray, because he had been warned. Taihei, by the draught, holding on. Gojo, who had arrived on time for once, because Senku had told him at dinner there would be a telescope and he wanted to look at Senku's face while Senku set it up.

Mau, in his place.

Armin stopped at the top of the hatch and looked round.

At the stars. Too many of them. Strange ones. At the dark yard below, and the ink tree, and the long roofs going off in every direction. At the five people sitting on the flat part of the roof with cups.

"Oh," said Armin.

"Everyone says that," said Rimuru. "Sit down. Not there. That's Gojo's. Not there either. That's—"

"He can have the draught," said Taihei.

Everyone looked at him.

"I had the draught," said Taihei. "When I came. Umaru said. It's tradition. The new one gets the draught." He moved over, slightly, so there was room beside him by the draught. "It's very drafty. But you get used to it."

Armin looked at him.

"You came by coin too," he said.

"Umaru's," said Taihei. "In the spring. No. Summer. Early summer." He thought about it. "I was making curry. I'd just put the onions in."

Armin sat down by the draught, beside Taihei.

"I was looking for them," he said. "With a lamp. And a map." He looked at his hands. "I dropped the lamp. In the hall. It cracked."

"I dropped a spoon," said Taihei. "Wooden. It didn't crack." He paused. "Wakana kept it. It's on a shelf in the workshop. He says it's the first thing that came through by coin that wasn't a person."

Armin almost laughed.

"Is the lamp anywhere?"

"Ask Wakana," said Taihei. "He keeps everything."

* * *

Senku set up the telescope.

It took a long time. He unwrapped it from the blanket very carefully, like something asleep. He set the three legs Byakuya handed him, one at a time, and adjusted them on the uneven roof boards until the brass plate at the top was perfectly level. Then he fitted the tube. Rolled copper, beaten thin, joined with a seam that had been soldered and re-soldered. Then the eyepiece. Then he crouched beside it with a lantern turned down so low it was nearly out, and adjusted a screw.

Gojo watched his face the whole time.

"He's very happy," said Gojo, to nobody.

"Shut up," said Senku, without looking up.

"I'm just observing. You told me I'm very good at observing."

"I told you you're very good at *seeing*," said Senku, adjusting the screw. "Observing requires a hypothesis." He turned the screw a hair. "Done."

He stood back.

He looked at Armin.

"First light was Suika," he said. "Because she wanted to see. Tonight's yours. Because you asked about the sky on nineteen." He gestured at the eyepiece. "Look."

* * *

Armin looked.

He put his eye to the small fort-glass eyepiece, carefully, the way Suika had, without touching the tube. He did not say anything for a long time.

The roof was quiet.

"There's more," said Armin at last. Very softly. "Than you can see. With your eyes. There are—" He stopped. "There are ones behind the ones. Small ones. Hundreds. Where there's just dark when you look up. They're all—"

Byakuya made a small sound.

Senku did not say anything. But Mau saw his face in the very low light of the lantern. It was doing something it had done once before. On the night of first light, on this roof, when Suika took her glasses off and put her eye to the eyepiece and said almost exactly that.

"Suika said that," said Byakuya, very quietly. "On the first night. *There are ones behind the ones.*"

Armin lifted his head from the eyepiece.

"Did she?"

"Almost word for word," said Byakuya.

Armin looked up at the sky. With his eyes. At the stars, too many of them, and the dark between them.

"Then it must be true," he said. "If two people who'd never met said it the same way."

Senku looked at him for a long moment.

"That," he said, "is a ten billion percent scientific way of thinking about it."

* * *

"Which one's yours?" said Armin.

Senku pointed. Low in the east. Bright. Steady.

"*The Hundred and First*," he said.

Armin found it through the telescope. He looked at it for a long time.

"Why that name?"

Senku did not answer at once.

He stood beside the telescope with his hands in his pockets and his hair sticking up in the dark, looking at the bright star low in the east. Byakuya, beside him, did not say anything. He did not need to.

"My father told stories," said Senku at last. "When I was small. A hundred of them. One after another." He did not look at Byakuya. "And then he stopped, because there weren't any more, and I grew up, and a lot happened." He looked at the star. "And then I came here. And I used my coin. And he came. And I stood on this roof on the night of first light, and everyone named a star, and I thought—"

He stopped.

"Because it isn't finished," he said. To the sky. Not to anyone. "Like the story. We're still in it." He paused. "That's the hundred and first. The one after the last one. The one that's still going."

Nobody said anything.

Byakuya had put his hand on Senku's shoulder. Just for a moment. Then he took it away.

Armin looked at the bright star low in the east for a long time.

"I like that," he said quietly. "A story that isn't finished."

"Ten billion percent," said Senku. His voice was not quite steady. "Me too."

* * *

Then Armin asked about nineteen.

He asked about it the way Mau had seen him ask Anko about the board. Fast. Precise. One question after another. How fast did it turn. Around which point. Did the point stay still or move. How long did it take one star to go all the way round. Did anyone measure.

"Frieren drew it," said Senku. He took the folded paper out of his breast pocket. The drawing. The turning sky, the arcs, the still star at the centre. He unfolded it on the roof boards beside the telescope, and Byakuya held the lantern over it.

Armin looked at it for a long time.

"If it turns around one point," he said slowly, "and the point stays still, then it's like ours. Like this sky." He looked up. "This sky turns too. Around something. Slower. You can't see it unless you watch for hours." He looked back at the drawing. "So whoever built the sky on nineteen built a copy. Of a real one. But faster." He paused. "Why faster?"

Senku looked at him.

"Ten billion percent," he said, "that's the question I've been asking for a month."

"What's the answer?"

"I don't know," said Senku. "Yet." He grinned, the very wide one. "But now there's two of us asking."

Gojo, lying back on his elbows, looked at the two of them bent over Frieren's drawing in the lamplight.

"Oh no," he said. "There's two of them."

"There were already two of us," said Byakuya mildly.

"There's *three* of them," said Gojo, to the stars. "I'm going to have to start going to bed early."

* * *

Later, when Senku had wrapped the telescope back up in its blanket and Byakuya had gathered the legs, Armin stayed by the draught.

Taihei was still beside him. The other three had gone back to their places. Rimuru, complaining. Gojo, lying back. Yuta, collecting the cups.

Mau sat down on Armin's other side.

He did not say anything for a while. Neither did Armin. They looked at the stars. Low over the hills, a little apart, the small steady one.

"Which one's yours?" said Armin.

Mau pointed.

"*Still Here*," he said.

Armin found it. He looked at it for a long time.

"That's a good name," he said.

"Frieren's is *The Party*," said Mau. He pointed higher. The cluster. "Umaru named two. *The Ministry*, and *Taihei (later)*." He pointed at a faint one near the horizon. "She named that one before he came."

Taihei, on Armin's other side, made a small sound.

"She named a star after me?"

"Before you came," said Mau. "On first light. *Taihei (later)*." He paused. "She was very sure there'd be a later."

Taihei did not say anything for a long time. He looked at the faint star near the horizon.

"I didn't know," he said.

"She didn't tell you?"

"She doesn't tell me things," said Taihei. "She lets me find them." His voice was very quiet. "That's how she's always been." He looked at the star. "*Taihei (later)*."

Armin looked at the faint star. Then at the man beside him, by the draught, looking at it.

"Someone named a star after you," he said, "before they knew you'd come."

"Yes."

Armin was quiet.

"Mikasa didn't name one after me," he said. "I wasn't here yet. On first light."

"There are a lot of stars," said Mau. "Nobody's named most of them."

Armin looked up at the sky. Too many stars. Strange ones. Hundreds behind the hundreds, the ones you could only see through the telescope.

"I could find one," he said slowly. "And name it. Not after me." He thought about it. "After the sea. The one Eren wants to take us to. A sea that's just a sea."

"That's a good name for a star," said Taihei.

"I'll find it tomorrow," said Armin. "With Senku. Through the telescope." He looked at the sky. "One that nobody's named yet."

* * *

They went down at midnight.

Gojo first. Then Yuta with the tray. Then Senku, with the telescope on his back, very slowly. Then Byakuya with the legs. Then Rimuru, complaining about the ladder. Then Taihei, carefully, getting better at it.

Then Armin.

He stopped at the top of the hatch and looked back at the roof. At the stars. At the house below, going dark.

"Mau," he said.

"Mm."

"Is it always like this? The roof?"

Mau thought about it.

"Every seventh night," he said. "Since before most of the house came." He paused. "Usually it's just five of us. Tonight it was eight."

Armin looked at the roof.

"Can I come again?" he said. "Not every time. Sometimes."

"Ask Taihei," said Mau. "It's his draught now. He's the one who'll have to share it."

From below, on the ladder, Taihei's voice: "He can have it."

Armin went down.

Mau sat alone on the roof for a moment. Then he went down too. The hatch squeaked behind him, the ninth time that night. Downstairs, in Seiko's room, the crackers table was still going. Through the door, faintly, he heard Frieren laugh at something Seiko said, and Turbo Granny say she was not participating.
