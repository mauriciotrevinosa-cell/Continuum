## Chapter 17 — Facing the House

*When: Day 379, dusk. A rest day; the cup is refilling, nothing can come out of Mau's shadow until tomorrow. The vermilion columns at the gate of the real house. Himmel is standing between them. He is facing the wrong way.*

Mau came out with two cups of tea.

He had not planned to. He had been in the kitchen when Touka asked if he wanted coffee. He had said *tea, please, two*, before he knew he was going to, and Touka had looked at him and then out of the window toward the front of the house, and made two cups of tea without asking who the second was for.

"I'm not grateful," she said, handing them over.

"You're open late," said Mau.

"I'm open late," Touka agreed, and went back to the stove.

He carried the two cups out through the long room and the front door. Under the vermilion columns at the gate. The way he had carried two cups out once before, at dusk, a long time ago now. Seventeen days after the bell, in the spring.

Himmel was standing between the columns.

Mau stopped.

* * *

Himmel was facing the house.

Not the road. The road went away behind him, white and dry, toward the hills. He had his back to it. He was standing between his two red columns with his hands behind him and his cape hanging still, looking at the front of the real house. At the long roof. At the windows coming on gold, one by one, as the light went.

He had stood between those columns every day since the house was built. At dawn, mostly. Sometimes at dusk. Always facing the road. Everyone knew. It was where heroes faced.

He was facing the house.

He heard Mau. Of course he did. He turned his head.

He looked at Mau standing in the doorway with two cups of tea.

His face did something. Not much. Something at the corner of his mouth. The thing that comes before a smile when the person is not sure yet that they are going to let it happen.

"You brought tea," he said.

"I brought tea."

"Again."

"Again," said Mau.

He came down the step and across to the columns. He gave Himmel one of the cups. Himmel looked at it. Then took it.

They stood between the red columns and drank.

* * *

Mau did not turn round to face the road.

He stood beside Himmel, facing the same way. The house. The long roof. The windows. In one of them, upstairs, at the end of the east corridor, Ori's window. Lit. A small shape moving behind it, and a smaller one floating above it on a ribbon. In another, the long room, where Fern was at the head of the table with the ledger. In another, the kitchen, where Touka was at the stove and Taihei at the left end of the counter. Momo was between them, saying something that made Taihei laugh.

It was very quiet.

"She laughs more," said Himmel, after a while.

Mau did not need to ask who.

"Yeah," he said.

"On the slope," said Himmel. "When the ant went into the sky. And on the roof, the night of the wyvern. I could hear her from the north field." He drank his tea. "And at breakfast. All the time now. At things that aren't even very funny."

"Gojo," said Mau.

"Gojo is never very funny," said Himmel. "She laughs anyway." He was quiet for a moment. "It's a good laugh. I always thought it would be. If someone ever got it out of her properly."

Mau did not say anything.

"Good," said Himmel.

That was all. That was the whole of it.

* * *

They stood there for a long time.

The windows came on one by one. The music room. The clinic. At the end of the west wing, the room with two windows, and past it, the new frame. Armin's. Nearly finished now, with its window facing south and west, toward the sea. Wakana was still working on it by lamplight. You could see him from here, small, at the far end of the house, with Taihei holding the lamp for him.

"You turned round," said Mau.

Himmel did not answer at once.

"At the gate," said Mau. "You used to face the road. Every morning. Everyone knew."

"I know," said Himmel.

"You're facing the house."

Himmel looked at the house for a long time.

"I stand Mikasa's hour," he said. "Every other day. At the north end. Facing the field." He drank his tea. "And I found out I like it. Facing something that isn't a road. Watching for nothing to come, so two people can sit in a field." He was quiet. "And then I came back here, one evening, to the columns. Out of habit. And I went to face the road, the way I always do." He paused. "And I didn't want to."

Mau looked at him.

"So I turned round," said Himmel. "And looked at the house. And it was—" He stopped. He looked at the windows. "It was very nice to look at. All the windows. All the people in them." He almost smiled. "I'd been standing with my back to it for a long time. Looking at an empty road. Waiting for something that already came and went past me to someone else." He drank his tea. "And the whole time, behind me, there was this."

He lifted his cup a little, toward the house.

"I'm not *better*," he said. "I want to be clear. I'm not standing here telling you I'm all better and you two can go and get married tomorrow and I'll dance at it." He looked at Mau sideways. "I'm not there. Some days the shelf's still lower than I'd like."

"I know."

"But I turned round," said Himmel. "That's something."

"That's something," said Mau.

* * *

They stood there.

After a while, Mau shifted his weight. He did not mean anything by it. He had been standing in one place for a long time, and his legs were tired from the furnace-hall on seventy, still, two days later. He shifted onto one foot and put his free hand on his hip without thinking, and looked at the house.

"Nice pose," said Himmel.

Mau turned his head.

"You didn't look," he said.

"I heard your coat," said Himmel.

Mau stared at him.

"I don't have a cape," he said.

"I know you don't have a cape," said Himmel. "That's why I said coat." He drank his tea, very calmly, looking at the house. "You stood like a hero for one moment. With your hand on your hip. And your coat moved. In the wind." He paused. "I've been posing for a very long time. I know the sound of a coat in the wind when someone's posing."

"I wasn't posing."

"You were posing a little," said Himmel.

Mau looked at him.

At the hero between his two red columns, with his cape hanging still and his tea in his hand and his face doing nothing at all. Facing the house.

Then Mau started to laugh.

He could not help it. It came out of him the way it had come out of him the first time, a long time ago, at dusk, between these columns, when he had said *I heard your cape*. And Himmel, beside him, after a moment, started to laugh too. The real one. The one that came out of him easily his whole life, loud and helpless and completely free. The two of them stood between the vermilion columns in the dusk and laughed, facing the house, with their tea going cold.

* * *

When they had finished, Himmel wiped his eyes with the back of his wrist.

"She's going to ask what that was," he said. "Frieren. She'll have heard. She hears everything."

"She'll ask," said Mau.

"Tell her I said *nice pose*," said Himmel. "And that you didn't look." He paused. "She'll understand."

He finished his tea. He gave Mau the empty cup.

"Thank you," he said. "For the tea. Again."

"Thank you for turning round," said Mau.

Himmel looked at him for a long moment.

Then he took the brush out of his coat and brushed his hair. Once. Not because it needed it.

"Tomorrow is my day," he said. "At the north end. If you want to bring tea there, I'll be facing the field." He put the brush away. "It's a better view than the road."

He went in.

Mau stood between the columns alone for a moment, with two empty cups, facing the house.

Then, because nobody was watching, he turned round and looked at the road. White. Dry. Empty. Going away toward the hills.

It was not a very good view, he decided.

He turned back round and went in. Frieren was at the bottom of the stair. She had clearly heard everything and was pretending she had just happened to be standing there.

"What was that?" she said.

"He said *nice pose*," said Mau. "I didn't look."

Frieren looked at him for a long moment.

Then her ears went pink at the tips, and she took his hand, and they went up.
