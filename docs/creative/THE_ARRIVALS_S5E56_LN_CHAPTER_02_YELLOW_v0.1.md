# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 31
## Chapter 21 — Yellow

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 31
**Reading order:** 21
**Continuity state:** R54, initiated by Frieren. She takes Mau to the river bend and casts her teacher's spell for a field of flowers (Frieren source canon: the first spell she loved), choosing yellow on purpose, like the south field, not the white and blue of the Trial wedding. It is "counting" from R57: one small word of the memory looked at in daylight with someone beside him. No frost comes. They talk about ordinary future things (next year's festival, the sea with Armin, learning the green book together). There is no proposal and no wedding talk beyond a single honest line

*When: Day 380, late morning into afternoon. A rest day. Lantern is still in pieces; the cup is half full and refilling fast. Frieren came down to breakfast with a basket and did not explain it. Shoko asked her, two weeks ago, to take him somewhere he can't climb. Two days after seventy, he cannot climb anything. She floats him most of the way.*

Frieren had a basket.

She came down to breakfast with it over her arm. A real basket, from the kitchen, the kind Momo carried bread in, with a cloth over the top. She set it down at the end of the long table beside her place and did not say anything about it. She ate her porridge.

Mau looked at the basket.

Then at Frieren.

"What's that?" he said.

"A basket," said Frieren.

"What's in it?"

"Lunch," said Frieren. She ate her porridge. "And a blanket." She paused. "And the green book. And your coat, because you'll forget it."

Mau looked at her.

"Are we going somewhere?"

Frieren put down her spoon. She looked at him. Her ears had gone very faintly pink at the tips.

"I'm taking you somewhere," she said. "Today. You don't have to go into the tower. The cup is half full and Lantern's in pieces, and Shoko says rest." She looked at the basket. "So I'm taking you somewhere to rest. It's my idea." She paused. "I've been planning it. Since the dragon."

At the far end of the long table, Seiko put down her tea and looked extremely pleased with herself for no reason she was prepared to explain.

* * *

She took him to the river bend.

Past the workshop. Past the bench Ori had drawn three faces for. Past the fork where the path went down to the water. Along the bank under the trees to the bend, where the river widened and slowed and went clear over pale stones. The place Gojo had taken Shoko, the first time. The place Frieren had taken Fern, with the green book.

She did not stop there.

She went on, past the bend, a little further, to a place where the trees stepped back from the bank and there was a wide flat stretch of grass going down to the water. Long grass, pale, dry from the summer. A few stones. Nothing else. Open sky.

She stopped in the middle of it.

She put down the basket. She took out the blanket and spread it on the grass. She did all of this without saying anything. Mau stood at the edge of the grass and watched her.

Then she straightened up and looked at the dry pale grass, all round them, going down to the river.

"Sit down," she said.

He sat down on the blanket.

She did not sit down. She stood in the middle of the dry grass with her staff in her hand and looked at it for a long time. The way she looked at a spell before she cast it.

"Frieren?"

"Wait," said Frieren.

* * *

She lifted her staff.

Not high. Not the way she lifted it for Zoltraak. Low. Loosely. The way you hold something you have held a great many times and do not have to think about.

"This was the first spell I ever loved," she said. Not to him. To the grass. "My teacher's favourite. A long time ago. Longer than you'd believe." She was quiet for a moment. "It doesn't do anything useful. It never has. She used to say that was the whole point of it."

Then she cast it.

Mau did not see anything happen at first. No light. No sound. Frieren stood with her staff held low and her eyes half closed.

Then, at her feet, in the dry pale grass, a flower opened.

Small. Yellow. On a thin green stem, coming up out of the dry grass where there had been no stem a moment before. It opened all at once, the way a hand opens.

Then another, beside it.

Then a dozen. Then a hundred. Then more than he could count. Opening out from where she stood, in a ring, wider and wider. Through the dry grass, across the whole flat stretch to the trees on one side and the river on the other. Yellow. All of them. Small, bright, yellow, on thin green stems, until the whole wide stretch of grass by the river was a field of them. Moving very slightly in the breeze off the water, under the open sky.

Frieren lowered her staff.

She stood in the middle of the field she had made, up to her knees in yellow flowers, and did not look at him.

* * *

Mau sat on the blanket.

He looked at the flowers.

He waited for it to come. He knew it would. Flowers, everywhere, a field of them, made by magic, under an open sky. He knew exactly what was going to come out of the back of his head where he had kept it. Frost. White and blue. Everyone coming up through the trees. A seam in the sky.

He waited.

It did not come.

He looked at the flowers. Yellow. Small. Bright. Moving in the breeze. Not white. Not blue. Yellow, like the small flowers that had grown in the south field this summer, where the dead earth came back green. Where Eren and Mikasa sat on Himmel's days.

He looked at Frieren, standing in the middle of them up to her knees.

"Yellow," he said.

Frieren did not turn round.

"I know," she said.

"You chose yellow."

"I chose yellow," said Frieren. She was looking at the flowers. "I can make them any colour. I've made them every colour, over the years. My teacher liked them all colours at once." She was quiet. "I chose yellow."

"Why?"

Frieren turned round.

She looked at him across the field of flowers. Her face was very still. Her ears were pink.

"Because the ones in there were white and blue," she said. "You told the house. The night you came back. I remember every word." She looked down at the flowers round her knees. "White and blue. In the frost. You made every one." She looked back up at him. "So these are yellow. Like the south field. Like something that grew back."

Mau could not say anything.

"*Count it first*," said Frieren. "I said that. In the east room. After Seiko. After the garden." She was quiet. "Find the parts that keep coming back. Look at them one at a time. In the daylight. With someone sitting next to you." She looked at the flowers. "Flowers are one of the parts. A field of them. Made by someone." She looked at him. "So I made a field. In the daylight. And I made it a different colour. So you could look at the part, and not the whole thing."

* * *

Mau sat on the blanket and looked at the yellow field for a long time.

He looked at it the way she had said. One part. Not the whole thing. A field of flowers, made by someone. In the daylight. With someone standing in the middle of it.

He could look at it.

That was the strange thing. He could look at it, and his chest did not close, and his hands did not go cold. There was a feeling. There was always going to be a feeling, he thought. Something in the back of his head, turning over, remembering. But it stayed in the back of his head. It did not come out and stand in front of everything.

"The ones in there," he said slowly. "I made them. With my own mana. After years. After failing and failing." He looked at the yellow flowers. "I was so proud of them. Every single one. And then—"

He stopped.

Frieren did not say anything. She waited.

"And then the sky opened," said Mau. "And they got cut. With everything else." He looked at the flowers. "I didn't think about the flowers, after. Not once. I thought about everyone. About you. About the space that wouldn't let me move." He was quiet. "I never thought about the flowers. That I'd made them, and they got cut too."

"Now you have," said Frieren.

"Now I have."

"That's one," said Frieren. "One small word." She came across the field toward him, through the yellow flowers, up to her knees. "You looked at it. In the daylight. With someone here." She stopped at the edge of the blanket. "That's counting."

* * *

She sat down beside him.

Close. Her shoulder against his. She took the cloth off the basket. Bread, from Momo. Cheese, from somewhere Hollis had brought it. Two pears. A flask of Touka's coffee, still hot. The green book, from the drowned library, with the pale flower from five pressed in its first page.

And his coat.

"You'll forget it," she said, putting it round his shoulders. "You always forget it."

"It's summer."

"It's the end of summer," said Frieren. "The nights are getting longer. Nazuna says." She handed him a pear. "Eat."

He ate.

They sat on the blanket in the middle of the yellow field by the river, and ate bread and cheese and pears, and drank Touka's coffee. They did not talk about the Trial. They did not talk about the wedding, or the frost, or the seam in the sky.

They talked about ordinary things.

"Next year," said Frieren. "The festival. When the fireflies go." She looked at the river. "Ori wants a bigger stall for the curry. Umaru's already drafting a petition."

"Taihei will make it slightly worse again."

"He'll fail again," said Frieren. "Momo will rule it acceptable."

"Armin wants to see the sea," said Mau. "Eren promised him. A sea that's just a sea." He looked at the river, going away round the bend. "Someday. When it's safe that far out."

"We went once," said Frieren. "The whole house. The family trip." She was quiet. "A long time ago now."

"I remember."

"I'd like to go again," said Frieren. "When Armin goes. With Eren and Mikasa." She looked at him. "With you."

"Yes," said Mau.

He meant it.

* * *

"What do you want to do?" said Frieren.

Mau looked at her.

"I mean it," she said. "Not the tower. Not the floors. Not getting stronger." She looked at the green book in her lap. "You always ask me what I want. What grimoire. What spell. What I'd like to do on a rest day." She paused. "I never ask you. Not properly. So I'm asking. What do *you* want to do? This summer. After the tower. Anything."

Mau thought about it.

He thought about it for a long time. The way she would have. Every part.

"That," he said.

He pointed at the green book in her lap.

Frieren looked down at it.

"The book?"

"I want to learn to read it," said Mau. "With you. Counting the marks. Finding the small words." He looked at the book. "You and Fern found the most common ones. At the river. You wrote them down in two columns. In two hands." He paused. "I'd like to be the third hand."

Frieren did not say anything for a long time.

Then she opened the green book on her knees. To the first page. To the hook at the top, with the pale flower from five pressed beside it. She took out the slip of Fern's ledger paper, the two columns of marks in two hands. She found a pencil in the basket.

She held the pencil out to him.

"Third column," she said.

Mau took the pencil.

* * *

They counted until the light went gold.

The yellow flowers stayed. Frieren's flowers always stayed, she said, until they faded on their own. These would last a few days, and then go back into the grass, and the grass would be grass again. Nobody would know they had been there, except the two of them.

"And Seiko," said Mau. "She'll know. She always knows."

"She'll know," Frieren agreed.

When they packed up the basket, there was a third column on Fern's ledger slip. In Mau's hand. Uneven. A little crooked, because he had never done this before. Twelve marks, from the first twelve pages, that came up more often than the rest.

Small words, probably.

Something for the big words to hang on.

On the walk back, along the river bank, past the bend, Frieren took his hand. She did not let go until they reached the yard. At the yard, she did not let go either.

At the back step, Ori was waiting.

She had the list. *SHADOWS (NAMED)*. And something else. A page of her own. Written out in her careful printing, the way she wrote out plans.

"Tomorrow," she said, before either of them could say anything. "I have a plan. About Lantern." She held up the page. "It's my plan. Not a fort plan. Mine."

Frieren looked at the page.

Then at Mau.

Then she sat down on the back step, still holding his hand, and pulled him down beside her.

"Tell us," she said.

* * *

Fern did not need to be told. She saw the yellow on Frieren's sleeve at dinner, a single petal caught in the cuff, and the way Mau sat a little closer to her than usual, and she opened the ledger at the page called *Days* and found the line in Frieren's own untidy hand.

*Frieren. The river bend. A spell I haven't cast for anyone.*

She did not write *done*. She looked at it for a while, and then she wrote, small, under it:

*Cast.*
