# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 31
## Chapter 24 — Ram's Wind

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 31
**Reading order:** 24
**Continuity state:** The second black-iron furnace (Day 382), five days after it was designed. Kaneki's dense charcoal, two bellows (Stark and Taihei), and a third hole for Ram's steady wind, once a week within her borrowed mana; Rem watches and stops her at the hour. The egg-sized piece melts fully. Wakana and Senku pour it into a clay mould: one palm-sized black plate, the first finished piece, which goes over the heart on Stark's quilted jacket. Armour is still mostly to come. The seventh night falls that evening (roof and crackers)

*When: Day 382, all day. The far end of the timber yard, beside the kilns, where a second fat clay chimney stands. Wakana built it in five days, as he said. It has three holes at the bottom now, not two. The third is narrower, angled upward into the belly. Ram looked at it for a long time yesterday and said, "Adequate."*

Ram came at noon.

Not at dawn, with everyone else. She had said she would come at noon, when the furnace was already hot, and not before. "Ram does not waste wind on a cold furnace," she had said. Nobody argued with Ram about wind.

She came across the timber yard with Rem a step behind her, the way Rem walked behind her everywhere. She stopped three paces from the furnace, which was as close as anyone could stand now. She looked at it with an expression of tremendous condescension.

The furnace was roaring.

It had been roaring since dawn. Kaneki had lit it, again, with a coal from the middle kiln, and blown on it, and stepped back. Stark and Taihei had been on the bellows since. Push. Pull. Six hours. Stark in Marin's quilted jacket, soaked through. Taihei in his apron, because he had come straight from making breakfast and had not thought to change.

The clay at the neck was red. The air above it shimmered so hard the kilns behind looked underwater.

"Not hot enough," said Senku. He was walking round it with a wet cloth over his face, the way he had walked round the first one. "Same as last time. Hot. Not hot enough." He stopped by the third hole. The narrow one, angled up. "That's what the third hole's for."

Ram looked at the third hole.

"Ram knows what the third hole is for," she said. "Ram designed it."

"Wakana designed it."

"Wakana built it," said Ram. "Ram told him where."

Wakana, standing beside the furnace with his hands black, did not say anything. But he nodded, very slightly, which meant Ram was right.

* * *

Ram stepped up to the third hole.

She did not hurry. She stood in front of it with the heat coming off the furnace in waves, and her hair moving in it, and her face perfectly calm.

Rem stood behind her.

"One hour, Nee-sama," said Rem. Very quietly. "Rem will count."

"Ram knows," said Ram.

"One hour."

"*Ram knows*," said Ram, and lifted her hand.

* * *

Mau had never seen Ram use her wind.

He had heard about it. In the clock tower on forty-three, Ram had fixed the clocks with one breath. He had not been there. He had heard that in her own world, before, with her horn, Ram's wind could cut a forest down. That was a long time ago and a long way away. Ram's horn was gone. Ram's mana was borrowed, every week, from Frieren, who sat with her and filled her the way you fill a lamp.

He watched her now.

She did not do anything dramatic. She lifted her hand, palm out, toward the narrow third hole. And there was wind.

Not a gust. A stream. Steady. Narrow. Mau could see it, because the heat-shimmer bent round it. A thin line of moving air going out of Ram's open palm and into the third hole of the furnace. Straight. Unwavering. Like a needle of wind.

The furnace changed.

He heard it. The roar went up. Not louder. Higher. A different note, like a kettle just before it sings. The red clay at the neck went orange. Then yellow at the very top, where the heat came out.

Senku stopped walking.

"Oh," he said, through his wet cloth.

Stark and Taihei, on the bellows, did not stop. Push. Pull. But Stark was staring at the neck of the furnace, gone yellow. Taihei had his mouth open.

Ram stood with her hand out. Perfectly still. Her face perfectly calm. Her hair moving in the heat.

"Ten minutes," said Rem.

* * *

It went on for an hour.

Ram did not move. Mau did not know how she did it. He had held the space round people. He knew what it was to hold something steady for a long time while it cost you. He watched Ram stand in front of a roaring furnace with her hand out and her wind going in a thin needle into the third hole, and he could see that it cost her. Her face did not change. But her other hand, at her side, had closed into a fist. At thirty minutes, it started to shake, very slightly.

"Thirty," said Rem.

"Ram knows."

At forty, Ram's breathing changed. Shorter. Mau could hear it.

"Forty," said Rem.

"*Ram knows*."

At fifty, her outstretched hand had begun to tremble too. The needle of wind went thinner. Wavered. Steadied.

Rem stepped closer. Not touching her. Close.

"Fifty, Nee-sama."

Ram did not answer.

At fifty-five, Frieren came across the timber yard.

Mau had not seen her coming. She came and stood on Ram's other side. She did not say anything. She did not touch Ram either. She stood there, close, the way Rem stood. The way you stand beside someone holding something heavy, so they know you are there if they drop it.

Ram's eyes went to her. Very briefly.

Then back to the third hole.

"Sixty," said Rem.

Ram lowered her hand.

* * *

She did not fall.

Mau had thought she might. She swayed. Rem's hands were on her arm at once, and Frieren's on the other. Between them, Ram stood. Breathing hard. Her face still perfectly calm. Her hands shaking.

"Ram is fine," said Ram.

"Nee-sama is shaking," said Rem.

"Ram is *fine*," said Ram. "Ram is generous. Ram is not stupid." She looked at the furnace. The neck was still yellow. Fading to orange now, without her wind. "Ram did one hour. Ram said one hour." She looked at Senku. "Was it enough?"

Senku had not taken his eyes off the furnace.

"I don't know," he said. "Yet." He looked at the yellow neck going orange. "Stop the bellows. Let it cool. Two hours. Then we break it."

Stark and Taihei stopped.

Both of them sat down on the ground beside their bellows. Taihei lay back on the dry earth with his arms out and said, to the sky, "I'm a cook," as if reminding himself.

Ram looked at Frieren, beside her, still holding her arm.

"The elf came," she said.

"I came," said Frieren.

"Ram did not need the elf."

"I know," said Frieren. "I came anyway." She did not let go of Ram's arm. "You're using what I filled you with this week. All of it, in an hour. I wanted to see what you did with it."

Ram looked at her for a long time.

"Ram made iron melt," she said.

"Maybe," said Frieren.

"Ram made iron melt," said Ram, with complete certainty, and let Rem lead her to the woodpile to sit down.

* * *

At three o'clock, Wakana broke it open.

The same way he had broken the first. Carefully. With the hammer. At the sealed door. The clay cracked. He pulled the pieces away. Inside, the crucible. Inside the crucible, under the grey ash—

Senku reached in with the tongs.

He did not lift anything out.

He looked into the crucible for a long time. The whole timber yard was watching. Stark and Taihei on the ground. Kaneki at the kilns. Ram on the woodpile with Rem. Frieren. Marin, who had come out at two to watch and had not gone back in. Mau.

"Senku?" said Wakana.

Senku tilted the crucible toward them.

It was full.

Not of the rough egg-sized piece of black iron that had gone in. Of something liquid. Black. Shining. Moving very slowly, like honey. Cooling at the edges, where it touched the clay. Still liquid in the middle.

All of it. The whole egg-sized piece. Melted.

"Ten billion percent," said Senku. Very quietly. Almost a whisper. "It melted. All of it."

Nobody said anything.

Then Wakana said, "The mould. Now. Before it sets."

* * *

The mould was ready. Wakana had made it the day before. Clay, fired in the middle kiln, a shallow tray with a shape pressed into it. A plate. The size of a palm, a little more. Curved, slightly, like the curve of a chest.

Senku poured.

Very slowly. With the tongs, tilting the crucible. The black liquid ran out of it in a thin shining stream into the clay mould and filled the shape. Spread. Settled.

It cooled.

They watched it cool. All of them. Nobody went anywhere. The black liquid in the mould went from moving to still. From shining to a duller black. Like the black iron always was. Deep. Dense. Like wet stone.

At five o'clock, Wakana tapped the mould with the hammer.

The clay fell away.

In the middle of the broken pieces lay a plate. Black. Palm-sized, a little more. Curved slightly, like the curve of a chest. Smooth on one side, where it had lain against the mould. Rough on the other. Heavy. Shining faintly, like wet stone.

Senku picked it up.

He did not say anything. He turned it over in his hands. He held it up to the light. He tapped it with his knuckle, and it rang, very faintly, like a bell a long way off.

Then he walked over to Stark.

* * *

Stark was still sitting on the ground beside his bellows. Soaked through. Marin's quilted jacket dark with sweat. He looked up at Senku and the black plate in his hands.

"It's not armour," said Stark.

"It's one piece," said Senku. "Of armour. The first one." He held it out. "It goes over the heart. Wakana says. On the jacket. Marin will sew a pocket for it."

Marin, beside the broken furnace, already had a needle in her hand. Mau did not know where she had got it.

Stark took the plate.

He held it in both hands. Heavy. Black. Curved. He looked at it for a long time.

"From the iron wood," he said. "From the rock I carried."

"From the rock you carried," said Senku.

Stark looked at it.

Then he stood up, slowly, in his soaked jacket. He held the black plate against his chest, over the quilting, over his heart. Where it would go.

It fitted.

Across the timber yard, at the woodpile, Fern had come out at some point. Nobody had seen her come. She was standing beside Ram and Rem, with the ledger under her arm. She was looking at Stark with the black plate against his chest.

She did not say anything.

But that evening, before the roof, before the crackers table, she wrote it on the day's page, in her small neat hand:

*Day 382. The second furnace. Ram's wind, one hour. It melted. All of it. One plate (over the heart). Stark.*

And in the margin, smaller:

*Ram did it. (Don't tell Ram I said so. She already knows.)*

* * *

After the furnace, when the plate was cooling and Ram had been put to bed by her sister, Stark went out to the north field and lay down in the long grass with his arm over his eyes.

He did not say anything to anyone.

Ten minutes later Yuta came out of the back door carrying Mau, because Mau could not yet walk that far and had asked, and set him down in the long grass a few paces from Stark, carefully, the splits from seventy still pink on his arms, and went back in. Mau lay down in the long grass too. Not close. A few paces off. Same field. Same time.

Neither of them said anything at all.

They were both asleep inside a quarter of an hour. Frieren came out at dusk, looked at them for a long moment, and went back in without waking either.

On the page called *Days*, under *Stark. A nap. Same field. Same time.*, Fern wrote:

*Both.*
