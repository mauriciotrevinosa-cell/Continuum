# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 31
## Chapter 29 — The Hook Means Floor

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 31
**Reading order:** 29
**Continuity state:** The green book from floor 15 gives up its first meaning. Frieren, Fern and Mau (the three columns) and Armin, comparing the margin drawings with Anko's board, find that each page is a floor: a train on page 8, a bell on 9, a drowned shelf on 15, turning statues on 56 and 88. So the hook at the top of each page means *floor*, and the book is a climber's diary from long ago. Page 100 shows a tree; page 101 shows a seated stone figure surrounded by standing ones, and one word that recurs on every statue page, which they read from context as *watches*. Only a few words are read; no full translation. Like the katana, the book seems to have been put down for them on purpose

*When: Day 390, afternoon. The long table. Mau is out of the clinic, in the east room by order, and has come down to the long table anyway, because Frieren said "if you sit and do nothing but count, Shoko can't object". Shoko has objected. Frieren has ignored her.*

The green book lay open on the long table.

Three columns on Fern's ledger slip beside it. Fern's small neat hand. Frieren's careful one. Mau's, uneven, a little crooked, twelve marks long from the day of the yellow flowers and twenty more since.

Small words, probably. The marks that came up most often on every page.

They had found forty of them now.

They did not know what any of them meant.

"Forty small words," said Fern. She was looking at the slip with her pen in her hand and an expression Mau had seen on her face when the ledger would not balance. "And not one of them means anything yet. We know they're common. We don't know what they *say*."

"That's how it starts," said Frieren.

"It's very slow."

"It's the slowest thing in the world," said Frieren. "That's how you know it's going to work." She turned a page. "I learned three languages this way. In my world. It took me about two hundred years. The first sixty were the worst."

Fern looked at her.

"We don't have two hundred years."

"No," said Frieren. "But we have three columns. I only ever had one."

* * *

Armin came in from the yard at three.

He had the map of the house under one arm and the board under the other, and Anko's pencil behind his ear. He had been at Anko's real board all morning, copying the tower: every floor, every line, in his small neat hand, onto a new sheet. *So someone who comes later can read it in one place*, he had said.

He stopped behind Mau's chair and looked at the green book.

He did not say anything for a while. He looked at the page it was open to. Page 8. Close writing, small, in rows, in the script nobody knew. The hook at the top, and the mark after it. And in the margin, a tiny drawing.

A long box. With smaller boxes inside it, in a row. And circles under it.

Armin looked at it for a long time.

"That's a train," he said.

Everyone looked at him.

"In the margin," said Armin. He pointed. "That drawing. It's a train. Carriages. Wheels." He looked at the page. "What page is this?"

"Eight," said Fern. "Counting from the front."

Armin put his board down on the table. The new sheet. The tower, copied from Anko's board. He ran his finger down it. Floor 1. Floor 2. Floor 3.

Floor 8.

*Day 319. Mau, Kaneki, Stark, Milim. Floor 8: a stopped train. Sleeping grey shapes. Kaneki heard who was about to wake.*

Nobody said anything.

* * *

"Turn to nine," said Armin.

Frieren turned to nine.

In the margin, a tiny drawing. A shape like an upturned cup. With a line hanging from it.

A bell.

Armin did not need to look at his sheet. Everyone at the table knew floor 9. The school. The corridor. The bell at the end of it, that Yuta had rung, and the small grey shapes that had walked home.

"Fifteen," said Armin.

Frieren turned to fifteen.

In the margin, a drawing of tall shapes in rows. Shelves. And under them, a wavy line. Water.

"The drowned library," said Fern. Very quietly. "Where I found the book."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"Fifty-six," said Armin.

Frieren turned. She had to turn a long way. The book was thicker than it looked.

In the margin of fifty-six, a drawing of figures. Robed. Hooded. Standing. And beside each one, a tiny curved line, like a head turning.

The garden of statues. That turned their heads.

"Eighty-eight," said Armin. His voice had gone very quiet.

Frieren turned.

The same drawing. Robed figures, hooded, with turning heads. And one of them, at the edge, with its head turned the other way. Toward a blank space in the margin. Toward nothing.

Armin looked at it.

"That's the one I marked," he said. "On eighty-eight. The one that was looking somewhere else."

* * *

Frieren closed the book on her finger.

She did not say anything for a long time. She sat at the long table with the green book closed on her finger and looked at nothing.

"The hook," she said at last.

Everyone waited.

"The mark at the top of every page," said Frieren. "The hook with the bar. And the mark after it, different on every page, in order." She opened the book again. To page 1. To the hook. "Fern found it. At the river. We thought it might be *day*. Or *entry*." She looked at it. "It's *floor*."

Fern made a small sound.

"And the mark after it is which floor," said Frieren. "One. Two. Three. All the way through." She turned the pages slowly, watching the marks change. "It's not a diary of days. It's a diary of floors." She looked up. "Somebody climbed this tower. A long time ago. And wrote down every floor. And drew something in the margin so they'd remember."

"This tower," said Mau.

"This tower," said Frieren. "Or one exactly like it." She turned to page 8 again. To the train. "Kaneki heard them breathing faster when they were about to wake. Did whoever wrote this hear that too?" She looked at the close rows of writing. "I don't know. I can't read it." She touched the drawing. "But they drew a train."

* * *

Mau looked at the book.

"Somebody put it down," he said.

Everyone looked at him.

"In the drowned library," said Mau. "On fifteen. On a shelf in the reading room. Where Fern would find it. The notice said *eligible to take: one*." He looked at the book. "And on eighty-six, the katana was lying in the middle of the field. Not stuck in the ground like the others. Put down. On purpose."

Nobody said anything.

"Somebody climbed this tower before," said Mau slowly. "And wrote down every floor. And then the book was on a shelf on fifteen. Where someone climbing would find it." He looked at Frieren. "Like a letter. Left for whoever came next."

Frieren looked at him for a long time.

"Or a map," said Armin. Very quietly.

They looked at him.

"I make maps," said Armin. "For people who come after. So they know where they're going." He looked at the book. "That's what this is. Someone made a map of the tower. And left it for the next one." He looked at Mau. "For you."

* * *

"The end," said Fern.

Frieren looked at her.

"If it's every floor," said Fern, "it ends at a hundred. Doesn't it?" She looked at the book. "What's on a hundred?"

Frieren turned to the back.

She went slowly. Page by page. Eighty-nine, with a drawing of banners. Ninety, with a drawing of a long whip, coiled, and a figure kneeling. Mau's side ached when he saw it. Ninety-one. Ninety-two. On through the nineties, each with its small drawing in the margin, things none of them had seen yet.

One hundred.

In the margin, a tree.

Not a twisted one, like the tree on five. Straight. Tall. With leaves drawn very carefully, one by one. And under it, a small round shape, like a pool. And beside it, something smaller. A stone, maybe. Or a seed.

The writing on page 100 was shorter than the others. Three lines. Then a space.

"A tree," said Fern. "At the top. That's all?"

Frieren did not answer.

She was turning one more page.

* * *

There was a page 101.

The hook at the top. *Floor.* And after it, a mark none of them had seen before. Not the next number. Something else. A mark that was not like the numbers at all.

And the margin drawing was not in the margin.

It filled the whole page. The only page in the book where the drawing was bigger than the writing. A figure, seated. Huge. Robed. On a throne. Stone, from the way it was drawn. Smooth face. No eyes. And all round it, in a ring, standing figures. Smaller. Robed. Hooded. Each one with something in its hands: a sword, a bell, a lamp, a key, a scale.

And under the drawing, one line of writing. Larger than the rest of the book. Pressed into the page so hard the pen had nearly gone through.

Frieren looked at the line for a long time.

Then she took Fern's ledger slip. She ran her finger down the three columns. Fern's. Hers. Mau's.

She stopped at a mark in Mau's column. One of the twelve he had found on the day of the yellow flowers.

She looked at the line on page 101. The same mark was in it.

"This one," she said. Very quietly. "Mau found it. In the first twelve pages. It comes up on—" She turned back. Quickly now. Page 56. She ran her finger down the close writing. There. Page 88. There. Page 80. She looked at Mau. "Did anything happen on eighty?"

"The stone figure," said Mau. "At the back of the army. That watched."

Frieren turned to 80. Ran her finger down.

There.

"It's on every page with a statue," she said. "Every page where something stone was looking at them. And nowhere else." She turned back to 101. To the last line, pressed so hard into the page. "And it's here."

"What does it mean?" said Fern.

Frieren looked at the mark for a long time.

"I can't read it," she said. "Not properly. Not the way I'd read a word." She looked at it. "But if it's on every page where the stone was watching, and only on those pages—" She stopped.

"*Watches*," said Armin.

Frieren looked at him.

"Or *watcher*," said Armin. "Or *eyes*. Or *it sees*." He looked at the drawing. At the seated stone figure with no eyes, and the ring of statues round it. "Something like that."

Nobody said anything for a very long time.

The long table was very quiet. Outside, in the yard, someone was laughing. A door. Milim's voice. Then quiet again.

"Floor one hundred and one," said Mau.

"There isn't a hundred and one," said Fern. "The notice on the first floor said a hundred."

"Anko wrote *after?*," said Mau. "At the bottom of the board. Under everything." He looked at the drawing. "Senku named a star *The Hundred and First*. Because it isn't finished. Like the story." He looked at Frieren. "Somebody climbed to a hundred. And then there was a hundred and one. And they drew this. And pressed that word into the page so hard the pen nearly went through."

Frieren closed the book.

She held it shut in both hands on the long table.

"Nine more floors," she said. "Then the tree. Then this." She looked at him. "And we've got forty small words and one that means *watches*."

"Forty-one," said Mau.

"Forty-one," said Frieren.

She opened the book again. To page 1. To the hook that meant *floor*. She took the pencil from Fern's hand and gave it to Mau.

"Count," she said. "We've got nine floors' worth of time. Let's find out what the rest of page one hundred and one says before we get there."

* * *

Armin did not go back to Anko's board that afternoon.

He sat at the long table beside Mau with his new sheet, the tower copied from Anko's board. Beside each floor, in his small neat hand, he wrote the page number from the green book, and a description of the margin drawing.

*Floor 8. Page 8. A train.*

*Floor 9. Page 9. A bell.*

*Floor 15. Page 15. Shelves and water.*

All the way down. All ninety of the floors they had climbed. Then the nine they had not. Ninety-one to ninety-nine, with their drawings, things nobody had seen yet: a staircase made of light, a door with no wall, a lake with a single boat.

Then one hundred.

*A tree. A pool. A stone or a seed.*

Then, at the very bottom of the sheet, below everything, the way Anko had written *after?* at the bottom of her board:

*101. Not a number. A seated stone figure. A ring of statues, each holding something. One word, pressed hard: (watches?).*

He drew it as a broken line.
