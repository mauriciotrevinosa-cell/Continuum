# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 31
## Chapter 30 — Where the Flower Came From

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 31
**Reading order:** 30
**Continuity state:** Mai's own choice. She asks to see the garden on floor 5, where Yuka picked the pale flower Maki wears. Shoko allows it as "garden only, no floor, no shadows", since Mau is still healing. The party is Mau (owner), Maki, Mai and Yuka. Mai spends one day's construction on a second silver flower, for Yuka ("little rope"), and says aloud that she has stopped making a bullet every day. Mai's private-history arc (DH-057) stays untouched. No combat

*When: Day 390, late afternoon. Mau's shoulder is stitched and his side holds. Shoko has said he may walk to the screen and stand on the seal on five and sit under a tree, and nothing else. Mai asked at lunch. Nobody had ever heard Mai ask to go anywhere.*

Mai asked at lunch.

She did not ask the table. She asked Maki, very quietly, across the bench, when she thought nobody was listening. Mau was listening. He was sitting two places down with his bound shoulder, eating one-handed, and he heard all of it.

"The garden," said Mai. "On five. Where the flower came from."

Maki looked at her.

"The one in your hair," said Mai. "Behind your ear. Yuka picked it. You've worn it since." She looked at her bowl. "I made the silver one from it. From looking at it. I've never seen where it came from." She paused. "I'd like to."

Maki did not say anything for a long moment.

"It's in the tower," she said.

"I know."

"You've never been in the tower."

"I know," said Mai. "I've watched everyone else go in. Every day. All summer." She turned her spoon over. "I make one thing a day and it costs me. I've never seen anything worth spending it on except a bullet." She paused. "Then I made you a flower. And it was the best thing I ever made." She looked up. "I'd like to see where it came from. That's all."

Maki looked at her sister for a long time.

Then she turned her head and looked down the bench at Mau.

* * *

Shoko said yes with conditions.

"Garden only," she said. "No floor. You stand on the seal on five, you walk to the tree, you sit. Nothing comes out of you. No shadows. Nothing. You've got stitches in your shoulder and a side that's held together by Yuta's stubbornness." She looked at him. "One hour."

"One hour."

"And Maki carries you if you fall over."

"I'm not going to fall over."

"I know you're not," said Shoko. "Maki knows too. She's going to carry you anyway, if it comes to that. I'm just saying it so it's on record."

Yuka came too.

She asked at the screen, very carefully, not looking at anyone. "Can I come? It's my flower. I picked it." She paused. "I'd like to show Aunt Mai the tree."

Mai went very still.

Nobody had ever called her that. Not here. Mau could see it on her face. Yuka had not even noticed she had said it. She was looking at the screen, at Anko's board, at the empty space under today.

"Yes," said Mai. Very quietly. "Come."

Anko wrote it on the board.

*Day 390. Mau, Maki, Mai, Yuka. From 5. The garden. Not a floor. One hour. (Shoko.)*

And under it, smaller, without being asked:

*Mai's first time.*

* * *

They stepped onto the seal on five.

The garden was exactly as it had been the last time Mau saw it. On the day he could not breathe and then could. The wide flat terrace, open on all sides. The low broken walls. Nothing beyond them but grey mist. Long grass, waist-high, pale green. Tangles of briar, dark, quiet. And in the middle, the tree. Twisted and old, with branches that went out sideways. Covered in small pale flowers.

Mai stood on the seal and did not move.

She looked at the mist beyond the broken walls. At the grass. At the briar. At the tree.

"Oh," she said.

Mau had heard everyone say it. At the top of the stair, at the first sight of the iron wood, or the drowned library, or the sky on nineteen. Armin at the roof. Taihei at the roof. Everyone said it.

Mai said it as if she had not said it about anything in a very long time.

"That's the tree," said Yuka. She came up beside her aunt. "That one. In the middle. I took the flower from the lowest branch. On the day of the first transfer point. There were thorn-dogs. Grandma killed most of them." She looked at Maki. "I took a flower afterwards. To show her the garden."

"You told me," said Mai.

"I'll show you which branch," said Yuka.

* * *

They walked through the long grass to the tree.

Mau walked slowly. His side pulled with every step. Maki walked beside him, close, not touching, ready. Yuka went ahead with Mai, through the waist-high grass. She was talking. She told her aunt everything. The thorn-dogs. The rope. The seal appearing. The small frame. *Can I take a flower?* Mai listened to all of it without saying anything, the way she listened at the crackers table.

At the tree, Yuka stopped.

She reached up and touched the lowest branch. There was a small bare place on it, where a stem had been.

"There," she said. "That one."

Mai looked at the branch. At the bare place where the flower had been.

Then she looked at Maki. At the pale flower behind Maki's ear, dried now, faded, five petals. It had been on this branch eighty-eight days ago.

Then at the spear on Maki's back. The silver flower tied below the blade.

"Two," said Mai.

"Two," said Maki.

* * *

Mau sat down under the tree.

He had promised Shoko. He sat with his back against the twisted trunk, the way he had sat on the day of the yellow flowers and the garden and Gojo saying it won't happen here. The grass came up round him. A pale flower let go of a branch and drifted down and landed on his knee.

He did not move.

He watched the three of them under the tree. Maki standing with her arms folded, looking at her sister. Yuka at the lowest branch with her hand on the bare place. And Mai, between them, looking up into the twisted branches, at the hundreds of small pale flowers.

Mai closed her eyes.

Mau saw her hands turn over at her sides. Palms up. The way they had turned over on the woodpile behind the workshop, the first time.

"Mai," said Maki. Very quietly. "You don't have to. Not here."

"I want to," said Mai. Without opening her eyes. "I haven't made today's yet."

Maki did not say anything else.

Mau felt nothing. Mai's technique was not something he could feel. He watched her face. He saw the cost go across it. The same cost, every time, whether it was a bullet or a flower. Something given away that would not come back.

She opened her eyes.

In her palm was a flower.

Silver-grey. Five petals. Thin, so thin the grey light came through them at the edges. A short stem with a tiny loop at the end. The same shape as the one on Maki's spear. The same shape as the dried one behind Maki's ear.

She turned round.

Not to Maki.

To Yuka.

* * *

Yuka looked at the flower in her aunt's palm.

"That's—" she said.

"Yours," said Mai.

Yuka did not move.

"Your grandmother has two," said Mai. "The one you picked, and the one I made from looking at it." She held the flower out. "You don't have any. You picked the first one, and gave it away." She was quiet. "Someone should make you one."

Yuka looked at it for a long time.

"Beru calls me little rope," she said. Very quietly. As if it were an answer.

"I heard," said Mai. "At the crackers table. Maki told us." She held the flower out a little further. "Little rope. Then tie it to your rope."

Yuka took it.

Carefully. Between her finger and thumb, the way Maki had taken the first one. She turned it over. The grey light came through the petals at the edges.

Then she took the short coil of rope from her belt. The one she practised with every evening on the fence posts, with Maki watching from the step. She tied the silver flower to it by its tiny loop, just above the weighted end. She tied it tight. She tested the knot. She tied it again.

Then she held up the coil, and the flower caught the light.

"It won't fade," said Mai.

"I know," said Yuka. Her voice was not steady. "Grandma's won't either. The silver one."

She put her arms round her aunt.

She did not ask first. She just did it. Mai went perfectly still. As if nobody had put their arms round her in a very long time. In any world.

Then, slowly, her arms came up too.

* * *

Maki had turned away.

She was standing with her back to the three of them, looking out at the grey mist beyond the broken wall. Her arms were folded. She was very still.

Mau did not say anything. He sat under the tree with the pale flower on his knee and looked at the grass.

After a while, Maki spoke. Without turning round.

"You stopped," she said. To Mai. "Didn't you. The bullets."

Mai let go of Yuka. Slowly. She looked at her sister's back.

"Not every day," she said. "I made one on the first day of the festival. And one on the day Touka came. And one the day after the Warden." She was quiet. "And the other days, nothing. Or something else. A button for Ichika's sash, because it kept slipping. A hook for Marin's sewing room, because she lost hers." She paused. "And this."

Maki did not turn round.

"There are a lot of bullets in that drawer," said Mai. "More than I'll ever fire." She looked at the silver flower on Yuka's rope. "I think I've got enough bullets."

Maki was quiet for a long time.

Then she turned round. She looked at her sister. At the flower on her granddaughter's rope. At the dried pale one behind her own ear, and the silver one on her spear.

"Good," she said.

That was all.

But she came across the long grass and sat down under the tree beside Mau. She pulled Mai down beside her, by the sleeve. Yuka sat down on Mai's other side, with the rope across her knees and the silver flower on it. The four of them sat under the twisted tree in the garden on five, in the grey light, with the mist beyond the walls and the pale flowers drifting down. Nobody said anything until Mau's hour was up.

* * *

On the other side of the frame, Anko looked at the watch.

"Fifty-eight minutes," she said. She looked at the four of them. At Yuka's rope, with something silver on it now. At Mai.

"How was it?" she said. To Mai. Directly.

Mai thought about it.

"There's a tree," she said. "With a lot of flowers. And a bare place on the lowest branch." She paused. "I'd like to go again."

Anko wrote it down.

*Day 390. The garden. Mai's first time. A third flower (Yuka). Mai would like to go again.*

And under it, smaller:

*Maki said good.*
