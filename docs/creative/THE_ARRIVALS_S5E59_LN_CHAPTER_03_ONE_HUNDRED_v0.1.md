# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 31
## Chapter 38 — One Hundred

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 31
**Reading order:** 38
**Continuity state:** Floor 100 (Day 393, afternoon), the second visit, after the Ruler fell at dawn. Gojo looked again: nothing there and nothing watching, and he asked for Ori to be taken. Shoko set Mau's broken left arm and allows a family party: Mau (owner, arm in a sling), Frieren, Fern and Ori. The top is a garden: a tall straight tree, a still pool and a stone seed, as on page 100 of the green book. It is not a fight. The notice says the tower is complete and gives three components (fruit, water, seed) toward something whose name it shows in a script nobody can read: the divine potion's parts, not a potion. A second notice says The Noise's extraction eligibility has widened to otherworlders and monarchs; it is inactive, and Mau does not activate it. Completion raises him to level 106 (two hundred and eighty-four unspent). Far off in the mist, a seated stone shape

*When: Day 393, afternoon. Mau's left arm is set and splinted and in a sling, and his eyes have been washed twice. At dawn there was a light in front of a gate. Now there is only a garden. Gojo went up a second time to be sure, came back down and said "garden. Nothing in it. Nothing watching. I looked very hard." Then he said "take Ori," and went to sit on the clinic step.*

Ori had not asked.

That was the strange thing. Everyone had expected her to ask. She had asked for the dragon and the knight and the flight over the house. She had come to the clinic with her list and stood in the doorway until Shoko said yes. She had written out plans.

She had not asked to go to a hundred.

Mau found her on the back step after lunch, with the list, looking at the screen across the dry ground. She had been there when they came back at dawn. She had seen his arm.

"Gojo says it's a garden," he said, sitting down beside her, carefully, because of the sling. "At the top. Nothing in it now. Nothing watching."

"I heard," said Ori. She looked at the sling. "Now."

"Now," said Mau. "It was something else this morning. It isn't any more."

"He said take Ori."

Ori looked at the list in her hands.

"I didn't ask," she said.

"I know."

"Because it's the last one," said Ori. "A hundred. Everyone's been going for the whole summer. Gojo and Yuta and Maki and Frieren and Fern and Stark and Kaneki and Rem and everyone." She looked at the list. "I've never been in. Not once. I named the shadows. I've never been where they came from." She paused. "And the last one should be for the people who went. Not for me."

Mau looked at her.

"Frieren's coming," he said. "And Fern. And me. That's three." He looked at the screen. "There's room for four."

Ori did not say anything for a long time.

"Is it really a garden?" she said.

"Gojo looked very hard."

"Then I'd like to see it," said Ori. Very quietly. "The top. Where it ends." She looked up at him. "If that's all right. If there's room."

"There's room," said Mau.

* * *

Anko wrote it on the board.

*Day 393, afternoon. Mau, Frieren, Fern, Ori. From 90. Floor 100. (Gojo looked again. A garden.)*

And under it, without being asked, smaller:

*Ori's first time.*

Shoko came to the screen. She did not usually come to the screen. She stood beside Anko with her arms folded and looked at Ori, in her coat, with the list in her pocket and her shoes on, for once.

"You hold Frieren's hand," said Shoko. "The whole way up. Through every floor. You don't let go of it."

"I won't let go."

"And if anything at all happens, anything, you go back through the frame. Straight away. Without waiting for anyone." She looked at Ori. "Is that clear?"

"That's clear," said Ori.

Shoko looked at her for a long moment more.

Then, unexpectedly, she crouched down. She did up the top button of Ori's coat, which Ori had missed.

"It's cold at the top of things," she said. "Usually."

She stood up and went back to the clinic step, where Gojo was sitting, and sat down beside him.

* * *

They walked from ninety.

Ori held Frieren's hand the whole way. She did not let go once. Mau walked behind them with Fern, his arm in its sling, and watched Ori see the tower for the first time.

She saw the staircase of light, and looked at it the whole way up, the way Armin's sheet said. She saw the water wall, held open still where Gojo had walked through it. She put her free hand out and touched it as they passed, and it was cold and stood upright, and she said *oh*. She saw the three doors, and the field with the small statue in it, and the statue turned its head to follow her as they passed. Ori turned her head to follow it right back, very seriously, until it was out of sight.

"It looked at me," she said.

"They do that," said Frieren.

"I looked back," said Ori.

"I saw."

She saw the lake, and the boat. She saw the place where fire had fallen like rain, cold now, with ash on the ground. She saw the door with no wall, standing open. Mau told her about the one heartbeat and the four paces and the plan he had told before. She listened to all of it, holding Frieren's hand, and at the end she said, "You told them first. Before."

"I told them first."

"That's good," said Ori. "That's like telling Frieren first. But for fights."

She saw the bridge. And the stair going up into white.

At the bottom of the white stair, she stopped.

She looked up it. Straight. Stone. Into a light so bright you could not see where it ended.

"That's the last one," she said.

"That's the last one," said Mau.

Ori held on to Frieren's hand very tightly.

"Okay," she said.

* * *

They climbed.

It was a long stair. Longer than the hot stair on sixty-nine. It went up and up into the white, and the white got brighter. It was not hot. It was not cold either. It was like walking up into the inside of a cloud at noon. Mau could not see the steps ahead of him. He could only see the next one, and Frieren's back, and Ori's hand in hers, and Fern beside him.

Then the white thinned.

And they were at the top.

* * *

It was a garden.

Gojo had said so. Mau had believed him. He had not imagined it.

Grass. Short, soft, green, the green of the south field where the dead earth came back. Going out on every side to a low stone wall. Beyond the wall, white mist, soft, moving slowly, like the edge of the sky. Above, not a grey lid like the other floors. Blue. Real blue. Or something that looked exactly like it. A summer sky with a few small white clouds in it, going nowhere.

In the middle of the garden, a tree.

Not twisted, like the tree on five. Straight. Tall. Very tall. Its trunk was pale and smooth, and its branches went up and out like the arms of someone stretching in the morning. Its leaves were small and green and moved in a breeze Mau could not feel. In among the leaves, here and there, fruit. Round. Gold. Not bright. Soft gold, like late afternoon light.

At the foot of the tree, a pool. Small. Round. Perfectly still. The water so clear you could see every pebble at the bottom.

And beside the pool, on the grass, a stone.

Small. Round. Grey-green. The size of an egg. Lying in the grass the way the katana had lain in the field on eighty-six. As if someone had put it down there on purpose.

Nobody said anything.

Ori let go of Frieren's hand.

* * *

She did not run.

She walked across the soft green grass toward the tree. Slowly. The way she had walked toward Lantern in the north field, and toward Igris in the yard. She stopped at the edge of the pool and looked down at the water, at the pebbles at the bottom. Then up at the tree, at the gold fruit in the leaves. Then down at the stone in the grass.

She did not touch anything.

She turned round and looked at the three of them, still standing at the top of the stair.

"It's the drawing," she said. "In the book. Page one hundred. The tree, and the pool, and the stone." She looked back at it. "Armin showed me. On his sheet. It's exactly the drawing."

"It's exactly the drawing," said Frieren.

"Someone was here," said Ori. "Before. And drew it." She looked at the tree. "And then they went home and put the book on a shelf on fifteen. So we'd find it." She was quiet. "So we'd know it was a garden at the top. And not something bad."

Nobody said anything.

Mau had not thought of it that way. He had thought of the book as a map. A warning. A diary with *watches* pressed into its last page so hard the pen nearly went through.

He had not thought that someone had climbed all the way to the top and found a garden, and drawn it carefully, leaf by leaf, so that whoever came next would know that at the top, after everything, there was a tree.

"Maybe," he said.

"Maybe," said Ori, satisfied, and sat down on the grass beside the pool.

* * *

The notice came up.

Not small, at the edge of his sight. Large. In the middle. The largest notice he had ever seen.

*Floor 100 of 100.*

*The tower is complete.*

He read it. He read it again.

Then the notice changed.

*Owner progress: Floor 100. All floors cleared.*

*Reward: three components.*

*Component: the Fruit. (From the tree.)*

*Component: the Water. (From the pool.)*

*Component: the Seed. (From the grass.)*

*Combined: ———*

The last word was not a word. It was a mark. A long one. In a script Mau did not know.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he looked at Frieren.

"There's a word," he said. "At the end. After *combined*. I can't read it." He tried to describe the shape. A hook, and a long line, and three small marks above it.

Frieren went very still.

"That's the script," she said. "The green book's." She looked at the tree. "It's the same writing."

"Can you read it?"

"No," said Frieren. "It isn't one of our forty-one." She was quiet. "But it's the same hand. The same letters." She looked at the pool. "Whoever wrote the book. Whoever climbed it before. They wrote in the tower's own writing."

* * *

Ori picked the fruit.

Mau asked her to. He did not decide to ask; it came out. He looked at the gold fruit in the leaves, low enough to reach, and at Ori sitting by the pool. He thought about the first time she had told anyone anything, and the first plan she had written, and the shadows she had named.

"Ori," he said. "Will you pick the fruit?"

She looked up at him.

"Me?"

"You named them all," said Mau. "The shadows. Lantern and Corner. You wrote down every one." He looked at the tree. "You should have something from the top."

Ori got up.

She went to the tree. She looked up into the leaves for a long time. Then she reached up and, very carefully, took one gold fruit from the lowest branch. The way Yuka had taken the pale flower from the tree on five. In both hands.

It came away easily. It was warm. Soft gold, like late afternoon. She held it against her chest.

Fern took the water.

There was a flask on the grass by the pool that had not been there before, or had, and they had not seen it. Small. Clear. Stoppered. Fern knelt and filled it from the still pool and stoppered it and held it up to the blue light. The water did not move inside it. It stayed perfectly still, even when she turned it.

Frieren took the seed.

She knelt in the grass beside the pool and picked up the small grey-green stone. She held it in her open palm and looked at it for a long time.

"It's warm," she said.

"Everything's warm," said Ori. "Up here."

Frieren closed her hand round it.

* * *

Then the second notice came.

Mau knew it was different before he read it. It did not come up in the middle of his sight. It came up from underneath. From the place where the building happened, where the wheel slept and the walls held.

The Noise.

*Extraction scope expanded.*

*Eligible: otherworlders. Eligible: monarchs.*

*Status: inactive.*

He read it.

He read it again.

He did not understand it at first. Then he did. He felt cold, all over, standing in the warm garden at the top of the tower under the blue sky.

*Ciel.*

*I see it,* said Ciel. Very quiet. Very careful. *The Noise. It's widened what it can do. With the shadows. With extraction.* A pause. *Not just guardians. Not just copies made in this tower.* Another pause. *People. From other worlds. And something it calls monarchs. I don't know what those are.*

*People.*

*It's inactive,* said Ciel. *It's not doing anything. It's telling you it could.* A pause. *It's asking, I think. The way the Noise asks. By showing you something and waiting.*

Mau looked at the notice for a long time.

He looked at Ori, sitting by the pool with a gold fruit against her chest. At Fern, kneeling with the flask of still water. At Frieren, with her hand closed round a warm grey-green stone.

People from other worlds.

Everyone he loved was a person from another world.

"No," he said. Aloud.

Frieren looked up.

"No," said Mau again. To the notice. To the Noise. To whatever was underneath, building in the dark. "Not that. Not now. Maybe not ever." He looked at the notice. "I don't want to know how to do that."

The notice did not change.

*Status: inactive.*

He closed it.

*It's still there,* said Ciel, very quietly. *Closing it doesn't take it away. It's a thing the Noise can do now. Whether you want it or not.* A pause. *But you said no. Out loud. I heard. I think it heard.*

* * *

"What was it?" said Frieren.

He told her. Not the words exactly. *Extraction. People from other worlds. Something called monarchs. Inactive.* He told her he had said no.

Frieren listened. She did not say anything for a long time.

"Good," she said at last.

"It's still there. Ciel says. It's something the Noise can do now."

"I know," said Frieren. "Lots of things are things we can do." She looked at the seed in her hand. "That doesn't mean we do them." She looked at him. "You said no. Out loud. At the top of the tower." She paused. "I'll remember you said it."

* * *

The third notice was small. At the edge. The kind he was used to.

*Level 106. Two hundred and eighty-four points unspent.*

"A hundred and six," said Fern. She had heard him read it. "It went up five. At once."

"For finishing," said Mau.

Ori, by the pool, looked up.

"Is it finished?" she said.

Mau looked at the garden. At the tree, and the pool, and the place in the grass where the seed had been. At the low stone wall. At the white mist beyond it, moving slowly.

And far off, in the mist, beyond the wall, where the white was thickest, something.

A shape. Very faint. Very far. Grey. Like a hill, or a building, or something very large, seated. With smaller shapes round it, in a ring.

He looked at it for a long time.

Frieren was looking at it too.

Neither of them said anything.

Ori was looking at the gold fruit in her hands.

"The tower's finished," said Mau at last. "A hundred floors. All of them."

"That's good," said Ori.

She stood up, with the fruit against her chest, and looked round the garden at the top of everything. The tree, the pool, the soft green grass, the blue sky.

"Nothing happened," she said. "On the last floor. Nothing at all." She looked at him. At the sling. She knew. She had seen him come back at dawn. "Not this time," she said. "Not while I was here." She looked at the garden. "That's my favourite."

Mau did not look at the mist again. He did not look at the place by the gate where the grass was pale, as if something very bright had stood on it for a very long time.

"Mine too," he said.

* * *

They went home through the small frame at the edge of the garden. Ori first, holding the fruit, with Frieren's hand in her other one. Then Fern with the flask. Then Frieren with the seed. Then Mau.

At the frame he stopped.

He did not look at the mist. He looked at the tree. Tall, straight, pale, with its gold fruit in the moving leaves and the still pool at its foot. Someone had climbed all the way up here once, a long time ago, and drawn it, leaf by leaf, so that the next one would know.

He thought: *thank you*.

He did not know who to.

He went through.

On the other side, Anko looked at the watch. She looked at them. At Ori with a gold fruit. At Fern with a flask of water that did not move when she turned it. At Frieren with her hand closed. At Mau.

"Well?" she said.

"A garden," said Ori. "At the top. Nothing happened. This time." She held up the fruit. "I picked this."

Anko looked at the fruit for a long time. Then at the board. At four planks, a hundred floors, a whole summer, in a dozen hands.

She took the stub of pencil from behind her ear and wrote the last line.

*Day 393, dawn. Floor 100. The Light of the Hundredth (Ruler). Mau finished it. Left arm broken, eyes. Not eligible. A fragment: the Ruler's Authority (rough).*

*Day 393, afternoon. Floor 100. A garden. Tree, pool, seed. Ori picked the fruit. Nothing happened.*

Then, in larger letters, across the bottom of the fourth plank, under everything:

*COMPLETE.*

She did not underline it.

Below it, where she had written *After?* eight days ago, the question was still there. She looked at it for a long time. She did not cross it out.
