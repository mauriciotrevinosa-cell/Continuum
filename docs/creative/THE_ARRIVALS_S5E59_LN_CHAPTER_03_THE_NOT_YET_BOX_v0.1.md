# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 31
## Chapter 33 — The NOT YET Box

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)
**Volume:** 31
**Reading order:** 33
**Continuity state:** The evening of floor 100 (Day 393). At the long table Mau tells the whole house about the widened extraction notice and that he said no (the house has told Mau everything since S4; this time he tells the house). The newer arrivals react in their own ways. Senku, Maomao and Shoko examine the three components and can make nothing of them yet; the fruit does not rot, the water does not move, the seed is warm. They go into Senku's NOT YET box (from S4 V21, where the first bulb glowed one second) under Maomao's eye. Umaru declares Tower Day. The question *After?* stays on the board

*When: Day 393, evening. The long table. Everyone is here, because Anko wrote COMPLETE across the bottom of the board, and the house read it before dinner. Ori has carried the gold fruit around all afternoon. She has not let anyone else hold it.*

The house did not fuss. It had learned not to.

But Momo had made pear bread. And Taihei had made curry, though it was not his day, and Momo had looked at the pot and then at the board through the window, with *COMPLETE* across the bottom of it, and said nothing. Touka had made coffee for everyone who could drink it. Kessoku had brought their instruments in from the music room and put them in the corner, not playing, just there. The five had made a banner out of the back of an old ledger page that said, in Hakari's enormous uneven letters, *100!!!*, and pinned it over the kitchen door. Umaru had confiscated it for unauthorised exclamation marks, then given it back and added a fourth.

Nobody made a speech.

They ate.

* * *

Mau waited until the plates were mostly empty.

He sat at his place with Frieren beside him. Her shoulder against his. Ori on his other side, with the gold fruit in her lap, under the table, where she could keep a hand on it. Rimuru across from him, watching his face, because Ciel had already told Rimuru, and Rimuru knew what was coming.

He put his spoon down.

"I want to tell everyone something," he said.

The long table went quiet. Not all at once. In a wave, from his end outward. Fern put down her pen. Seiko put down her tea. At the far end, Himmel stopped brushing his hair.

"At the top," said Mau. "On a hundred. After the fruit and the water and the seed. A notice came." He looked at the table. "Not from the tower. From underneath. From the Noise. The thing that builds things in me that I don't know about."

Nobody said anything.

"It said the Noise can do something new now. With the shadows. With extraction." He looked at his hands on the table. "It used to be only the guardians. Copies, made in the tower. Igris. Beru. Kamish." He paused. "The notice said it can be people now. From other worlds. And something it called monarchs. I don't know what those are."

The table was perfectly silent.

"It said *inactive*," said Mau. "It's not doing anything. It was telling me it could." He looked up. "I said no. Out loud. At the top of the tower. I said I don't want to know how to do that."

He looked down the long table. At all of them.

"Everyone in this house is someone from another world," he said. "Everyone. Every one of you." He was quiet. "I wanted you to know I said no. And I wanted you to hear it from me. Not find out later."

* * *

Nobody moved for a long time.

Then Touka put down her cup.

"Can it be made to?" she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"The thing in you," said Touka. Her voice was very flat. "The Noise. Can someone make it? Against what you want?" She looked at Mau. "In my world, there were people who would have done anything to have a thing like that. To use it on people like me." She did not look at Ichika, beside her. "I want to know if someone could make it happen. Or if it's only you."

Mau looked at her.

He did not know. He said so.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "Ciel doesn't know. The Noise doesn't listen to anyone. It doesn't listen to me, mostly. It builds what it builds." He paused. "But it showed me the notice and waited. It didn't do anything. It waited for me to say."

Touka looked at him for a long time.

"Then don't let anyone else stand where you're standing when it asks," she said.

"I won't."

"I mean it," said Touka. "I've seen what happens when the wrong person's standing in the right place."

She picked up her cup again. She did not say anything else.

* * *

Armin spoke next. Very quietly. From the end of the bench, beside Mikasa.

"Monarchs," he said.

Mau looked at him.

"The notice said monarchs," said Armin. "You don't know what that means." He looked at the table. "But the general said something on ninety. Gojo told Anko. Anko wrote it down. *It said you are not yet what it needs.*" He looked at Mau. "Something needs you to be something. And now the thing inside you can reach for monarchs." He was quiet. "I'm only saying they might be the same thing. I don't know."

Nobody said anything.

"Broken line," said Umaru, from her place. Very quietly. "That's a broken line."

"It's a broken line," Armin agreed.

* * *

Kaneki had not said anything.

He sat at the end of the long table with Touka on one side and Ichika on the other, and a cup of coffee Touka had made properly that he was not drinking. He was looking at Mau.

"You told us," he said.

Mau looked at him.

"You didn't have to," said Kaneki. "You could have kept it. Nobody would've known. It's inside you. It's inactive." He looked at his coffee. "You told us anyway. The whole table. People from other worlds. So we'd know what's in the room with us." He was quiet. "In my world, nobody ever told me what was in the room with me. I always found out after."

He picked up the coffee. He drank it.

"Thank you," he said.

* * *

Then Seiko laughed.

Not loudly. A small dry sound from the high seat. Everyone turned.

"Monarchs," she said. "Extraction. People from other worlds." She shook her head. "Do you know what I see when I look at this table, dear?"

Mau looked at her.

"I see a boy who came back from the top of a tower with a piece of fruit," said Seiko. "And gave it to a little girl to carry. And then sat down at dinner and told forty people the most frightening thing that happened to him all day. So none of them would have to be frightened alone." She picked up her tea. "Whatever that thing in you can do, dear, that's what *you* do." She drank. "I'm not worried about the thing. I'm worried you'll forget to eat."

Turbo Granny, in her cat on the table beside her, said, "He did eat. I watched. Two bowls."

"I know he did," said Seiko. "I'm saying it so it's on record."

Somebody laughed. Then someone else. Then the long table breathed out, all at once. Talk came back, from Seiko's end, the way it had gone quiet from Mau's.

Frieren, beside him, did not say anything. But under the table, her foot was against his ankle. It stayed there.

* * *

Senku took the components after dinner.

All three. To the workshop, with Maomao and Shoko and Byakuya, and Ori, who would not let the fruit go until she saw where it was going. They laid them on a clean cloth on Senku's workbench, under a lamp. The gold fruit. The flask of still water. The small grey-green seed.

They looked at them for a long time.

"Fruit," said Senku. "Water. Seed." He did not touch any of them. "*Combined*: a word nobody can read." He looked at Mau. "And you don't know what they make."

"No."

"Nobody does," said Maomao. She was looking at the fruit very closely, with her nose almost touching it. "It doesn't smell like anything. Fruit always smells like something." She looked at the water. "That doesn't move. Water always moves." She looked at the seed. "That's warm. Stones aren't warm."

"So they're not what they look like," said Senku.

"They're not what anything looks like," said Maomao. "They're three things that look like ordinary things and aren't." She sat back. "I'm not cutting the fruit. I'm not tasting the water. I'm not planting the seed."

"Neither am I," said Senku.

"Neither am I," said Shoko.

They looked at each other.

"Ten billion percent," said Senku slowly, "we don't know what this is. We don't know what it does. We don't know what *combined* means, or if you're supposed to eat it, or drink it, or plant it, or do something nobody's thought of." He looked at the three things on the cloth. "And there's only one of each."

"So we wait," said Byakuya.

"So we wait," said Senku.

He went to the shelf at the back of the workshop. To a box on it, plain wood, that had been there since the Muslin. Since the winter of the waterwheel and the snapped wire and the bulb that glowed for one second and went out. Painted on its lid, in Senku's quick hand, long ago:

*NOT YET.*

He took it down. He opened it. Inside: a small heap of grey dust, which had been a bulb. A crystal, dead and cloudy. A few other things Mau had never asked about.

Senku made room.

He did not put the components in himself. He looked at Ori, standing by the workbench with her hands behind her back because she had promised not to touch the fruit once it was on the cloth.

"You carried it all day," he said. "You put it in."

Ori looked at the box. At the grey dust and the dead crystal.

"What's this box for?" she said.

"Things that aren't ready yet," said Senku. "Things that didn't work. Or might work later. Or that I don't understand yet." He looked at the bulb dust. "That was the first light I ever made here. It lasted one second. Then I put it in the box." He paused. "Less than a year later we had forty lamps at a festival."

Ori looked at him.

"So the box is for things that will work later," she said.

"The box is for things that *might*," said Senku. "Ten billion percent might."

Ori thought about this for a long time.

Then she picked up the gold fruit from the cloth, very carefully, in both hands, and put it in the box, beside the bulb dust. Maomao put the flask of still water beside it. Shoko put the warm seed beside that.

Senku closed the lid.

*NOT YET.*

He put the box back on the shelf.

"When we know," he said, "we'll open it."

"When will we know?" said Ori.

"I don't know," said Senku. "That's why it's in the box."

* * *

Umaru declared Tower Day before bed.

She stood on the bench at the end of the long table with her slate, as the last of the house was going up. She did not ask anyone to stay. The house stayed anyway.

"The Ministry," said Umaru, "declares that today is Tower Day. Every year. On the day the tower was finished."

"How will we know which day that is?" said Hakari.

"Fern will know," said Umaru. "Fern always knows what day it is." She looked at her slate. "On Tower Day, everyone who went in writes one thing on the board. Not the floor. One thing they remember." She paused. "And everyone who didn't go in writes one thing they remember about waiting."

Nobody said anything.

"It's a constitutional amendment," said Umaru. "Effective immediately. No objections."

There were no objections.

* * *

That night, before he went up, Mau stood in front of Anko's board.

Four planks. A hundred floors. A whole summer in a dozen hands. Anko's plain one. Fern's small one. Gojo's, without permission. Yuta's, and Eren's, and Armin's, and Mikasa's, which almost nobody had ever seen. Rem's, about kitchens. Gojo again. Armin's sheet pinned beside it, with every page of the green book and its drawing.

*COMPLETE*, across the bottom of the fourth plank.

And under it, small, in Anko's hand, not crossed out:

*After?*

*Something watches. Through stone. (Gojo saw.)*

*It told the general: he will be close. (The general said: closer than it thinks.)*

*One more floor.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he took the stub of pencil Anko had left on the ledge of the board, and under everything, very small, he wrote:

*101.*

He did not underline it.

He went up to bed. Frieren was in the reading chair he had said no to, with the green book on her knees, counting. She did not look up when he came in.

"I saw it too," she said. "In the mist. At the top. Seated." She turned a page. "With the ring round it."

"I know."

"It's the drawing," said Frieren. "Page one hundred and one."

"I know."

She closed the green book on her finger and looked at him.

"Rest first," she said. "Fern will write it on the board."

"Fern already did," said Mau. "Underlined twice."
