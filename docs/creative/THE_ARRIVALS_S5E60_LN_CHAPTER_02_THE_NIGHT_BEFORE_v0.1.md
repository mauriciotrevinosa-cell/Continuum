*When: Day 396, night. The seventh night. The hatch has squeaked five times. Downstairs, in Seiko's room, the crackers have been put out. Mau's shoulder is out of its bandage, for the first time since ninety.*

Nobody said *a hundred and one* for the first hour.

They sat on the roof in the warm dark and drank whatever Yuta called tea. Rimuru complained about the draught that was not his. Taihei, in the draught, held on to the roof with one hand and his cup with the other. Gojo lay back on his elbows with his blindfold off and looked at the stars, the way he did now, every seventh night, as if they were a book he had only just learned to read.

Mau sat in his place and moved his shoulder.

It did not hurt. That was the strange thing. It had hurt all week. A deep dull ache where Bellion's whip had gone in. And then yesterday at the long table, fifty points into a word on a window, and the ache had gone down, and today it was almost gone.

"Stop doing that," said Rimuru.

"Doing what?"

"Moving your shoulder like you've never had one before."

"I've never had one that stopped hurting this fast," said Mau.

Rimuru looked at him sideways.

"Ciel says it's mending," he said. "Not healed. Mending. Don't go and hit anything with it."

"I'm not going to hit anything with it."

"You're going to a hundred and one tomorrow," said Rimuru. "You're going to hit everything with it."

That was the first time anyone said it.

* * *

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Yuta put his cup down on the roof boards.

"Who's going?" he said.

Mau did not answer at once.

He looked at the four of them. Rimuru, with his chin on his knees. Gojo, on his elbows, looking at the stars. Yuta, with his hands round his empty cup. Taihei, in the draught, holding on.

"Frieren," he said. "She saw it. In the mist. She's been reading the book all summer. She'll want to read the walls."

Nobody argued.

"Yuta," said Mau. "Because of the sword. If I have to draw it." He looked at Yuta. "If something comes out with it again, I want someone there who knows what it is when it isn't."

Yuta looked at him for a long time.

Then he nodded.

"Gojo," said Mau.

Gojo did not move from his elbows. He did not look away from the stars.

"Obviously," he said.

"I asked you to stay home for ninety."

"Shoko asked me to stay home for ninety," said Gojo. "You told me. That's different." He looked at the stars. "Shoko hasn't asked about a hundred and one." He was quiet for a moment. "She won't. She knows I'm going. She said so at dinner, without saying it. She put an extra piece of bread on my plate."

Mau looked at him.

Then he looked at Rimuru.

* * *

Rimuru had not moved.

He sat with his chin on his knees and looked at the dark yard below. At the ink tree. At the long roofs going off in every direction. At the lit windows going dark one by one.

"I'm staying," he said.

Mau did not say anything.

"I've been thinking about it since eighty," said Rimuru. "Since the stone turned its head." He did not look up. "Every big one, I've been home. Twenty. Forty. Seventy. Ninety. With Diablo. Holding the house." He was quiet. "I thought, for this one, I'd go. Because it's the last one. Because whatever's up there has been watching you since fifty-six." He paused. "And then I thought about what I am."

"What are you?"

"The other half of Ciel," said Rimuru. "She's in you. She's in me. Everything she sees through you, I know. Every time." He looked at the house. "If something goes wrong up there, if the walls go, if the wheel turns three times, if something comes out of that sword and doesn't go back, I'll know. Here. The moment it happens." He looked at Mau. "And if I'm here, I can do something about it. Get Shoko to the screen. Get the house ready. Open the frame from this side if it can be opened." He paused. "If I'm up there with you, there's nobody down here who knows."

Nobody said anything.

"I built a nation once," said Rimuru. "In my world. Do you know what I learned?" He almost smiled. "The person who knows what's happening shouldn't always be in the room where it's happening. Somebody has to be at the other end of the thread." He looked at the yard. "That's me. Tomorrow. Diablo and me, holding the house. With Ciel telling me everything."

Mau looked at him for a long time.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me," said Rimuru. "It's the most annoying thing I've ever decided." He put his chin back on his knees. "I'm going to sit on the back step all day and pretend to be calm. Diablo's going to stand at the corner of the house pretending he isn't watching me pretend." He paused. "It's going to be very embarrassing for both of us."

* * *

Taihei had not said anything.

He sat in the draught with his cup and listened to all of it. When the others had gone quiet, he spoke.

"Will you come back?" he said.

Nobody answered.

Taihei looked at his cup.

"I'm sorry," he said. "That's a stupid question. I'm a cook. I don't—" He stopped. "I just. In my world, when someone went somewhere dangerous, someone at home always asked that. *Will you come back?* And they always said yes." He looked at the four of them. "Even when they didn't know."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Gojo sat up.

He did it slowly. He sat cross-legged on the roof boards and looked at Taihei in the draught. His bare eyes, very blue in the starlight.

"I'm not going to say yes," he said. "Because I don't know." He was quiet. "I've said yes before. Plenty of times. In my world. To people who asked." He looked at the stars. "And then I was in a box for a long time. And then I wasn't there at all."

Taihei went very still.

"So I'm not going to say yes," said Gojo. "I'm going to say something else." He looked at Mau. "I said it in the garden on five. When Mau couldn't breathe. *It won't happen here.*" He looked back at Taihei. "I can't know that. Nobody can. But I'm going to spend tomorrow making it true. All of us are." He paused. "That's not yes. It's better than yes. It's a plan."

"Mau tells the plan now," said Yuta. Very quietly. "Before."

"Mau tells the plan now," Gojo agreed.

Taihei looked at Mau.

Mau looked back at him.

"I don't know what's up there," said Mau. "I don't know what the plan is yet. I'll know when I see it." He paused. "But I'll tell them. Before. Every time. And then we'll make it not happen there."

Taihei looked at him for a long moment.

"Okay," he said.

He drank his tea. It was a crime against tea. He drank all of it.

"I'll make breakfast," he said. "Early. Four o'clock. For the four of you." He put the cup down. "And then I'll make dinner. For when you're back." He looked at the dark yard. "Curry. Even though it's not my day. Momo will let me."

"Momo will let you," said Rimuru.

* * *

Downstairs, in Seiko's room, the crackers were on the chipped plate.

Seiko in her chair. Maki on the end of the bed, with her spear in the corner and the silver flower catching the lamplight. Mai on the floor beside her, close. Turbo Granny in her cat on the windowsill, facing the wall, not participating.

Frieren had been holding a cracker for twenty minutes.

"Well," said Seiko.

Frieren put the cracker down.

"I'm frightened," she said.

Nobody said anything. That was the rule. You let someone finish.

"Tomorrow," said Frieren. "A hundred and one." She looked at her hands. "I saw it. In the mist. From the top of a hundred. A seated stone shape with a ring round it. The same as the drawing in the book." She was quiet. "There's a word on the last page of that book. Pressed so hard the pen nearly went through. We think it means *watches*."

She looked at the cracker.

"I've been frightened before," she said. "A great many times. In my world. Demons. Kings. A dragon on a mountain." She was quiet. "I've been frightened for people. Himmel. Eisen. Heiter. Fern." She paused. "I've never been frightened like this."

"Like what?" said Seiko gently.

Frieren thought about it. Properly. The way she thought about a spell she did not understand yet.

"Something's been watching him," she said. "All summer. Since fifty-six. Through stone. It told a general he wasn't what it needed yet, but he would be." She looked at her hands. "It *needs* him. For something. And tomorrow he's going to walk into the room where it's been waiting." She was quiet. "And I'm going to be beside him. And I don't know if beside him is enough."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Maki spoke. From the end of the bed. Very quietly.

"It wasn't enough," she said. "In the Trial."

Frieren looked at her.

"You stood at a wall," said Maki. "And said his name every hour. For as long as it took. And you couldn't get in." She looked at Frieren. "That's what you're frightened of. Not the stone. The wall."

Frieren did not say anything.

"Tomorrow there's no wall," said Maki. "Tomorrow you're inside. Beside him." She looked at her spear in the corner. "I've been inside the reach of things that wanted to kill the people I love. A lot of times. It's very different from standing outside a wall." She was quiet. "Inside, you can do something."

Frieren looked at her for a long time.

"Inside," she said.

"Inside," said Maki. "Beside him. With your staff. Doing something." She paused. "That's not the Trial. That's the opposite of the Trial."

* * *

Frieren picked up the cracker again.

She turned it over in her fingers. Then she did something nobody at the crackers table had ever seen her do. She ate it.

The whole thing. Dry. In two bites.

Seiko's eyebrows went up.

"Nobody eats the crackers," she said.

"I know," said Frieren. She brushed the crumbs off her lap. "I'm putting it down. The thing I brought." She looked at Maki. "Inside. Not outside. Doing something."

"That's the rule," said Seiko. "Nobody leaves this table carrying what they came in with."

"I'm not carrying it," said Frieren. "I ate it."

On the windowsill, facing the wall, Turbo Granny said, "That's disgusting. Those crackers are from before the bell."

"Noted," said Seiko.

* * *

Upstairs, the hatch squeaked five times.

The five of them came down. Gojo first. Then Yuta with the tray. Then Rimuru, complaining about the ladder. Then Taihei, carefully. Then Mau.

At the bottom of the ladder, in the dark corridor, Frieren was waiting.

She did not say anything. She took his hand. They walked to the east room together. Ori was asleep through the connecting door, open a crack. Milim was floating over her bed on the red ribbon.

Frieren lay down in the dark. She put her cold foot against his shin. Not because it was the reset hour. It wasn't.

"One," she said, into the pillow. "Still here."

"Still here."

"Tomorrow," said Frieren, "I'm inside."

"I know."

"Beside you."

"I know."

"Doing something," said Frieren.

She was asleep before he could answer. Mau lay in the dark with her cold foot against his shin and listened to the house. All of it. Every room. Somewhere at the far edge of the timber yard, the kilns were breathing red. Somewhere at the end of the west wing, in a room with a window toward the sea, a pencil was moving on paper: Armin, drawing a hundred and one from the green book, for whoever came after.
