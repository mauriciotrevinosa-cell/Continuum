# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 31
## Chapter 43 — Six Lessons

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S5 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-18)

- **Lamp (7):** near end first; Frieren's lights.
- **Bell (9):** stand where a teacher stands, as stone children walk home.
- **Star (19):** find the one that doesn't move. It is the seated god, which makes it north.
- **Scale (10):** keep a little left. Mau pours his reserve and stops short.
- **Staff (60):** the shaman's weight comes down on the hall. Mau stands inside full Infinity, then opens it wider, over Frieren, Yuta and Gojo; four people stand up.
- **Rope (49):** throw at the thing that won't let go. He falls and reaches for Frieren.
Frieren reads one carved line per statue with the small words. Gojo sees a thread from each statue to the seated god

*When: Day 397. Floor 101. Frieren has read for what Ciel counts as forty minutes. She has found twelve lines on the walls, one beside each drawing, and in each line one of the small words they know. She says it is enough to start.*

"It's the same line," said Frieren. "Twelve times."

She came back from the last pillar with the green book and the slip. She stood beside Mau in the middle of the hall. Gojo and Yuta came closer.

"Not the same words," said Frieren. "The same *shape*. Beside every drawing. A hook that means *floor*. A number. A small word we know, one of ours, that comes up everywhere in the book. Then a word we don't know. Then the mark that means *watches*." She looked at the ring of statues. "Twelve times. One for each."

"What's the small word?" said Gojo.

"I don't know what it means," said Frieren. "It's the most common word in the whole book. It's on almost every page." She looked at the slip. "Fern found it first. On the first day, at the river. It's at the top of her column."

"Then it's something small," said Yuta. "Like *the*. Or *and*."

"Or *remember*," said Mau.

Everyone looked at him.

"It's a diary," said Mau. "Of someone climbing. Every floor. If I was writing down a hundred floors, the word I'd use most is—" He stopped. "*Remember*. Or *learned*. Or *don't forget*."

Frieren looked at the slip for a long time.

"*Floor seven. Remember. Something. Watches*," she said slowly. She looked at the first statue, holding its lamp. "*Floor seven, remember the lamps, it watches.*"

Nobody said anything.

"It's a test," said Gojo. "Of what you remember."

"Of what we learned," said Frieren. "Every floor. Every one."

* * *

Mau looked at the ring.

Twelve statues. Each holding its floor. Standing round the foot of the hundred steps, facing outward.

"The owner goes in first," he said. "That's how every floor's been. The notice. The seal. The frame. It needs the owner." He looked at the statues. "So I go in first. Into the ring. And we see what happens."

"And if what happens is bad?" said Yuta.

"Then you're outside the ring," said Mau. "And you can see it before I can." He looked at the three of them. "That's the plan. I go in. You watch. You tell me what you see. Before."

Frieren did not say anything.

Then she closed the green book on her finger.

"I'm coming in with you," she said.

"Frieren—"

"I can read the walls," said Frieren. "You can't. If they're going to ask you what you remember, you'll want the one who can read the question." She looked at him. "Inside. Not outside. I'm not standing at a wall."

Mau looked at her for a long time.

"Inside," he said.

* * *

They stepped into the ring together.

Between the first statue and the twelfth. Between the lamp and the coin. Onto the grey stone tiles at the foot of the hundred steps.

The hall went dark.

All at once. Every scrap of the grey light from nowhere went out, the way a lamp goes out when you blow on it. The dark was total. Mau could not see his hand. He could not see Frieren beside him. The eyes behind his filter reached out into the dark and found nothing.

*Fifty-seven,* he thought. *The dark floor. Beru.*

He did not call Beru.

"*Lamp*," said Gojo. From outside the ring. Very fast. "The first one. It's moving. It's lifting the lamp."

In the dark, a light.

Small. Gold. Very far away. At the end of the hall, by the wall where the frame had been. The far end. A single lamp, burning, in the dark.

And between the light and them, footsteps. Stone on stone. Slow. Coming.

"*Near end first*," said Frieren. Very calm. In the dark beside him.

She lifted her hand.

A light came out of it. Small. Wonky. The way her lights always were. Not at the far end. Here. Right beside them. At the near end, where they stood. Then another, a pace further. Then another. Lighting the dark from where they were, outward, toward the far lamp. One after another. Never dark on both sides.

Floor seven. The arcade. Yuka. *Near end first. Never dark on both sides. Does it fit? Always.*

The footsteps stopped.

In Frieren's wonky lights, Mau could see it. The first statue. It had come three steps out of the ring toward them, with its lamp held high. It stood very still now, a pace from Frieren, with her small lights burning all round it, from where she stood to where it stood.

It lowered the lamp.

Then it went down on one knee.

The hall came back. The grey light from nowhere. The dark was gone.

The first statue knelt where it had stopped, with its lamp set down on the stone in front of it, and its smooth face bowed.

* * *

"One," said Gojo. Very quietly.

"The bell," said Yuta. "It's lifting the bell."

Mau turned.

The second statue had lifted its bell. Brass, small, the way the bell at the end of the school corridor on nine had been small. It shook it, once.

The sound was very loud. Bright and brass and sudden.

And out of the walls, all round the hall, from between the rows of carved writing, came children.

Stone children. Small. Grey. Smooth-faced, the way the shapes in the classrooms on nine had been smooth-faced. Dozens of them. Stepping out of the walls as if out of doorways. Walking. Not running. Walking, the way children walk at the end of a long day: a little slow, heads down, bumping into each other. All of them walking toward the same place.

The far wall. Where the frame had been.

And the ring of statues, and the two of them inside it, were in the way.

"*Stand where a teacher stands*," said Mau.

He did not wait for Frieren. He took her hand and stepped. Not back. Sideways. To the edge of the ring, between the second statue and the third, where there was a gap. Out of the way. In a doorway, the way Gojo had stood in the doorway of 3-A, leaning on the frame with his arms folded.

The stone children came.

They walked through the ring. Past the kneeling first statue. Past the two of them, standing in the gap between the bell and the star. None of them looked up. None of them lifted a smooth grey face. One of them bumped Mau's knee, very lightly, a small grey shoulder, and did not stop.

They walked to the far wall. And into it. And were gone.

The second statue lowered its bell.

It knelt.

* * *

"Two," said Gojo.

He did not sound pleased. He sounded like a man counting something he did not like.

"Mau," he said. "Each time one kneels—" He stopped. He had his blindfold up. "There's a thread. From the statue. Going up." He was looking up the hundred steps. "To the one on the throne. When it kneels, the thread goes tight. Like someone pulling on a rope." He was quiet. "The one on the throne is holding all twelve threads."

Mau looked up the hundred steps.

The seated figure had not moved. Its head was still slightly bowed. Its stone hands were still on the arms of the throne.

It was looking at him.

"*Star*," said Yuta.

* * *

The third statue lifted its staff. At the top of the staff, a carved star.

The roof of the hall went away.

Not broke. Went. The shadow up there, where the pillars rose into the dark, became sky. Night sky. Enormous. Full of stars. And the stars were turning.

All of them. The whole sky wheeling round, fast, the way it had wheeled on nineteen. Long arcs. Faster and faster.

And the floor began to turn with it.

Mau felt it go. The grey stone tiles under his feet sliding, very slowly, round. The pillars sliding. The walls. The whole hall turning like a plate on a table, and him on it, and Frieren beside him, and the ring of statues, and nothing to hold on to.

"*Find north*," said Frieren. "*The one that doesn't move.*"

Mau looked up at the sky.

The arcs. Getting smaller toward the middle. The way Frieren had shown him on nineteen. *Look at the middle. Not the edges. Where the arcs get small.* He looked for the centre of the turning, for the still star, the one that stayed while everything else went round.

There was no still star.

Every star moved. All of them. Even the ones at the very centre went round in tiny circles. There was nothing in the sky that stayed.

"Mau—" said Frieren.

He looked down.

The hall was turning. The pillars. The walls. The twelve statues, and the two of them. Everything going round, slowly, like the sky above.

Except one thing.

At the top of the hundred steps, on the throne, the seated figure.

It did not move.

Everything else in the hall turned round it. The pillars swept past behind it. The walls slid past. And it sat, perfectly still, with its head bowed and its hands on the arms of the throne. The one point in the whole turning hall that stayed.

"*There*," said Mau.

He pointed. Up the hundred steps. At the seated figure.

"That's north," he said. "That's the one that doesn't move."

The hall stopped turning.

The sky went away. The shadow came back up among the pillars.

The third statue lowered its star.

It knelt.

* * *

Frieren was looking up the hundred steps.

"North," she said. Very quietly. "It made itself north."

"Everything turns round it," said Mau.

"Everything in this hall turns round it," said Frieren. "Every floor. Every lesson. Every statue." She looked at the threads Gojo could see and she could not. "It built a tower so that everything in it would turn round one point." She looked at the seated figure. "Itself."

Nobody said anything.

"*Scale*," said Yuta. Quietly. "Mau. It's holding out the scale."

* * *

The fourth statue had stepped forward.

One step, out of the ring, toward Mau. It held out a scale. Stone. Two pans, hanging from a beam. Both empty. It held the beam level in front of him, at the height of his chest.

The notice came up.

From underneath. The Noise's place.

*Weigh.*

That was all.

Mau looked at the scale.

He felt it. He did not know how. He felt the empty pan on the left pulling at him. At the cup. At the reserve, fifteen times deep, full. The pan wanted it. All of it.

*Ciel.*

*I feel it,* said Ciel. *It's asking for the reserve. Not the shadows. The cup itself.* A pause. *It wants you to pour it in.*

*How much?*

*It doesn't say,* said Ciel. *It just pulls.*

Floor ten. The guardian. The dark under the blue light, deeper than the sentry's, weighing him before it rose. And raising it, and the cup going out of him all at once, and sitting down on the roof with nothing. *Next time, I want a little left.*

The scale.

He poured.

He let the cup tip toward the left pan, the way you tip a jug. He felt the reserve go out of him, steady, into the stone. The pan sank. A little. More. The beam tilted. The pan sank further. It wanted more. It wanted all of it. He could feel it wanting.

He kept pouring. Half. More than half. Three quarters.

The beam was nearly level now.

*Nearly,* said Ciel. *It's nearly balanced. A little more and it would be level.* A pause. *Mau. If you pour the rest, there's nothing left.*

He stopped.

He stopped pouring with the beam almost level. Not quite. The left pan a hair higher than it wanted to be. A margin left in the cup. A little. For the next thing.

The statue looked at him with its smooth stone face.

For a long moment, nothing happened.

Then the right pan, the empty one, sank. On its own. A hair. Until the beam was level. Perfectly.

The statue lowered the scale.

It knelt.

And the reserve he had poured came back. All of it. Out of the stone and back into the cup, the way water runs back downhill. He felt it fill.

*It wanted to see if you'd stop,* said Ciel. Very quietly. *Not how much you had. Whether you'd keep some.*

* * *

"Four," said Gojo.

His voice was very tight.

"*Staff*," said Yuta.

The fifth statue stepped forward.

It lifted a staff. Stone. Long. Knotted. With a skull bound at the top, carved, and inside the carved skull, faintly, a light.

The notice.

*Stand.*

The air came down.

The same weight. The shaman's sky. Mau felt it come and knew it before it arrived: the whole hall pressing down, every stone of it, every tile. He heard Gojo grunt. He heard Yuta go to one knee. He heard Frieren, beside him, hit the stone.

He did not wait for it.

He put the space round himself. A skin of it. A hand's breadth on every side. The weight came down on it and slowed and slowed and did not arrive.

He stood up.

And then, because he had learned something on sixty that he had not had time to do there, he opened the skin. Wider. Slowly. Over Frieren, on her knees beside him. Over Yuta. Over Gojo, last, three paces off, who looked up at the edge of the space coming toward him with his bare eyes and an expression Mau had never seen on his face.

It cost. It cost more than it had on sixty. He held it.

Under the space, in the middle of the crushing hall, four people stood up.

The statue lowered its staff.

It knelt.

* * *

"Five," said Gojo.

"*Rope*," said Yuta, and then, "*Mau*—"

The floor opened.

Not in front of him. Under him. The grey stone tile he was standing on, the size of a room, went away. Not falling. Gone. There was nothing under his feet, and then there was dark, a long way of dark, and he was in it.

Falling.

He did not have time to think. He did not have time to reach for the space, or the pull, or Okarun's speed, or anything in the bag.

He had time for one thing.

*Grandma says, if you're falling, throw at the thing that won't let go.*

Yuka, hanging off the end of the rope over the open sky on forty-nine, looking up at him along the rope. *You never let go.*

He threw his hand up.

Not at the edge. Not at the stone. At the thing that would not let go.

At Frieren.

* * *

Her hand caught his.

Cold. Hard. Round his wrist. She was on her knees at the edge of the hole where the tile had been, leaning out over the dark. Her staff was gone somewhere behind her. She had both hands round his wrist. Her face was very close above him.

She did not let go.

He hung over the dark with her hands round his wrist and looked up at her.

"*Throw at the thing that won't let go*," he said.

"I know," said Frieren. Her voice was very level. Her hands were shaking. "I was there. On the cloud stair. I heard her say it."

She pulled.

She was not strong. Not the way Maki was strong, or Stark. She was an elf who had been carrying a staff for a thousand years. She pulled anyway. Both hands. Her whole weight thrown back. She pulled him up out of the dark, over the edge, onto the grey stone, and fell backward with him, and they lay on the tiles together, breathing.

The tile came back.

Under them. Where the hole had been. Grey and smooth and level, the size of a room, as if it had never gone.

The sixth statue lowered its coil of rope.

It knelt.

* * *

Mau lay on the stone with Frieren's hand still round his wrist.

He did not let go of it. She did not let go of him.

"Six," said Gojo. From outside the ring. His voice was very strange.

"Six," said Yuta.

Mau lay on his back and looked up into the dark where the pillars went up.

"You didn't let go," he said.

"I'm not standing at a wall," said Frieren. Beside him, on her back, holding his wrist. "I'm inside."

He turned his head and looked at her.

Then up the hundred steps. At the seated figure. Its head still bowed. Its hands still on the arms of the throne.

Six threads tight, Gojo said. Six statues kneeling. Six lessons, remembered.

Six to go.

Up on the throne, very slowly, the seated figure lifted one stone finger off the arm of the throne. One finger. A hair.

Then put it down again.

"It moved," said Gojo. Very quietly. "Mau. The one on the throne. It moved a finger."

"I saw," said Mau.

He sat up. He did not let go of Frieren's hand. He pulled her up with him.

"Six more," he said.
