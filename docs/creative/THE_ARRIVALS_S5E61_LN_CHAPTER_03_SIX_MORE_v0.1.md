*When: Day 397. Floor 101. Six statues kneel. Six are standing. On the throne at the top of the hundred steps, the seated figure has moved one finger, once.*

"*Spear*," said Yuta.

The seventh statue was already moving.

Not slowly, like the lamp or the bell. Fast. It stepped out of the ring with its stone spear levelled, long and plain and heavy. It came straight at Mau, the way the guardian on ten had come at Maki, the way Igris had come down the hall on twenty.

Mau did not put up the space.

He thought about it. He had a heartbeat to think, and he used it. Floor twenty. Maki's spear in his hands, and a knight with a long sword, and the lesson underneath all the others. Not the space. Not the pull. *Inside.* A long weapon is nothing at the hand that holds it.

He went in.

Along the spear. Inside its reach. The stone point went past his shoulder, close, so close he felt the wind of it on his neck. He was inside, a hand's breadth from the statue's chest, where the long weapon could not follow.

He did not strike. He had nothing to strike with. He put his hand flat on the cold stone of its chest, over where a heart would be, and left it there.

*Inside,* he thought.

The statue stopped.

It lowered its spear.

It knelt.

* * *

"Seven," said Gojo.

His voice had gone very quiet. He had his blindfold up. He was looking at the throne.

"It's watching harder," he said. "The one on the throne. Every time one kneels. The thread goes tight and it—" He stopped. "It leans. Not its body. Its attention. It's leaning in."

"*Cup*," said Yuta.

* * *

The eighth statue held out a cup.

Stone. Wide. Shallow. Like a bowl. It held it out to Mau in both hands, and Mau looked into it.

It was empty.

The notice.

*Fill.*

He looked at the cup. He did not know how to fill it. There was no water. There was no pitcher. There was nothing in the hall but stone and carved writing and the twelve statues and the four of them.

*Ciel?*

*I don't know,* said Ciel. *It's not pulling. Not like the scale. It's just—empty.* A pause. *Waiting.*

Floor seventy. The furnace-hall. Kamish on the mound, and the dark under it deeper than a well, and the cup nearly dry. Ciel saying *not now*. Saying *if you rest here, an hour, maybe more, the cup may have enough*. Sitting on the black stone with his back against Frieren's shoulder while Gojo hummed badly and Yuta hummed underneath. The cup filling, slowly, faster than it ever had. And the old dragon's dark, waiting for him.

*Wait for the cup to fill.*

He sat down.

On the grey stone tile, in the middle of the ring, in front of the statue with the empty cup. Cross-legged. He did not do anything.

Frieren sat down beside him. Close. Her shoulder against his.

Outside the ring, after a moment, Gojo sat down too. Then Yuta.

They waited.

* * *

It was a long time.

Ciel counted it. Twenty minutes. Thirty. The statue stood holding out its empty cup. The four of them sat on the grey stone and did not move. The hall was silent. Nothing happened.

At forty minutes, Gojo started to hum.

The same song. The one from school. Badly. Under his breath. Yuta, after a moment, came in underneath it with a low steady note.

Frieren did not hum. She looked at the throne.

At fifty minutes, the seated figure on the throne moved.

Not one finger. Two. Both hands. The stone fingers lifted off the arms of the throne. A hair. Then came down again. And lifted. And came down. Tapping.

Impatient.

"It's tapping," said Gojo. Very quietly. In the middle of the song. "Look at it. It's *tapping*."

"I see it," said Frieren.

"It doesn't want to wait," said Yuta.

"No," said Frieren. "It wants us to do something." She did not look away from the throne. "So we don't."

They waited.

At an hour, Mau looked into the cup.

It was full.

He did not know when it had filled, or with what. Something clear. Still. Like the water in the pool at the top of a hundred, that did not move when Fern turned the flask. It was full to the brim.

The statue lowered the cup.

It knelt.

On the throne, the tapping stopped.

* * *

"Eight," said Gojo.

He stopped humming.

"*Book*," said Yuta.

* * *

The ninth statue held out a book.

Stone. Open. Two stone pages, carved, covered in the script. And in the middle of the left page, one line, carved deeper than the rest.

The statue held it out. Not to Mau.

To Frieren.

Frieren looked at it.

She stood up. She did not take the stone book. She looked at the line, carved deep in the middle of the page. Then at the slip in her hand. Fern's three columns. Forty-one small words.

"Read it," said Mau.

"I can't," said Frieren. "Not all of it."

"Read what you can."

Floor fifteen. The drowned library. The reading room. Fern taking down a small book in faded green cloth and holding it against her chest. *Mistress Frieren can't read it either. She'll hate that. She'll love it.* And at the river, Fern saying: *you don't start a book you can't read by reading it. You count it first.*

Frieren counted.

She did it aloud. She went along the carved line, mark by mark, and when she came to a mark she knew, she said it. When she came to one she did not, she said *one I don't know*. Slowly. Carefully. The way she had counted at the river with Fern, and at the long table with three columns.

"*Floor*," she said. "Fifteen." She went on. "*Remember.* One I don't know. One I don't know. *The.*" Her finger moved over the slip, not the stone. "One I don't know. One I don't know. One I don't know." She stopped. She looked at the last mark in the line. "*Watches.*"

She looked up.

"That's all I can read," she said. "Five words. Out of eleven."

The statue looked at her with its smooth stone face.

"It's not enough," said Frieren. To the statue. "I know. I can't read your book. Not yet." She was quiet. "But I counted it. Every mark. I know which ones I don't know. That's how you start." She looked at the carved line. "That's how you start a book you can't read."

The statue lowered the book.

It knelt.

* * *

Frieren stood very still.

"It knelt," she said.

"You read it," said Mau.

"I read *five words*."

"You counted it," said Mau. "That's the lesson. Fern's. Fifteen. You don't start by reading. You start by counting." He looked at the kneeling statue. "It didn't want you to read it. It wanted to see if you knew how to start."

Frieren looked at him.

Then her ears went faintly pink.

"Fern taught me that," she said. "At the river. Except I taught it to her first." She looked at the slip in her hand. "And I forgot."

"Nine," said Gojo.

* * *

The tenth statue did not step forward.

It turned its head.

Slowly. The way the statues on fifty-six had turned their heads. The way the one at the back of the army on eighty had turned its head when Mau said *finish it*. The smooth stone face inside the deep hood turned, a little, to one side.

Toward Mau.

It held a mask. Stone. Blank. A smooth face with no features, like its own. It did not hold the mask out. It held it against its own chest and looked at him.

"*Mask*," said Yuta. Very quietly. And then: "Gojo."

Gojo had stood up.

He was looking at the tenth statue with his bare eyes. His face was white.

"It's the same," he said. "As eighty. The thread." He was looking at something nobody else could see. "From the back of its head. Going up." He looked up the hundred steps. "To the throne." He looked back at the statue. "It's not a test of what you learned. This one. It's *him*. Looking through it. The way he looked through the one at the back of the army."

Nobody said anything.

The tenth statue looked at Mau with its smooth face.

Mau looked back.

He thought about eighty. The grey field, and the fallen banners, and the heap of grey dust on the low mound. And Gojo standing at the frame, looking back at the dust.

*Whoever you are,* Gojo had said. *I saw you.*

Mau looked at the tenth statue. At the thread he could not see, going up the hundred steps to the throne. At the seated figure at the top, with its head bowed and its stone hands still now on the arms of the throne.

"I see you," he said.

Not to the statue. Up the steps. To the throne.

"I've seen you since eighty," said Mau. "Gojo saw you first. I see you now." He did not look away. "You can stop looking through stone. I know you're there."

The tenth statue turned its head back. Slowly. To face outward.

It lowered the mask.

It knelt.

And on the throne, at the top of the hundred steps, the seated figure lifted its head.

Not all the way. A hair. The bowed head came up, a hair, inside the deep hood. As if it had heard something it liked.

"Ten," said Gojo. His voice was not steady.

* * *

"*Flower*," said Yuta.

The eleventh statue stepped forward.

It held out a flower.

Mau looked at it.

He knew before he looked properly. He knew from the colour at the edge of his sight. He knew the way you know a smell before you know what it is.

White.

And blue.

Not one flower. A spray of them. Small, in the statue's stone hand. White petals and blue petals, the white and the blue together, the way they had been in the frost, in the first clearing. Thousands of them, that he had made with his own mana, on the elf route, after years. Everyone coming up through the trees. Because he asked.

His chest closed.

The door, shutting fast inside his ribs. His breath going in and stopping halfway. The frost at the edges of the grey hall. The flowers. A seam in the sky.

*No,* he thought. *Not here. Not now.*

"Mau."

Frieren's voice. Very close.

He could not look at her. He was looking at the white and blue flowers in the stone hand. The way he had looked at the long table on the day of the front row.

"Mau," said Frieren again. "Count it."

He heard her.

*Count it first. Find the parts that keep coming back. Look at them one at a time. In the daylight. With someone sitting next to you. Until they're just small words.*

He looked at the flowers.

One part. Not the whole thing.

Flowers. White and blue. In a stone hand. In a grey hall with no frost in it, under no sky with no seam in it. With Frieren beside him, not a finger's width away. Here. Her hand on his arm. Cold. On his skin.

Just flowers.

He breathed in. It went in halfway. Stopped.

"Again," said Frieren.

He breathed in again. It went in further.

He looked at the white and blue flowers. He let himself look at them. Not at the clearing behind them, or the trees, or the sky. At them. Small. Five petals each. White ones and blue ones. In the daylight of a grey hall, with someone beside him.

They were just flowers.

He reached out.

He took the spray of flowers from the stone hand. He held them. White and blue. In his own hand. They did not turn yellow. He did not need them to. He held them, and his chest stayed open, and the frost did not come.

"That's one," said Frieren. Very quietly.

"That's one," said Mau.

The statue lowered its empty hand.

It knelt.

* * *

Mau did not put the flowers down.

He held them. He looked at them. Then he put them inside his coat, on the left. With Ori's folded list, and Anko's pencil, and Mai's bullet.

"Eleven," said Gojo.

He sounded like a man who had been holding his breath for a very long time.

"*Coin*," said Yuta.

* * *

The twelfth statue held out its hand.

Empty.

Palm up. Open. Stone. Nothing in it. It held it out to Mau, at the height of his chest.

The notice.

*The coin?*

Mau looked at the empty stone palm.

He put his hand inside his coat. On the left. He did not need to. He knew what was there. A folded list. A stub of pencil. A spray of white and blue flowers. A bullet, cold.

No coin.

"I gave it away," he said.

The statue held out its empty hand.

"I had one," said Mau. "From the Goddess. Because there was nobody on the other side of my clearing. She couldn't give me a person, so she gave me a coin." He looked at the empty palm. "I carried it for nearly a year. In this pocket. Warm." He was quiet. "And then I gave it to a man who had two people and one coin. So he wouldn't have to choose."

The statue did not move.

"His wife came," said Mau. "And his daughter. She hit him first. And then she held on." He looked at the empty stone palm. "His daughter salutes the wrong way round. She drinks water to the line. She's on the step every morning with her mother, being open early."

He looked up. Past the twelfth statue. Up the hundred steps to the throne.

"I don't have a coin," he said. "I gave it away. That's all."

The statue lowered its empty hand.

It knelt.

* * *

Twelve.

All twelve. In a ring round the foot of the hundred steps, kneeling, each with its object set down on the stone in front of it. The lamp. The bell. The star. The scale. The keys. The rope. The spear. The cup. The book. The mask. The flower, empty-handed now. The coin, empty-handed always.

"Twelve," said Gojo.

And then, very fast: "The threads. All of them. Tight. All at once. He's—"

He did not finish.

On the throne, at the top of the hundred steps, the seated figure lifted its head.

All the way.

The deep hood fell back. Under it, the smooth stone face. No eyes. No mouth. Nothing. Turned toward Mau at the foot of the steps.

And it cracked.

A line. Down the middle of the smooth stone face, from the top of the head to the chin. Thin. Dark. Then another, across. Then a dozen, spreading from the centre outward, like ice breaking on a pond.

And through the cracks, from inside the stone, a sound.

Laughter.

Not loud. Delighted. The laughter of someone very old, who has been waiting for something for a very long time, and has just watched it arrive exactly as they hoped. And then a little better.

"*Given*," said the voice. From inside the cracking stone. "*He gave it away.*"

The stone face broke.

It fell away in pieces, down the hundred steps, clattering. Behind it, inside the seated figure, where there should have been more stone, there was something else.

Something stood up.
