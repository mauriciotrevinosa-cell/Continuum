## Chapter 55 — The Night Shift

*When: Day 402, dinner, then dusk, then all night. The burn on Mau's chest is a pink line now. His eyes have stopped bleeding. The second heart has kept time with the first for five days without missing a beat.*

He said it after the first bowl.

He had learned that from himself. Put the spoon down. Say it plainly. Let the table hear it all at once.

"I want the shadows to take the nights," he said.

The long table went quiet.

"The north end," said Mau. "The round. The lime line. All night. Every night." He looked down the table. At Mikasa, halfway along, who had stood the north-end hour at dusk, evening after evening. At Anko, at the end, who walked the round at three every night. At Himmel, who stood the hour on alternate days. At Eren, beside Mikasa, who waited behind the oak stack where he thought she could not see him. "So nobody has to."

Nobody said anything.

"I'm not saying anyone did it badly," said Mau. "I'm saying I've got a knight who doesn't sleep, and an ant who doesn't sleep, and a dragon who asked me four days ago to let him keep watch over a house." He looked at his hands. "And I've got people who stand in the cold every night so the rest of us can sleep. And they've been doing it for a year. Longer." He paused. "I want them to sleep too."

He did not say the other thing. He did not say *the north road*. He did not have to. Across the table, Frieren's hand had gone, for a moment, to the middle of her chest, and half the table had seen it go.

* * *

"Can you afford it?" said Shoko.

She had not put her spoon down. She was looking at him across the table the way she looked at him in the clinic.

*Ciel.*

*Three standing,* said Ciel. Out loud, through Rimuru, because Mau had asked her to when it was about the house. Rimuru, at the end of the table, still a little grey, said it in her words. *"Standing, not fighting. Igris, Beru and Kamish. All night. I've worked it out four times."* A pause. *"For the first time, the cup refills faster than three standing shadows spend it. Not much faster. A little. He'll wake up with it about where he went to bed."*

Shoko looked at Rimuru. Then at Mau.

"One night," she said. "A trial. I look at your face at dawn. If it's grey, it's over. If your eyes have started, it's over." She picked up her spoon again. "If it's fine, we talk."

"One night," said Mau.

* * *

"The round is mine," said Anko.

Everyone looked at her.

She had not put her spoon down either. She sat at the end of the long table with her coat on and her stub of pencil behind her ear and looked at Mau with her plain face, and it was not angry. It was something else.

"I've walked the round for years," she said. "Longer than most of you have been here. Every hundred paces. Count of ten. Every night." She was quiet. "It's not a chore. It's what I do. I watch for what comes, and I write it down."

"I know," said Mau.

"Then you know what you're asking."

"I'm asking for one night," said Mau. "For Beru to walk it. Every hundred paces. Count of ten. The way you do." He paused. "And you write down what he says in the morning."

Anko looked at him for a long time.

"He'll talk," she said. "The whole way round."

"Probably."

"He'll apologise to every fence post."

"Probably."

Anko was quiet. Then she took the pencil from behind her ear and looked at it.

"One night," she said. "And he does the count of ten. Properly. Not a count of five because he's bored." She put the pencil back. "And I'm not going to bed. I'll be on the stake. Watching him do it."

At the far end of the table, Nazuna, who had come down for dinner for once because there was curry, looked up.

* * *

"I'll keep mine," said Himmel.

Mau looked at him.

"My hour," said Himmel. "On the alternate days." He smiled. The easy smile, the one that had walked into a hundred villages. "Not because it's needed. I can see it isn't, now." He looked at the window, at the north field going gold. "Because I like it. It's the best view in the house. And I told Armin once that I stand there so two people can sit in a field." He paused. "I'd like to stand there sometimes and look at the field. Even if a knight's standing next to me."

"Igris won't mind," said Mau.

"Igris will be very formal about it," said Himmel. "I'm looking forward to it."

* * *

Mikasa did not say anything.

She sat at the long table with her hands on either side of her bowl and looked at Mau. Then at Eren beside her. Eren was looking at his curry, very hard, the way he looked at things he did not want anyone to see him wanting.

"Dusk," said Mikasa at last. "The north end. That's my hour."

"I know."

"I'll stand it tonight," she said. "Until he comes." She looked at Mau. "And then I'll go."

* * *

At dusk, Mikasa stood at the north end.

She stood where she always stood, at the end of the lime line, with her blades at her hip, the new ones and the one old one, and her eyes on the dark coming down over the north field. The sun had gone behind the hills. The sky was going from gold to grey. Behind her, behind the stack of twisted oak, Eren was waiting where he thought she could not see him. She had stopped pretending a long time ago. She had stopped telling him she could.

Footsteps behind her. Light ones. Then a heavier tread. Metal.

She did not turn round.

Mau came up on her left, with his coat over his shoulders. And beside him, out of his shadow, rising off the grass in the last of the light, a knight.

Igris stood up to his full height. Dark armour. The long dark cloak falling into place, the long sword sheathed at his hip. Two cold blue lights behind the helmet. He looked at the north field. At the dark coming down. At the lime line.

Then he looked at Mikasa.

He bowed. Not deeply. The bow of one sentry to another, at a change of watch.

*"My liege tells me this is your hour,"* said Igris. *"I will keep it. Until you want it back."*

Mikasa looked at him for a long moment.

"The field goes dark from the ridge first," she said. "Watch the ridge. If anything comes, it comes along the dry ditch on the left, where the ground's lower. You won't see it till it's close." She paused. "The owls are nothing. Ignore the owls."

*"The ridge,"* said Igris. *"The ditch on the left. Not the owls."* He bowed again. A little deeper. *"Thank you."*

Mikasa let out a breath she had been holding for a long time.

She stood there a moment longer. Looking at the field. Her field. Her hour. A knight standing in it.

Then she turned and walked back along the lime line, past Mau, past the stack of twisted oak.

"Eren," she said. Not loudly.

He came out from behind the stack.

"The south field," said Mikasa. "The yellow ones are out again. Second flowering." She held out her hand. "Come and see."

Eren looked at her hand. At the knight at the north end. At Mau.

Then he took her hand.

They walked off together into the dusk, toward the south field, not hurrying. Mau watched them go until they were two shapes against the grey, and then one shape, and then gone past the corner of the house.

* * *

Beru came up at the lime line at full dark, already talking.

"*My King! A ROUND! Every hundred paces! A count of ten! I have been told the rules! I have been told them three times, by the round's Keeper, who has also told me she will be WATCHING—*"

"She will," said Mau.

On top of the red stake at the river bend, a small figure in a coat lifted one hand, very slightly. Anko. Beside her, sitting on the stake's edge with her legs hanging over, Nazuna, with a watch in her hand.

"*She is watching,*" said Beru, in a much smaller voice. "*Yes. Very well.*" He straightened. All of him. Black chitin and long arms and the antennae laid back, very serious. "*Every hundred paces. Count of ten. Not five. Ten.*"

He set off along the white line toward the river bend.

At a hundred paces, he stopped. He stood very still and looked out past the lime into the dark. Mau could hear him counting. Under his breath. Out loud. *One. Two. Three.*

On the stake, Anko had taken out her pencil.

* * *

Last, Kamish.

Mau stood at the north end beside Igris and knelt and put his hand on his shadow and said the name, quietly. The dark went out from under him across the north field like the tide coming in. It did not rise all the way. It rose as far as the low ridge at the far end of the field, and lay along it, long and dark and still.

From the house, it would look like the ridge had grown. A little higher. A little longer. A dark hill at the end of the north field, where there had only been grass.

And at the front of it, low to the ground, two great cold blue lights opened and looked out over everything.

*Small king,* said Kamish. Very quietly. In his ribs.

"Keep watch over a house," said Mau.

*Yes,* said Kamish. *At last.*

* * *

He went to bed.

That was the strange part. That was the part he would remember. He walked back along the lime line in the dark, past Igris at the north end, past the dark ridge with its two blue eyes, past Beru's voice going round the far side counting to ten. He went in through the back door. He climbed the stairs. He went into the east room, where Frieren was already in bed with the old grimoire on her knees, reading by the lamp.

She looked up.

"Who's out there?" she said.

"Three of mine," said Mau. "And Anko on the stake. Not walking." He took his coat off. "Nobody else."

Frieren closed the grimoire.

She looked at the window. At the dark north field, where a knight stood at the end of the lime line and a long dark hill lay with two blue eyes open.

"Mikasa?" she said.

"South field. With Eren. The yellow ones are out again."

Frieren was quiet for a long time.

"Good," she said.

* * *

On top of the red stake, Nazuna opened Anko's watch and noted the time.

"You're supposed to be in bed," she said.

"I said I'd watch him do it."

"He's doing it."

"He did a count of nine at the third post," said Anko. "I wrote it down."

Nazuna looked at her. Then out at the lime line, where a huge black shape was standing very still at a hundred paces, counting out loud into the dark. *Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten.* It moved on.

"That was ten," said Nazuna.

"That was ten," agreed Anko. She did not write anything down. She put the pencil back behind her ear.

They sat there. The two of them. On top of the red stake at the river bend, in the dark, with nothing to do. Anko had not sat anywhere at night with nothing to do in longer than Nazuna had known her. She did not seem to know what to do with her hands. After a while, she put them in her coat pockets.

"It's a long one tonight," said Nazuna, looking at the watch. "Longer than yesterday. Four minutes."

"You can tell already?"

"I can tell," said Nazuna. "I'll know for sure at first light."

Anko looked up at the stars. Too many of them, strange ones. Low over the hills, the small steady one that Mau had named. Higher, *The Party*. Further along, *The Hundred and First*. And lower, near the horizon, a new one Armin had found with Senku through the telescope, that Umaru had written on the back of the invitation card: *A Sea That's Just a Sea*.

"I didn't know there were this many," said Anko. "I've been looking at the ground. Every hundred paces. For years."

"There were always this many," said Nazuna.

"I know," said Anko. "I didn't look."

* * *

At dawn, Shoko looked at Mau's face across the clinic table for a long time.

Then she put two fingers on his wrist. Then her palm on his chest, for a moment. Both. Still there.

"Well?" said Mau.

Shoko picked up her coffee.

"You look like a person who slept," she said. She sounded faintly annoyed about it. "We'll talk."
