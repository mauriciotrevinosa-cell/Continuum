# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 32
## Chapter 1 — What It Built

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-20)
**Volume:** 32
**Reading order:** 1
**Continuity state:** Day 411, the first morning after summer. *When: Day 411. The first morning after summer, by Nazuna's count. Cold enough at dawn for Taihei to light the long-room stove. The tobacco in the barn is brown and not yet cured. Igris came in off the north end at first light and went down into Mau's shadow on the back step.*

It was Fern who said it.

Not at breakfast. She waited until the bowls were stacked and Momo had gone back to the counter and the long table had gone into the slow, warm, after-breakfast quiet it went into on cold mornings. Then she put the ledger on the table in front of her, closed, with her hand flat on it.

"We never did it properly," she said.

People looked at her.

"The hundred and first floor," said Fern. "We heard it at dinner. The night he came back. *Something called the Architect. It watched. It gave me something. There's a second heart.*" She did not look at Mau. "Then everyone was frightened, and then the night shift started, and then the window, and then it was the end of summer." She tapped the ledger once. "There's a page in here for it, and it's empty. It's the only empty page in this book."

Nobody said anything.

"So we do it now," said Fern. "Everything it said. All of it. While it's still in his head the way it was." She looked at him at last. "If you want."

Mau looked at the ledger under her hand.

"I want," he said.

* * *

It took the rest of the morning.

He told it slowly. He had not told it slowly before. At dinner on the night he came back he had told it the way you tell something when the curry is getting cold and Ori is watching your face: short, plain, the edges sanded off. He did not sand anything off now.

The temple. The seated god with no face. The twelve statues kneeling round the foot of the steps, each with a thing in its hands. *A lamp. A bell. A star, on a staff.* The lessons, one by one, turned back against him, and how each one had been a floor he remembered. The cup statue, kneeling alone, cracking down the middle, and how he had sat with it because he knew what it was to kneel alone in a hall where nobody could get in.

"Seventy," said Yuka, very quietly, from beside her grandfather. She was writing it in the copy book. "That was seventy."

"That was seventy."

Then the Architect. Long. Grey. Standing on nothing. Smiling. He told them what it had said, as close to the words as he could get them, and when he could not remember a word exactly he said so, and Fern wrote *(near)* in the margin.

"*You learned a hundred lessons,*" said Mau. "It said that. *You chose what to keep. You gave away what was yours. You told them the plan before.*" He looked at the table. "It knew I tell you the plan before. I learned that long before the tower. In this house."

Armin's pencil stopped.

"Go on," said Shoko.

"I asked what I'd work for. It said *for him. My monarch. Who rests.* I said I didn't know who that was. It said *not yet*." He swallowed. "It said *he left his door in you. And you will grow. And when you are grown enough—*" He stopped. "It didn't finish. That's when it went for Frieren."

Beside him, Frieren did not move.

"And at the end," said Mau, "when it put the thing in. It said *a gift. You will need it. You are still too small for what is coming.*" He put his hand flat on his chest without meaning to. Under his palm, two beats: his own, and the other, half a step behind, cold. "*Now you will grow.*"

Fern wrote it down. All of it. In her small exact hand, on the page that had been empty.

* * *

Armin had the map out before anyone asked.

Not the house map. The tower. His copy of Anko's board, every floor, the columns of arithmetic down the back. He spread it on the long table between the bowls and put a cup on each corner so it would lie flat.

"Can I try something?" he said.

Nobody stopped him.

"Floor ten," said Armin. He put his finger on it. "The Operator rule. *Companions may assist. Companions may not carry.* That's the first thing the tower ever told you, and it was a rule about being alone." His finger moved. "Then you went up alone. Twelve to twenty-nine. Dawn, noon, dinner. Nobody made you. The rule didn't say you had to. But it said nobody could carry you, and you heard that as *go by yourself*."

Mau did not answer.

"Twenty," said Armin. "Single combat. It asked. Forty." He looked up. "Forty didn't ask. *Companions who interfere will be removed.* And Yuta went in, and it took him." His finger moved again. "Seventy. *Party size: one.* It cut everyone out. You, alone, with the biggest thing you'd ever seen." He was quiet for a moment. "Every ten floors, it took one more person away from you. Every time, you won."

"It's a curriculum," said Senku, from the end of the table. He had not said anything all morning. "Graded. Each step harder than the last, and each step teaching the same lesson a little more. That's not a dungeon. That's a school."

"A school for what?" said Umaru.

"For being alone," said Armin. "And being strong enough that it doesn't matter."

* * *

Shoko put her coffee down.

She had been sitting at the corner of the table the whole time with the cup in both hands and her eyes on Mau's face, the way she sat in the clinic. She put it down now, carefully, on the edge of Armin's map, on floor forty.

"I said something," she said. "In the yard. After forty. I said *he's killing himself to get somewhere, and he feels better every time he gets closer*."

"I remember," said Frieren.

"I thought that was him." Shoko looked at the map. "His trauma. The Trial. The second he waited. I thought he was doing it to himself and the tower happened to be there." She shook her head once. "It wasn't happening to be there. It was built for it. Somebody looked at what he's most afraid of, which is being one second too slow while somebody he loves dies, and they built a hundred floors that pay him every time he stops waiting for anyone." She picked up the coffee again. She did not drink it. "Of course he felt better every time he got closer. It was feeding him."

The long room was very quiet.

"It made you into me," said Gojo.

He was leaning back against the wall by the window with his blindfold down and his arms folded. He did not move.

"The strongest," said Gojo. "Standing at the top so nobody else has to. That's what it was teaching. I told you on the roof not to do it the way I did." He was quiet. "I didn't know there was a building teaching you to."

Mau looked at the map. At the floors. At the line of them going up, ten by ten, each one taking somebody away.

He thought about the second on the north road. About the bench on thirty, where Stark and Maki and Kaneki had stopped him. *Together.* About Shoko's check every morning. About Fern's page of *Days*.

"You stopped it working," he said slowly. "All of you. Every time it took somebody away, somebody came back."

"Yes," said Maki. "That's what a house is for."

* * *

It was Frieren who asked.

She had not said anything since the Architect went for her. She sat beside him with her hands in her lap and her eyes on Armin's map, on the hundred and first floor, and nobody in the long room had seen her face do what it was doing now, which was think about something a very long way off.

"Mau," she said.

"Mm."

"The second on the road. The one it was feeding." She did not look up. "Where did you learn it?"

He knew. Everyone at the table knew. He said it anyway.

"In the Trial."

"Four hundred and thirty-seven times," said Frieren. "Somebody died every time you waited. Until you stopped waiting." She looked at the map. "That's a lesson too. It's the same lesson. It's the *first* one." She lifted her eyes to his. "If a tower can be built to teach you something, Mau, who built the Trial?"

Nobody answered.

Rimuru, on the bench, had gone very still. Ciel, inside Mau, did not say anything at all.

"The Goddess," said Mau at last.

"The Goddess," said Frieren. "Who gave you coins at the end of it. Who gave you a world to wake up in with no name in it. Who gave you coins so that other people could bring back the ones they'd lost." She did not raise her voice. "I'm not saying she did anything. I don't know. Nobody here knows anything about her except what she gave you." She paused. "I'm saying it's the same shape. And until today I never once thought to ask."

Mau looked down at his hands. At the scars. At the two fingers on the left that would not close.

He had always thought of the Goddess as the end of the Trial. The door at the bottom of it. The reward.

He had never once thought of her as its author.

* * *

Fern closed the ledger.

She did not write the question down. She looked at the page for a long moment, at everything on it, and then she turned to the back of the book, to the very last page, where nothing had ever been written, and in her small exact hand, at the top, she wrote one line.

*Who built the Trial? (Frieren. Day 411.) (Not answered.)*

She closed the book.

"That's enough for one morning," said Shoko. She stood. "Everyone out. Somebody take him outside. It's cold and the sun's up and he's been sitting in this room telling us the worst thing that ever happened to him for three hours." She looked at Mau. "Tomorrow, nobody asks you anything. That's not a request."

"Whose rule is that?" said Gojo.

"Mine," said Shoko. "As his doctor." She picked up her coffee. "And as somebody who thinks this house can plan one day for him that doesn't teach him a single thing."

At the end of the table, Umaru had already taken out a fresh sheet of paper.

At the top of it, in the largest letters the Ministry had, she wrote:

*TOMORROW. NOTHING.*
