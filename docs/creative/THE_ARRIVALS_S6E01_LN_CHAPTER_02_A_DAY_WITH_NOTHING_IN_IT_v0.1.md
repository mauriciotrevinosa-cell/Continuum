# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 32
## Chapter 2 — A Day With Nothing in It

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-20)
**Volume:** 32
**Reading order:** 2
**Continuity state:** Day 412, under Shoko's order and Umaru's sheet TOMORROW. NOTHING. The house plans a day for Mau that teaches him nothing: no lessons, no points, no shadows, no questions about the Architect or the Goddess. Each person gives him something small and useless on purpose. Anko lets him write the board ("Day 412. Nothing."), Stark naps beside him, Taihei cooks a dish for no reason, Kaneki gives him a cup with nothing in it to wash, Bocchi plays one song badly on purpose, Senku shows him the NOT YET box and says it is still not yet, and Shoko takes him to the barn to look at tobacco that is still not cured. *When: Day 412. Cold at dawn again, clear by mid-morning. Umaru's sheet is pinned in the middle of the first wall section, in the Ministry's largest letters: TOMORROW. NOTHING. Someone has crossed out TOMORROW in the night and written TODAY above it, in Ori's hand.*

He woke late.

That was the first thing. He woke at the reset hour the way he always did, and Frieren's cold foot found his shin, and she said "One," and he said "Still here," and then, instead of getting up, he felt her hand come flat on his chest and stay there.

"No," said Frieren, with her eyes shut.

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to get up." Her hand did not move. "Shoko said nothing. Getting up early is something."

He lay there.

He did not mean to sleep again. He woke with the sun across the floor of the east room, high, much too high, and the house loud underneath him in the ordinary way, and Frieren gone, and on the pillow where her head had been, a folded square of paper.

He opened it.

*NOTHING. (The Ministry.) (Frieren is at breakfast. She said to tell you she ate your egg.)*

* * *

She had not eaten his egg. It was on a plate on the back step, under an upturned bowl, with Touka's coffee beside it, still hot, which meant someone had been watching the stair.

He sat down on the step and ate it.

Ichika came out and sat down next to him with her own cup of water, filled exactly to the line, and drank it very seriously, looking at the yard, and did not say anything, because her mother had told her that today nobody was allowed to ask Mau anything. When she had finished she put the cup down beside his, lined up, and went back inside.

That was all.

He sat there with the two cups lined up on the step and found that he was smiling.

* * *

Anko found him at the board.

He had not meant to go there. His feet had taken him across the yard the way they did every morning, to read the line for the day, to see who was going up and from which floor. He stood in front of the third plank and looked at it, and there was nothing written for the day at all.

Anko came up beside him with her pencil behind her ear.

She took it out. She held it out to him.

He looked at it.

"Go on," said Anko.

"What do I write?"

"What happened."

He took the pencil. He stood in front of the plank for a long time. Then, in his crooked hand, under the last line of the summer, he wrote:

*Day 412. Nothing.*

Anko looked at it. She nodded once, as if he had done it correctly, and took her pencil back, and put it behind her ear, and went to bed, because she had sat up half the night on the red stake again, watching Beru walk her round, because she still could not quite leave it to him. He still apologised to the fence posts.

* * *

The day went like that.

Nobody planned it. Or everybody did, separately, without telling each other, which in that house came to the same thing.

Stark found him in the south field at noon and lay down in the long grass beside him without a word and was asleep inside a minute. Mau lay there too. He did not sleep. He looked at the sky. It was the pale, high, clean blue that only comes after summer, with nothing in it, and he looked at the nothing in it for an hour, and Stark snored, and it did not teach him anything at all.

Taihei made something at two in the afternoon that nobody had asked for and that was not a meal. A thin flat bread with sugar burned on top of it, cut into squares. He brought the plate out to the bench by the kitchen door and put it down beside Mau and went back in.

"What's it for?" called Mau.

"Nothing," said Taihei from the kitchen. "That's the recipe."

Kaneki came across from the kilns at three with one of the good cups. Not full. Empty. He held it out.

Mau took it. Looked into it.

"It needs washing," said Kaneki.

So Mau washed it. At the pump in the yard, slowly, with the cold water running over the scars on his hands, turning the cup round and round until it was clean, and dried it on his shirt, and gave it back. Kaneki looked at it, and nodded, and took it back to the shelf beside the kilns, with the others, and that was all.

At four, from the back step, Bocchi played one song.

She played it badly. Mau knew she could play it well; he had heard her play it well a hundred times. She played it badly on purpose, every third note wrong, and somewhere in the second verse Nijika started laughing on the step beside her, and Ryo said "That's not the chord," and Bocchi said, very quietly, red to the ears, "I know," and kept playing it wrong all the way to the end.

When she finished, she did not look at him. She put the guitar across her knees and said, to the yard in general, "That one wasn't for anything."

"I know," said Mau. "Thank you."

She went redder.

* * *

Senku showed him the box.

The NOT YET box, on the shelf in the workshop, with the warm seed from the hundredth floor still inside it in its nest of dry moss. Senku took the lid off and they both looked in.

"Still not yet," said Senku.

"Is it doing anything?"

"Nope." Senku put the lid back on. "Ten billion percent nothing. Same as yesterday." He looked at Mau sideways. "Figured you'd want to see something that isn't in a hurry."

* * *

At dusk Shoko came and got him.

She did not say where. She walked across the yard with her coffee and he followed her, past the clinic, past the workshop, to the barn, where the long rows of tobacco leaves hung from the rafters in the half-dark, brown now, all of them, and dry, and smelling of something deep and sweet and old.

Shoko stood under them and looked up.

"Not yet," she said.

"How long?"

"Weeks. Maomao says weeks. Curing is longer than drying." She took the cigarette out of her coat pocket. The one Gojo had given back to her on the night of the hundred and first floor, unlit, intact. She held it up beside the leaves, as if comparing them. "I was going to smoke this one when they were ready. The first one from here."

"You still will."

"I know." She put it back in her pocket. "I wanted you to see something in this house that you can't hurry. Not with points. Not with a shadow. Not by going up a stair at dawn." She looked at the leaves. "It'll be ready when it's ready. That's all."

They stood in the barn for a while in the half-dark, under the browning leaves, and did not say anything.

* * *

At the reset hour, Frieren's cold foot found his shin.

"One," she said.

"Still here."

Her ear on his chest. A long time.

"Two," she said.

He lay in the dark and thought about it. The whole day. The egg on the step and Ichika's cup lined up beside his. The pencil. The sky over the south field with nothing in it. The burned bread and the empty cup and the song played wrong. The box that was still not yet. The leaves that were still not cured.

"I didn't think about it," he said.

"About what?"

"The tower." He was quiet. "Not once. All day."

Frieren did not lift her head off his chest. Under her ear, two hearts went on: his own, and the cold one, half a step behind.

"Good," she said.

Through the connecting door, very faintly, from the floor just inside her own room, Ori said: "Two."

"Two," said Mau.
