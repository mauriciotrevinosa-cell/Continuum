# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 32
## Chapter 5 — Out of the Jar

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-20)
**Volume:** 32
**Reading order:** 5
**Continuity state:** Day 421. Three days after the overload, Shoko lets him out of bed. The house is tired of living inside the lime line like something in a jar: the roads have been closed since the devils and the titans, and the only news of the world comes from the merchant's thinning visits. At the long table Mau proposes shadow scouts on a rota. They cost nothing to send; the only cost is remaking one if it is destroyed. Nobody argues. Then, testing a soldier in the yard, he finds something new: with his eyes closed he can see through it. Ciel cannot say whether it comes from the overload, the new fields or the bar. The night shift continues; scouting is added by day. At dusk ten soldiers go out past the ridge

*When: Day 421, morning. Frost on the yard. Shoko took the wet cloth off his forehead at dawn and said "up, slowly", and Taihei made porridge with honey in it, which he only does when someone has been ill.*

It was Eren who said the word.

Not to Mau. To Mikasa, at breakfast, across the long table, in the low voice he used when he thought only she could hear him.

"It's like living in a jar."

Mikasa did not answer. She looked at her porridge.

But Mau heard it, and so did half the table, and nobody said he was wrong.

* * *

He thought about it all morning.

The lime line. The earth-and-timber perimeter, the two plain gates, the walks and the stonework that were still not finished. The red stake at the river bend. The north end, the round, the ridge. Everything inside it was the house. Everything outside it was a place you went into only with a reason, and with somebody, and with a time written on the board for when you would be back.

It had been that way since the roads closed. Since the devils first, small, and the house slowly learning that killing one was not the end of it. Since the titans. Since the merchant's visits had gone from every week to every month to whenever he dared.

Nobody in the house went anywhere.

He had not noticed. He had been in the tower. The tower had been his outside. A hundred and one floors of it, a whole summer, and he had come home every evening and eaten curry inside the lime and gone up again in the morning, and he had never once thought about what it was like for the people who stayed.

Eren was right. It was a jar.

* * *

"I want to send the shadows out," he said, after lunch.

The long table went quiet in the way it went quiet now, one end to the other, like a wave.

"Out where?" said Fern.

"Past the line. Past the ridge. Along the road south." He looked down the table. "To look. Not to fight. Just to look. Where the titans are. Whether there are more. Whether the road's open. What's out there." He paused. "We don't know anything any more. We just know the line."

"They cost nothing," said Armin slowly. He had the house map half unrolled beside his bowl already. "Calling them. Keeping them out. That's what Ciel said."

"Nothing," said Mau. "Only if something breaks one. Then remaking it costs. And I've got ninety-eight soldiers who don't do anything all day but stand in my shadow in rows."

"Who'd go?" said Maki.

"The soldiers. In fours. On a rota, like the night shift." He looked at Anko. "Your round, but bigger. Out there instead of in here."

Anko took the pencil from behind her ear and looked at it.

"Every hundred paces," she said. "Count of ten."

"If you want."

"I want." She put the pencil back. "And I want to know what they see."

Nobody argued.

He had expected somebody to. He had his reasons ready, all in a row, the way he had them ready on the morning of the check. Nobody needed them. Shoko looked at him for a long time across the table and said "*not* you, them," and he said "them," and she nodded, and that was all.

Himmel smiled, the easy one, and said, "Good. I've wanted to know what's past the ridge for a year."

* * *

He tried it in the yard at two.

One soldier. He called it up out of the frosted dirt by the pump, and it stood there, the way they stood: dark, faceless under the helm, a spear upright in its hand, waiting. The children watched from the back step. Ichika had her cup.

"Walk to the gate," said Mau. "And back."

It walked to the gate.

He watched it go. Across the yard, past the workshop, toward the north gate. He watched it with his eyes the way you watch anyone walk away from you, and then, without meaning to, the way you do when you are tired and the sun is warm, he closed them.

He could still see the gate.

He stood very still.

The gate. Close, so close he could see the grain of the timber Wakana had planed and a nail somebody had bent driving it in and left there, crooked, because the gate hung true anyway. The lime line under his feet that were not his feet. And beyond the gate, the north field, frost-grey, and the ridge at the end of it, low, where Kamish lay every night.

He was seeing it from the gate.

"*Turn round,*" he said. Out loud, with his eyes shut.

The gate swung away. The yard swung in. The pump, and a man standing beside it with his eyes shut, very far away, small. Himself. The children on the step beyond him. Ichika, holding her cup, staring.

He opened his eyes.

The soldier was at the gate, turned round, looking at him.

* * *

*Ciel.*

*I saw,* said Ciel. *Through it. With you.* A pause. *That's new.*

*Where's it from?*

*I don't know.* She said it plainly. She always said it plainly now. *It could be the overload. Everything the Noise put into you three days ago. It could be the new fields: your cursed energy is bigger, and your mana, and maybe it reaches further. It could be the bar.* Another pause. *I can't see into the bar. I can't tell what it's doing. It could be nothing to do with any of it, and you could have always been able to, and never closed your eyes.*

*Can it hurt them?*

*No.* That came fast. *Not that I can see. It isn't costing anything. Not the cup, not the other reserve. You're just—looking.* A pause. *It's the strangest thing that's happened in your window since the window came.*

He called the soldier back. It walked back across the yard and stood in front of him.

He looked at it.

"Thank you," he said.

It did not answer. The soldiers never did. But it stood a moment longer than it needed to before it went down into the dirt.

* * *

At dusk they went out.

Ten. The first ten, the ones in the front row, the ones who had been the ten in front since the Legion first stood up on eighty. They rose out of the grass at the north end beside Igris, who looked at them as a commander looks at a squad going out, and said nothing, and stood a little straighter.

The whole house came to watch. It was not a thing the house had decided. It was a thing the house did now: something happened at the north end, and the long room emptied, and people stood along the lime line in their coats with their breath going up white.

"Every hundred paces," said Anko. "Count of ten."

The ten soldiers went out across the north field in a loose line, spears upright, toward the ridge.

Kamish lifted his great head off the ridge as they passed him. Watched them go by. Laid it down again.

Then they went over the top of the ridge, one by one, and were gone.

Mau stood at the north end beside Igris with his hands in his coat pockets.

He closed his eyes.

On the other side of the ridge, in the last of the light, ten soldiers looked down into a country nobody in the house had walked since the north road.
