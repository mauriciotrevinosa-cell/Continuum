## Chapter 6 — Past the Ridge

*When: Day 421, full dark, then the small hours, then dawn. Igris at the north end. Beru on the round. Kamish on the ridge with his head down. Ten soldiers somewhere past him.*

He saw it in pieces.

That was the first thing he learned. He could not see through all ten at once. He tried, in the first minutes, standing at the north end with his eyes shut, and it was like trying to read ten pages laid one on top of another: everything at once, nothing at all, and a sharp pain behind his left eye where the filter was thinnest.

*One at a time,* said Ciel. *Like turning pages.*

So he turned pages.

The first soldier: the far side of the ridge, the slope going down into dark grass, the line of the others spreading out ahead of it in the starlight. The second: further, at the edge of the woods. The third—

He opened his eyes.

"Woods," he said. "The edge of the woods. North."

Beside him, sitting on an upturned crate Stark had carried out for her, Anko wrote it down.

*Day 421. 1st hour. Woods, north edge.*

* * *

He went on.

Anko wrote. That was the shape of the night. He stood at the north end beside Igris with his eyes closed and said what he saw, plainly, the way Beru said it every morning in the yard, and Anko sat on her crate with the lamp shaded at her feet and wrote down every word in her plain hand, and did not ask him anything.

"The road," he said. "The north road. The soldiers are on it."

*2nd hour. The north road.*

He knew it before he saw it. The ground went the way he remembered it going under his own feet, on Day 328, with Frieren beside him and Fern behind. The road narrowed into the trees. The trees—

He stopped talking.

"Mau," said Anko.

"The woods are still down," he said.

They were. In the starlight, through the soldier's eyes, the trees lay where they had fallen. Long pale rows of them, level, all at the same height, like corn behind a scythe. A whole wood, flattened by a thing that had stepped out of the dark between the trunks as easily as a man steps out of a doorway. Nobody had moved them. Nobody had come to take the timber. The rows went on into the dark further than the soldier could see.

And on the road, where the trees ended, a darker patch. Ash. Long since rained into the dirt, but still there, if you knew to look. The place where there had been four of them, and then three.

He did not say any of that. Anko did not ask.

*2nd hour,* she wrote. *The woods are still down.*

* * *

"Further," said Mau.

He sent them on. In fours, the way they had planned, with two hanging back at the edge of the cut wood to watch the road behind. Every hundred paces they stopped. He made them stop. He counted to ten with them, under his breath, and Anko heard him counting and did not smile, quite.

The road went on north and then bent east. The woods came back, whole, past the cut. Hills. A stream. An old milestone with the words worn off. Nothing.

"Nothing," he said.

*4th hour. Hills. A stream. Nothing.*

"Nothing's good," said Anko.

"I know."

* * *

In the fifth hour, the third soldier found the tracks.

He would not have seen them. He was not looking down. The soldier was. It stopped at the edge of a wide flat meadow below the hills, and looked at the ground, the way they looked at things, without expression, and Mau, turning to its page, saw what it saw.

A footprint.

It was the size of the long room.

He opened his eyes and stood at the north end for a moment with his heart going, both of them, the warm one and the cold one half a step behind.

"Titan," he said.

Anko's pencil stopped.

"A track," he said. "Old. The grass is growing up in it. Weeks. Not days." He closed his eyes again. Turned the page. The soldier had moved. There were more. A line of them across the meadow, sunk deep in the soft ground, the grass coming up green in the bottoms of them. "Going away. North-east. Away from here."

*5th hour,* wrote Anko. Her hand was steady. *Titan tracks. Old. Weeks. Going north-east. Away.*

* * *

He found the titan itself in the small hours.

Not with the third soldier. With the seventh, which had gone furthest, up over the hills on the east side of the meadow where the tracks went. It stood on the crest of a hill and looked down into a long valley, and in the bottom of the valley, in the starlight, something lay curled on its side like a hill that had grown in the wrong place.

It was not moving.

Mau watched it through the soldier's eyes for a long time. The size of it. The slow rise and fall of its side. Asleep. Alone. Nothing round it, no others, no tracks coming or going that were not old.

"One," he said. "Asleep. In a valley past the hills. Far. Alone."

*Small hours. One titan. Asleep. Far valley, past the hills. Alone.*

"Do we do anything?" said Anko.

He thought about it. About the lime line, and the jar, and Eren at breakfast. About the shadows, which cost nothing to send and nothing to keep out, and the soldier standing on the hill in the starlight looking down at a sleeping thing the size of a house.

"No," he said. "We know where it is. That's all we wanted."

Anko wrote it down.

*We know where it is.*

* * *

There was nothing else.

He turned every page before dawn. All ten. The road, the cut wood, the hills, the stream, the meadow of tracks, the valley. No camps. No smoke. No fires on the horizon. No army. No demons running with warm young voices and words in their mouths. Nothing coming toward the house at all.

At first light he called them back, and they came over the ridge one by one past Kamish, who lifted his head and watched them come, and went down into the frosted grass at the north end at Mau's feet, ten, all of them, none broken.

He opened his eyes.

His head was splitting.

*It's the switching,* said Ciel. *Not the seeing. Turning the pages. Ten windows, one after another, all night.* A pause. *You're not going to be able to do that every night.*

*Then what?*

*One picture,* said Ciel. *Instead of ten windows. Everything they see, put down in one place, where it stays. A map that fills itself in.* A pause. *I think I could build it. I'd need to think.*

* * *

Anko stood up off her crate.

She had the sheets in her hand. The whole night of them, written small and plain. She looked at them, and then out past the north end, past the ridge, at the country she had never seen. The country she had walked the edge of every night for years, a hundred paces at a time, looking out at the dark and writing down what came.

"I want to see it," she said.

Mau looked at her.

"Not your words," said Anko. "Theirs. What the soldier sees. I want to see it with my own eyes." She folded the sheets. "I've been watching the dark from the inside for years. I'd like to see it from the outside once."

"I don't know how to give it to you," said Mau.

From behind them, where he had come out to the north end at some point in the night with a blanket round his shoulders and Diablo beside him, Rimuru said:

"I might."
