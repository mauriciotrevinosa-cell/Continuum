# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 32
## Chapter 8 — The Map

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-20)
**Volume:** 32
**Reading order:** 8
**Continuity state:** Days 425–426. Ciel cannot show anyone the inside of Mau's head, so the map becomes a thing on the long-room wall: Armin's house map, enlarged on four boards, with pins. Each morning Ciel gives positions through Rimuru, Armin pins them, Anko checks them against her sheets. Then Mau finds the trick that makes it live: a soldier can be sent down into a monster's own shadow and stay there, riding it, so the house always knows where that thing is. The first one goes into the sleeping titan's shadow; then a pack of grey wolf-things in the eastern hills; then a slow cave-dweller by the stream. The children are allowed to move the pins under Armin's eye. For the first time in a year the house can see the world outside the jar. Nothing near. Nothing coming

*When: Day 425, morning, then Day 426. The scouts went out again last night, and the night before. Armin has not slept much. He has been at the long-room wall with a hammer.*

Ciel could not show anyone.

That was the problem, and she said it plainly on the morning after the loan, through Rimuru, at breakfast.

*"I can build the picture,"* said Rimuru, in her words. *"I have been. Everything the soldiers see, every night, laid down in one place, where it stays. It's in Mau. It's in the window, or near it. I can see it. He can see it, if he looks."* A pause. *"Nobody else can. And I can't lend it to the whole house at once. The loan's one thing to one person. Carefully."*

"So it has to be outside," said Armin.

Everyone looked at him.

He had the house map rolled under his arm. He always had it now. He unrolled it on the table between the bowls: the house, the yard, the lime line, the north field and the ridge, the river bend with the red stake, every room in his small careful hand.

"It stops at the ridge," said Armin. "Everything I've drawn stops at the ridge. Because nobody's been past it to tell me what's there." He looked at Mau. "Ciel can tell me. Every morning. Through Rimuru. And I'll draw it."

* * *

He did not draw it on the house map.

By the afternoon there were four boards on the long-room wall, planed smooth by Wakana and pushed edge to edge, and on them, in charcoal, the house map again, small, in the bottom corner. And above it and around it, for the first time, the world.

The ridge. The north road. The cut wood, drawn as rows of short level lines, which was how Anko had written it. The bend east. The hills. The stream. The meadow of tracks. The valley.

Armin drew all of it from Rimuru's mouth, sentence by sentence, with Ciel's careful distances, and when Ciel was not sure, Armin left the line faint and wrote a small question mark beside it.

"Pins," said Umaru, from beside him. She had a box of them. Nobody knew where she had got a box of pins. "The Ministry has pins."

"For what?"

"For things that move."

* * *

The trick came that night.

Mau was at the north end, with his eyes shut, turning the soldiers' pages. The seventh soldier was on its hill above the valley again, where it had stood three nights now, looking down at the titan asleep in the bottom.

It was always asleep. He had begun to wonder if it would ever move. And if it moved in the night, while the seventh soldier was looking at something else, or turning back, or being recalled at dawn, nobody would know where it had gone until somebody found the tracks.

*Ciel,* he said. *They go down into my shadow.*

*Yes.*

*Into anyone's shadow? Not just mine?*

A pause.

*I don't know,* said Ciel. *Nobody's tried.*

He tried.

He sent the seventh soldier down off the hill. Down the long slope of the valley in the starlight, slowly, the way they walked when he told them to be quiet, toward the great curled shape breathing in the bottom. It stopped at the edge of the titan's shadow, which lay out across the valley floor long and black under the moon.

*Go in,* he said.

It went in.

Not into the ground. Into the titan's shadow. It stepped into the black shape on the grass and sank, the way it sank into his, smoothly, all at once, the dark of it closing over the helm last.

He turned its page.

Dark. Close. Warm, oddly. And all round it, a vast slow breathing, rising and falling, and the deep sound of a heart much too big, going on and on, very slowly.

It was riding in the titan's shadow.

He opened his eyes.

"It worked," he said aloud, to Igris, because Igris was the only one there. "It's in it. Wherever it goes, the soldier goes."

Igris turned his helm, very slightly, toward him.

*"My liege,"* said Igris, *"is learning to be a general."*

* * *

Armin put the first pin in at breakfast.

A red one. In the bottom of the valley. With a small paper flag on it, in Umaru's hand:

*TITAN. (Asleep.) (We'll know if it moves.)*

By the next evening there were three.

A grey pin in the eastern hills, past the meadow: a pack of thin grey things like wolves gone wrong, nine of them, that hunted at night along the ridges and had never once come west. A soldier rode in the shadow of the biggest one.

A brown pin by the stream, at the old milestone: something slow and broad and blind that lived in a hole in the bank and came out at dusk to drink. A soldier sat in its shadow while it drank, and went back into the hole with it, and did not seem to mind.

And the red one, in the valley. Not moving.

* * *

The children found the wall the second day.

Nobody had told them not to. Ori came in from the yard with Ichika by the hand and the five behind them and stopped in the doorway of the long room and looked at the four boards, at the charcoal country and the pins and the little paper flags, and her face did something Mau had not seen it do since the fort.

"Is that out *there*?" she said.

"That's out there," said Armin.

She walked up to it. She stood in front of it with her hands behind her back, the way she stood in front of things she had been told were important, and looked at it for a long time. At the valley. At the red pin.

"Can I move one?" she said. "When it moves?"

Armin looked at Mau.

Mau looked at the wall. At the world on it, the one nobody in the house had seen for a year, drawn small and careful on four boards in the long room where everybody ate, where everybody could look at it every day, where a child could stand in front of it with her hands behind her back.

"When Ciel says it's moved," he said, "and Armin says where. Yes."

Ori nodded once, very seriously.

Then she took her list out of her pocket, the one she kept for the shadows, and on a fresh line at the bottom, under *Kamish* and *Bellion* and the ten in front, she wrote:

*The ones that ride. (In other people's shadows.) (Three.)*

* * *

That night, at the reset hour, Mau lay in the dark with Frieren's foot on his shin and turned the pages once more before he slept.

The valley: the slow breathing, the huge slow heart.

The eastern hills: the grey pack running along a ridge under the moon, a soldier riding in the leader's shadow, nine of them, going nowhere near the house.

The stream: the blind slow thing, back in its hole, asleep.

Nothing near. Nothing coming.

"Two," said Frieren, with her ear on his chest.

"Two," said Mau.

And for the first time in a year, he knew exactly what was out there in the dark past the ridge, and none of it was looking at them.
