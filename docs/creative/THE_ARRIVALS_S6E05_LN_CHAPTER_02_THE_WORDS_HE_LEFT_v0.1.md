# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 32
## Chapter 10 — The Words He Left

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-20)
**Volume:** 32
**Reading order:** 10
**Continuity state:** Reader-only. Nobody in the house knows any of this. Far to the north-east, in a camp that has been growing since the summer, the demon with the warm young voice (one eye black and red, one his own) recites Mau's message from the north road, Day 328, as he has been made to recite it to every new arrival. It is no longer the message Mau gave. A voice that is never seen has cut the condition ("if anyone disrespects her") and kept the threat ("every city, every house, every field"), and added a line: he keeps the people who came through from other worlds in his house, and none of them have been seen outside it. Tonight a new group hears it: among them a boy in a tracksuit with a small girl in a too-big dress holding his sleeve, who goes white at the word "keeps". The camp's fires go on further than the eye can see. The union stays unnamed

*When: Day 429, night. Far north-east, past the hills on Armin's map, past where the soldiers ride. A camp that was a few tents at the end of summer.*

He said it the way they had taught him.

Not the way he had first said it. The first time, on the road, with his knees in the dirt and the woods lying down in rows behind him and the boy crouched in front of him with that empty, level, almost kind voice, he had got every word right. He had been so frightened that every word had gone into him and stayed, exactly, the way a burn stays.

He still had them. All of them. Word for word. He could have said them in his sleep, and some nights he did.

That was not what they wanted.

* * *

"Again," said the voice.

It came from behind the lamp. It always came from behind the lamp. He had stood in this tent a dozen times since the end of summer, in front of a dozen different faces that had come in out of the dark to hear him, and he had never once seen where the voice came from. There was a lamp on a table, and a chair behind it, and the chair was always in shadow, and the voice was always there.

"From *I won't stop*," said the voice. "The way we practised."

He looked at the faces.

Six of them tonight. New. They had come in from the south road that afternoon with their own fires and their own banners and their own reasons, and they were standing in front of him in a half-circle in the lamplight with the look all the new ones had, the look of people who had heard a rumour and had come a long way to find out if it was true.

He said it.

"*I won't stop at them,*" said the demon. His voice was warm. It was always warm. That was the worst thing about it; it was a voice made for saying kind things, and it had only ever said this. "*Not at the ones who come. Not at the ones who sent them. I'll find everything they love. Every city. Every house. Every field. Every single thing they love. And I will flatten it.*"

The half-circle was very quiet.

"*Until there's nothing left standing anywhere that they ever cared about.*"

* * *

That was all of it.

That was all of it now.

It had not been all of it on the road. On the road there had been a beginning. *If anyone ever again so much as raises an eyebrow at her.* A reason. A condition. A line drawn round one person, and everything after it only for whoever crossed the line.

The voice behind the lamp had taken the beginning out on the first night.

"Without the elf," it had said. "Again."

"But he said—"

"I know what he said." The voice had not risen. "Again. Without her."

So he had said it again without her. And again. And again, until the words that were left stood up on their own without the beginning holding them up, and sounded like what they sounded like now. Not a promise to whoever hurt someone he loved. A promise to everyone.

* * *

"There's more," said the voice.

The demon closed his eyes.

"*He keeps them,*" he said.

He had not heard this on the road. He had been given it here, in this tent, on the second night, and made to say it until it sounded like the rest. It did not sound like the rest. To him it never would. But it sounded like the rest to the faces.

"*The ones who came from other worlds,*" said the demon. "*The ones who crossed over, like you. He keeps them. In his house. Behind a white line. Inside a wall.*" He did not open his eyes. "*Nobody has seen one of them outside it. Not in a year. Not on any road.*"

In the half-circle, someone made a small sound.

He opened his eyes.

A boy. Not much older than the boy on the road had been. Dark hair, a sharp tired face, and clothes that were wrong for any world the demon knew: soft and close-fitting, grey, with a white stripe down each sleeve. And holding his sleeve, very tight, a small girl in a dress much too big for her, pale, with long pale hair done up in two great curling coils, and the oldest eyes the demon had ever seen in a face that young.

The boy had gone white.

"Keeps them," said the boy. His voice cracked on it. "Keeps them *how*?"

The demon did not answer. He did not know. He had never known.

"That's what we'd all like to know," said the voice behind the lamp, gently. "That's why we're gathering."

The small girl's hand tightened on the boy's sleeve.

"*I suppose,*" she said, very quietly, "*you know someone who might be in there.*"

The boy did not answer her either.

* * *

Afterward, outside, the demon stood at the edge of the camp in the cold and looked at the fires.

They went on further than he could see.

At the end of summer there had been a dozen tents on this hillside. Now the fires went down the hill and across the valley and up the far side and over it, in rows, like the woods lying down on the road, so many that the dark between them was smaller than the light. Banners he did not know. Voices in languages he did not know. Things that were not men moving between the fires on too many legs. And every night, more, coming in from the south road and the east road with their own reasons, to stand in a tent in front of a lamp and hear the words.

His words. Not his words.

He thought about the boy on the road. Crouched in front of him in the dirt, level, empty, almost kind. *Say it back.*

He had said it back. Every word.

He had not said it to anyone the way it was given to him since.

Somewhere behind him, in the tent with the lamp, the voice was talking to the boy in the grey clothes, gently, for a long time. He could not hear what it said. He did not try.

He stood at the edge of the fires and looked south-west, toward the hills, toward the road, toward a ridge he had run away from at the end of summer with a warm young voice full of words, and found that he was hoping, very quietly, that the boy on the road would never hear what they had made of them.
