*When: Day 437. The first grey before dawn. Armin is at the long-room wall with his coffee, the way he is every morning. The three pins are where they were last night.*

The grey pin moved first.

Armin did not see it move. Nobody could see a pin move; a pin was a pin. What happened was that Rimuru came down the stairs before dawn in his nightclothes, which he never did, and went straight to the wall without stopping for tea, and said, in Ciel's words, very fast:

*"The pack. In the eastern hills. They're running."*

Armin put down his coffee.

*"West,"* said Rimuru. *"All nine. Fast. Not hunting. Not the way they run at night along the ridges."* A pause. *"Running away from something."*

Armin took the grey pin out of the board and held it.

"Where?"

*"I'll tell you when they stop,"* said Rimuru. *"They haven't stopped."*

* * *

The red pin moved at first light.

Mau was at the north end by then. Somebody had woken him; he did not remember who. He stood beside Igris in the frost with his coat over his nightshirt and his eyes shut, turning pages, and the seventh soldier, riding in the titan's shadow at the bottom of its valley, felt the vast slow body around it shift.

The titan woke.

He felt it through the soldier the way you feel a house move in an earthquake. The huge slow heart going faster. The great weight rolling over onto its knees. Getting up. He had watched it sleep for sixteen days and he had never once seen it stand.

It stood. It turned its head, slowly, toward the east.

Then it walked away.

North-east. Not toward the house. Away from the east, at an angle, the long slow strides eating the valley floor, the soldier riding along in its shadow underneath it in the dark.

He opened his eyes.

"The titan's moving," he said. "North-east. Away."

Igris did not answer. But the knight turned his helm and looked east, past the ridge, past the hills, at the grey line where the light was coming up, and stood a little straighter.

* * *

The brown pin did not move. It did something worse.

At dusk the day before, the slow blind thing by the milestone had come out of its hole in the bank to drink, the way it did every dusk. This morning it did not come out at all. The soldier in its shadow felt it go down instead. Deeper into the bank. Further back than it had ever gone. Until it was curled at the very bottom of its hole in the dark with its blind face pressed into the earth, shaking.

"It's hiding," said Mau.

By then half the house was in the long room. Nobody had called them.

* * *

He sent the soldiers east.

Not the ones riding in other things. The ones in the front row, the first ten, the ones who had gone over the ridge on the first night. He sent them out past the ridge at a run, all ten, along the north road and round the bend east and past the meadow of tracks and the valley and the hills, further than any of them had gone, along the east road toward the grey line where the sun was up now and something was making the animals run.

He stood in the long room with his eyes shut and his hand flat on the table and turned their pages, one at a time, and the house stood round him and did not say a word.

It took an hour.

Then the first soldier came over the last hill on the east road, and stopped, and looked down.

He did not say anything for a long time.

"Mau," said Frieren.

"Smoke," he said.

He kept his eyes shut.

"Fires. A lot of fires. Some of them out, from last night. Some of them still burning." He turned the page. The second soldier, a little to the south. The same hill. "Banners. I don't know them. I don't know any of them." Another page. "Tents coming down. People taking them down. A lot of people." Another. "Things. Not people. Big. On too many legs." He swallowed. "Moving. All of it. Packing up and moving."

"Which way?" said Armin. Very quietly.

Mau turned one more page.

"This way."

* * *

*How many?* he said.

*I'm counting,* said Ciel.

She took a long time. He felt her do it: going through every soldier's page at once, faster than he could, laying them over one another the way she had built the map, counting what they saw. Fires. Tents. Banners. Bodies. The long dark columns already forming on the east road.

*Roughly twenty thousand,* said Ciel at last. *I can't be closer than that. More than eighteen. Fewer than twenty-five.* A pause. *Most of them soldiers. Ordinary. Men and women with spears and shields, from a lot of places. In front of them, a smaller group. Different. I can't see them clearly from this far.* Another pause. *And behind them, things. Demons. Devils, I think. Some of them I don't have a name for.*

*How long?*

*At the pace they're forming up?* A pause. *A day. Maybe less. They'll be at the ridge by tomorrow afternoon.*

He opened his eyes.

The long room was full now. All of it. Everyone in the house, standing round the long table in their nightclothes and their coats, the children at the back with Anko, Ori in front of them with her hands behind her back.

He told them.

Everything. The fires and the banners and the things on too many legs. Ciel's count. The smaller group in front. A day. Maybe less. He told them before he had decided anything, before he had a plan, standing in the middle of them with his hand still flat on the table, because that was what he had promised, and because he did not know what else to do.

When he finished, nobody said anything.

* * *

"The note," said Maki at last. "On the north road. What you told the demon."

"I know."

"They heard it," said Maki. "Somebody heard it."

"I know," said Mau.

He looked at the map on the long-room wall. At the charcoal country Armin had drawn from Ciel's careful sentences, and the three pins, and the east road running off the edge of the fourth board into nothing, where Armin had not had anything to draw.

"I said I'd flatten everything they love," he said. "If anyone hurt her. I meant it." He looked at the wall. "But it was a condition. It was *if*. It wasn't—" He stopped. "I don't know what they heard."

"Twenty thousand people heard *something*," said Gojo, from the window. He had his blindfold up. He had had it up since dawn. "And came."

* * *

It was Rimuru who asked.

He had been on the bench all morning, with Diablo beside him, saying Ciel's words and nothing of his own. He stood up now. He was small, standing; he was always small, standing. He walked down the long room through the house until he was in front of Mau.

"Promise me something," said Rimuru.

Mau looked at him.

"Whatever they've heard," said Rimuru, "whatever they've been told you are, they're coming here because they think you're a monster." He did not look away. "So don't be one. Don't kill anyone. Not one. Not the soldiers, not the ones in front, not the things behind." He paused. "Let them go home and say *we went to fight the monster and he didn't kill any of us*. Let them say it everywhere. That's the only thing that will ever make them stop coming."

The long room was very quiet.

"Rimuru," said Shoko. "Twenty thousand."

"I know how many," said Rimuru. He did not look at her. He looked at Mau. "I'm asking anyway."

Mau stood there for a long time.

He thought about the north road. About the woods lying down in rows, and the three demons in the road where there had been four, and the small grey pile of ash. About the devil coming apart standing. About the boy he had been for one night, level, empty, almost kind.

He thought about twenty thousand people walking toward the house because somebody had told them a story.

"I promise," he said.

* * *

He wrote the note that night.

He did not tell anyone. Not because it was a secret. Because it was for later, for a moment he hoped would never come, and saying it out loud first would have made it real too early.

He went out to the ridge alone after dinner, past Kamish, who lifted his head and watched him go by, and through the stand of thorn and the fold in the hill, into the long low room the soldiers had dug. Dark. Dry. The three lamps on the shelf. The jar of oil. The blankets.

He lit one lamp. He sat down on the packed floor with a sheet of Umaru's paper on his knee and a stub of pencil Anko had given him months ago and he had never given back, and he wrote, in his crooked hand:

*I'm not taking you away.*

*You're on the ridge. Under Kamish. You're safe here. He'll keep you.*

*If I can run, I'll run. I promise. I'm not staying to be brave.*

*The Exchange is cooling. When it's ready, I'll come. Or I'll come walking.*

*Don't come out until I do.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then, at the bottom, smaller:

*I told you first. This is only the part I couldn't say out loud.*

He folded it once and put it on the shelf, on top of the blankets, under the lamp, where the first person to light it would see.

Then he blew the lamp out and went home to bed.

At the reset hour Frieren's foot found his shin, and she said "One," and he said "Still here," and she put her ear on his chest and said "Two," and he lay in the dark with her and listened to her breathe, and did not sleep at all.
