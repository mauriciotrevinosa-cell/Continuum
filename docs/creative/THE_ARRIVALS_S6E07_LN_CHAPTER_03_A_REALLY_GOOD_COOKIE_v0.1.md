# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 32
## Chapter 14 — A Really Good Cookie

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-20; known-authorship correction DH-134, 2026-10-22)
**Volume:** 32
**Reading order:** 14
**Continuity state:** Day 438, afternoon. The army forms up in the frosted fields east of the lime line: rank on rank of ordinary soldiers in front, behind them demons and devils and things with no names, and walking out ahead of all of it, a small group of otherworlders. Among them: a boy in a grey tracksuit with a small pale girl at his sleeve; a huge fair-haired man who grins; a man with a blade where his forearm should be; a woman with a crossbow; a woman who stands on the air. A hooded herald recites Mau's north-road message as the union retold it, without the condition and with the lie. Mau answers: "That's not what I said," and says the condition. The boy shouts for Rem and Ram; Mau tells him they are safe and not kept. The fair-haired man asks if he really thinks he can take all of them. Mau stretches; the wheel in his shadow turns once, waking. To the Noise: "You're going to have a lot of things after this, aren't you?" For the first time the Noise answers in a word, and it sounds pleased: "Maybe." Then another voice, a woman's, that he recognises from repeated encounters: "How interesting." The Goddess. Mau bets her. Twenty thousand people listen to half a conversation. The stake is a cookie ("A really good cookie"); he makes it two. Her terms: "You are not allowed to kill nobody. And you need to help them afterward." "Deal."

*When: Day 438, early afternoon. The sun low already, the way it is after summer. The east road. The dust coming over the last low hill.*

They came over the hill in columns.

He had seen them through the soldiers' eyes. He had stood in the long room with his eyes shut and Ciel counting and said *twenty thousand* out loud to a house in its nightclothes. He had thought he understood.

He had not.

They came over the low hill on the east road four abreast, then eight, then the road could not hold them and they spilled off it into the frosted fields on either side, and kept coming, and kept coming. Spears. Shields. Banners in colours he did not know on poles he could not count. The sound of it reached him before the sight did: not shouting, not drums, just the long low grinding noise of twenty thousand people walking on frozen ground.

They did not stop at the hill. They came down it. They came across the fields toward the lime line, and a quarter of a mile out, at a horn he did not hear, they stopped, and spread, and became a wall.

Rank on rank. As far as he could see to the left. As far as he could see to the right.

And behind them, on the rise, darker. Things that were not soldiers. Tall thin shapes with their heads at wrong angles. Squat ones. Shapes that moved between the others like smoke. Something enormous at the very back with too many legs, waiting.

*Demons,* said Ciel. Very quietly. *Devils. Others. I don't have names for all of them.*

Igris did not move.

Beru, on his right, made a very small sound, deep in his chitin, that might have been a growl.

* * *

Out of the wall, a small group walked forward.

Not many. A dozen, maybe. They came out of the front rank and across the frosted field toward the lime line on foot, without hurrying, and the wall behind them did not follow.

He saw them one at a time.

A huge man. Fair-haired, broad as a door, in a heavy coat that looked like it had been made for someone half his size and given up trying. He was grinning. He looked at Mau the way Milim looked at a mountain.

A thin man with a tired face and a dark coat, and where his right forearm should have been, a long straight blade, growing out of the flesh at the elbow as if it had always been there.

A woman with a crossbow on her back and three other women walking close behind her who moved wrong, too smoothly, like people wearing someone else's bodies.

A woman who was not walking at all. She was standing on the air a little way above the field, a step at a time, as if the air were a staircase only she could see.

A short wide boy with a heavy jaw who cracked his knuckles every few steps.

Others. He could not take them all in.

And at the edge of the group, a little apart from the rest, a boy in grey. Soft grey clothes with a white stripe down each sleeve, like nothing anyone in this world had ever made. Dark hair. A sharp, tired, frightened face. And holding the boy's sleeve, very tight, a small girl in a dress much too big for her, with long pale hair done up in two great coils, looking at Mau with the oldest eyes he had ever seen in a face that young.

*Otherworlders,* said Ciel. *All of them. Like the house. Like you.* A pause. *Every one of them came from somewhere else.*

* * *

The group stopped twenty paces from the lime.

One of them stepped forward. Not one of the dozen. Someone who had walked out behind them: a tall figure in a long grey hooded robe, with no face he could see under the hood. It carried nothing. It stood in the frost in front of the others and lifted its head.

When it spoke, its voice carried. Not loud. It simply carried, across the field and back to the wall, so that every one of twenty thousand people could hear it.

"*You were given words,*" said the herald. "*On the north road. At the end of summer. By the one who stands in front of you.*"

Mau did not move.

"*These are the words,*" said the herald.

And it said them.

"*I won't stop at them. Not at the ones who come. Not at the ones who sent them. I'll find everything they love. Every city. Every house. Every field. Every single thing they love. And I will flatten it. Until there's nothing left standing anywhere that they ever cared about.*"

The field was silent.

"*And he keeps them,*" said the herald. "*The ones who came from other worlds. In his house. Behind his white line. Inside his wall. Nobody has seen one of them outside it.*"

In the little group, the boy in grey went whiter.

* * *

"That's not what I said," said Mau.

He did not raise his voice. He did not need to; the field was so quiet his voice carried as well as the herald's had.

"It's close," he said. "Most of the words are mine. But you cut the beginning off." He looked at the hood, at the dark where a face should be. "I said *if anyone ever again so much as raises an eyebrow at her*. I said *if anyone disrespects her, touches her, says one word about her being weak*." He paused. "There was an *if*. You took it out."

The herald did not answer.

"And I never said anything about keeping anyone," said Mau. "I don't keep anyone. Nobody in my house is kept." He looked past the herald, at the little group of otherworlders standing in the frost. At the boy in grey. "They live there. They can leave. Some of them have."

"Then where are they?"

It was the boy. He had let go of the little girl's hand without seeming to notice and taken a step forward, out of the group, toward the lime.

"Rem," said the boy. His voice cracked on it. "And Ram. Rem and Ram. Where are they? If you don't keep anyone, where *are* they?"

Mau looked at him.

He knew the name. Not the face. The name. *Barusu.* Ram had said it, more than once, the week she arrived. *Barusu came closest.*

"They're safe," said Mau. "They're not here. I sent everyone somewhere safe this morning, because twenty thousand people were walking toward my house." He held the boy's eyes. "Rem made breakfast yesterday. Ram said it was the only thing in the house that wasn't crude. That's where they are."

The boy stared at him.

He did not believe it. Mau could see he did not believe it. He could also see, very faintly, under the not believing, something that wanted to.

The little girl took his sleeve again.

* * *

The huge fair-haired man laughed.

It was a good laugh. A big, easy, delighted laugh, like a man at a fair. He stepped forward past the herald, past the boy, until he was ten paces from the lime, and looked at Mau, and then past Mau at the house, and then back.

"Kid," he said. His accent was strange; heavy and flat, from nowhere Mau knew. "I like you. I'm going to say that first, so you know." He grinned. "But look behind me." He did not turn round. He jerked his thumb over his shoulder at the wall of twenty thousand. "And look at us." The thumb went to the little group. "You really think you can take all of us?"

Mau looked at him.

Then he took his hands out of his coat pockets, and put them over his head, and stretched.

* * *

He had not meant to. His body did it. It had been standing at the lime line in the cold since eleven in the morning, and it was stiff, and it stretched, the way a body stretches in the yard at dawn before the first drill. Arms up. Back long. A crack somewhere in the shoulder Beru had bitten twice.

And under him, in his shadow, the wheel turned.

He felt it. Not a click. Not adapting to anything; nothing had hurt him yet. It turned the way a sleeping dog turns over when it hears the door: once, slowly, all the way round, settling. Waking up.

He thought: *it knows.*

He brought his arms down.

*You're going to have a lot of things after this,* he said. Not to Ciel. Past her. Down, into the dark under the walls, where the bag was, where everything he had ever watched was kept, where something had been quietly building him for a year and a half without ever once saying a word. *Aren't you.*

Silence.

And then, for the first time in his life, the Noise answered him.

*Maybe.*

One word. Not a voice, exactly. A shape that meant a word. But he heard it, clearly, in the dark at the bottom of everything, and it was not cold, and it was not careful.

It sounded pleased.

Mau laughed.

He did not mean to do that either. It came out of him all at once, standing at the lime line in front of twenty thousand people, a short surprised laugh, the way you laugh when someone who never jokes makes a joke. The fair-haired man's grin slipped a fraction.

*Mau,* said Ciel. Very strangely. *Was that—*

*It talked,* said Mau. *It said maybe.*

* * *

"*How interesting.*"

It was not Ciel. It was not the Noise.

It was a woman's voice. Light. Amused. Coming from nowhere and everywhere, the way the herald's had carried, except that nobody in the field heard it but him.

He knew that voice.

It had set the Trial's rules. It had offered Return and Stay. Later, on the bridge, it had given them the coins.

The Goddess.

He stood very still.

He was afraid. He knew he was afraid; he could feel it in both hearts, the warm one going fast and the cold one half a step behind. *What did the Goddess want Mau to learn?* Fern's last page. *Not answered.*

But he was also, somewhere under the fear, already thinking about what came next. About twenty thousand people and the promise he had made Rimuru. About the boy in grey who wanted to believe him.

"Look who's watching," he said aloud.

In the field, the fair-haired man frowned. The herald's hood turned, very slightly.

Mau looked up. At nothing. At the cold clear afternoon sky.

"Do you want to make a bet?" he said.

* * *

Silence.

Twenty thousand people stood in the frosted fields and listened to a boy at a white line talk to the sky.

Then, light, curious:

"*Tell me.*"

"What would you give me," said Mau, "if I beat all of them?" He did not look away from the sky. "The whole army."

Silence again. Longer.

In the little group of otherworlders, the woman on the air had come down a step to hear better. The man with the blade arm had his head on one side. The boy in grey was staring at Mau as if he had gone mad.

"*A cookie,*" said the Goddess.

Mau laughed again. He could not help it.

"That's it?" he said. Out loud. "That's all?"

The army heard him say it. The army heard *that's it, that's all*, to the sky, and a murmur went along the front rank like wind through grass.

"*A really good cookie,*" said the Goddess, sounding faintly offended.

He was still laughing. Twenty thousand spears, and the goddess who had put him through the worst thing that ever happened to him, and she was offering him a cookie, and she was offended he had not been impressed.

"Make it two," said Mau.

* * *

Silence.

Then, slowly, delighted:

"*Two,*" said the Goddess. "*Okay.*" A pause. "*But you are not allowed to kill nobody.*"

He stopped laughing.

"*And you need to help them,*" said the Goddess. "*Afterward.*"

He stood at the lime line and thought about that.

*Not allowed to kill nobody.* He had already promised Rimuru. That was easy. That was already true.

*Help them afterward.*

Twenty thousand people who had walked across the country to kill him, because somebody had told them a story. Help them. After.

He thought about Rimuru in the long room. *Let them go home and say he didn't kill any of us.* He thought: *and say he helped us up.*

"Deal," said Mau.

* * *

The voice did not answer. He felt it go, the way you feel someone leave a room behind you.

He looked down from the sky.

The fair-haired man was looking at him with a very strange expression. Not the grin. Something more careful.

"Who were you talking to, kid?" he said.

"Someone who gave me a coin once," said Mau.

He put his hands back in his coat pockets.

"You asked if I think I can take all of you," he said. He looked past the man at the wall of twenty thousand. At the dark things on the rise behind it. At the boy in grey with the little girl at his sleeve. "I don't know. I've never tried." He paused. "But I'm not going to kill any of you. So you can stop being afraid of that part."

The field was very quiet.

"Igris," said Mau. "Beru."

They stepped forward. One on either side of him.

"Everyone else," said Mau. "Up."

And out of his shadow, across the frosted ground at the lime line, the dark came up.
