# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 32
## Chapter 16 — The Tango

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-20)
**Volume:** 32
**Reading order:** 16
**Continuity state:** Day 438, the field. The otherworlders fight, and it is even. Igris duels the man with blades for arms and head and cuts blades, never the man. The crossbow woman and her three wrong-moving companions run Beru ragged; Corner holds a corner for him. The woman who stands on the air grabs the sky like cloth and flips the field; Kaisel and Bellion's whip pull her down, carefully. The short wide boy fires granite from his head and breaks Tank (in pieces, needs remaking). A swarm of black insects comes at Tusk, and Tusk's fire holds it without killing the girl inside it. A new one, a hybrid who is also a sorcerer, pulls her cord and her head becomes a bell; every ring makes Mau's borrowed patterns stutter for a heartbeat. He learns her: the sound has to arrive, so he lets it never arrive. And he learns by watching: halfway through, the Noise hands him a rough copy of the sky-grab, and he uses it to turn a crossbow bolt round in the air. Nobody dies. He bleeds a little. The fair-haired man watches with his arms folded and stops grinning, then starts again

*When: Day 438. The sun touching the western hills. Twenty thousand ordinary soldiers standing still in the frost, watching a dozen people and a line of shadows.*

It was a dance.

He would think of it like that afterward, when he could think about it at all. Not a fight. Not the hive, not the old dragon's furnace, not the hundred and first floor. Those had been one thing trying to kill him and him trying to stay alive long enough to finish it.

This was a dozen things at once, all different, all moving, and him in the middle of them, not trying to finish anything.

Trying to learn the steps.

* * *

The man with the blades went first, and Igris met him.

It was the most beautiful thing on the field. Mau saw it out of the side of his eye the whole time, the way you see a fire in the corner of a room. The hybrid came in fast and low with three blades, two arms and the one through his skull, and Igris took the first on his sword and the second on his gauntlet and stepped inside the third, and they went round each other in the frost like two men who had both been doing this all their lives and were finally, for once, being given a real answer.

*"You are fast,"* said Igris.

The hybrid did not answer. He could not; he did not have a mouth any more. He came again.

Igris did not cut him. Mau had told him: *nobody*. So the knight cut blades. Once, clean through the blade on the left arm at the elbow, and the steel rang and fell in the frost, and grew back, slower, and the hybrid staggered. Twice. Three times.

*"You grow them back,"* said Igris, approvingly, as if it were a courtesy. *"Good. Then I will not run out."*

* * *

The crossbow woman was worse.

She was not fast. She did not need to be. She stood at the back with the crossbow at her shoulder and fired, and fired, and the bolts did not fly the way bolts fly. They split in the air. One became four. Four became twenty. They came across the field in a fan and every one of them went where she was looking.

She was looking at Beru.

"*My King!*" Beru went sideways across the frost faster than Mau had ever seen him move, the bolts whickering past him into the ground in rows. "*This one is very rude!*"

And her three companions, who moved wrong, were already on him. Too smooth. Too quiet. They did not fight like people. They fought like something wearing people, all angles and no hesitation, and they had blades of their own, and they went for Beru's joints the way the bear had gone for his, the way a pack goes for the legs of something big.

Beru could have eaten them.

Mau saw him want to. Saw the old hunger come up in him, the purple-red under the cold blue. Saw him stop.

"*Not allowed,*" said Beru, through his jaws, to himself. "*Not allowed. My King said. Not allowed.*"

"Corner," said Mau.

Corner went. The long hooked weapon cleared the frozen ground and caught the nearest of the three by the ankle, and pulled her off her feet, and held her there, and Corner planted himself between her and Beru's back, where the attack had been coming from, and held the corner. That was what Corner did. He had held a corner on a roof under a lid of a sky. He held one in the frost.

* * *

The woman on the air grabbed the sky.

He saw her do it. He did not understand it until it happened. She reached up, from her invisible stair, and closed her hand on nothing, on the empty cold blue above the field, and *pulled*, the way you pull a tablecloth.

The sky came with her.

Not the whole sky. A sheet of it, over half the field. It folded down like cloth, and everything under it went sideways. The frost. The line of shields. Lantern, in the air, suddenly flying the wrong way up. Mau's feet went out from under him and for a moment he was falling sideways along the ground, which was a wall now, toward the line of his own soldiers.

*Space,* said Ciel.

He put it under himself. Flat. Slanted. *You don't need a wall, you need an angle.* He hit it and slid and came up on his feet on a floor that was a wall, and the sky snapped back, and he was standing on the field again, the right way up, with his heart going.

She was already reaching for it again.

"*Kaisel,*" said Mau. "*Bellion.*"

The wyvern came down out of the cold air at her like a hawk on a pigeon, not to strike, to *be there*, wings wide, so that she had to look up at him instead of at the sky. And as she looked up, Bellion's whip uncoiled across forty paces of field and wrapped her ankles, gently, the way the Grand Marshal did nothing, and pulled her off her invisible stair.

She fell three paces into the frost and sat there, furious.

"*Stay,*" said Bellion. Formally. Commandingly. He did not let go of the whip.

* * *

The short wide boy broke Tank.

Mau did not see it coming. Nobody did. The boy had been standing at the edge of the group cracking his knuckles, and he planted his feet in the frost, and something came out of the top of his head, a column of grey stone and pressure and noise like a cannon going off in a quarry, and it hit Tank in the chest at the end of the shield line.

The bear came apart.

Not dying. Pieces. The dark shape of him blown into fragments across the frost, and the fragments going down into the ground, into Mau's shadow, all at once.

He felt the hole open.

*He's in pieces,* said Ciel. Very fast. *Not lost. Remaking later. It'll cost.* A pause. *Mau, he can do that again. I watched it build. He's building it again now.*

The boy was grinning. He planted his feet.

"*Lantern,*" said Mau.

The dragon came down on him. Not with fire. Not with claws. With weight. A wing, spread flat, coming down over the boy like a tent, and the boy's second shot went up into the underside of it and hit it and Lantern made a sound and did not move, and held the wing there, and the boy was under it in the dark, pinned, with granite dust coming out from under the edges.

* * *

The insects came at Tusk.

A girl. Small. Mau saw her for one moment at the edge of the group, and then he could not see her at all, because she was inside a cloud of black glittering things that came off her like smoke off a fire, thousands of them, and the cloud went across the field at the shaman.

Tusk lifted his staff.

The blue fire came out of the skull at the top of it. Not a breath. Not a blast. A wall. A curtain of blue flame drawn across the field in front of him, and the black cloud hit it and stopped, and the things at its front edge crackled and fell, and the cloud drew back, and came again, and stopped.

*"Great one,"* said Tusk, without turning his head. *"There is a child in the middle of this."*

"I know," said Mau.

*"I am holding it,"* said Tusk. *"I am not burning in. I could. I am not."*

"Thank you," said Mau.

*"It is very many insects,"* said Tusk, with great dignity.

* * *

Then the bell rang.

He did not see who rang it. He heard it. One clear deep note across the field, like a bell in a tower at dusk.

And the space under his feet went *wrong*.

Not gone. Wrong. For one heartbeat it stuttered, the way a voice stutters, like a word he had said ten thousand times suddenly catching in his throat. He felt the pull go strange in his chest. He felt the Ruler's grip, the sliver of it he still held round nothing, flicker. Everything he had borrowed, everything in the bag, everything the Noise had built in him from watching other people, stumbled at once, for one heartbeat, and came back.

He found her.

A woman at the back of the group, apart from the others, tall, in a long dark coat, with a cord hanging at the side of her chest that she had already pulled. And where her head should have been, a bell. Bronze. Green at the lip. Big enough to stand inside. It swung, slowly, on her shoulders, and as it swung back the other way, it rang again.

Stutter. The space. The pull. Everything.

*She's a sorcerer,* said Ciel. Very fast. *And one of those, the cord ones. Both. The bell isn't just a bell. It's a technique. Anything that hears it, its techniques catch.* A pause. *Yours are borrowed. They catch worse. They catch every time.*

The bell swung.

He felt the next note coming, the way you feel a sneeze coming.

*It has to arrive,* he thought.

* * *

Gojo had said it in the yard a thousand times. *Not a wall. You don't push it back. You let it never arrive.*

He had always thought of it as something you did with a fist. A claw. A spear. A breath of fire.

A sound was a thing that arrived.

He put the space round his own head. Not thick. Not a wall. Thin. A shell a hand's breadth from his ears, the way he had made it on seventy round himself in the dragon's true breath. And he did not make it stop anything. He made it so that anything coming through it had a little further to go. And a little further. And a little further than that.

The bell rang.

He saw it ring. He saw the bronze lip shiver, and the frost on the ground in front of her jump with it.

He did not hear it.

The note came across the field toward him and into the shell round his head and went on going, further and further, always nearly there, never there, the way the claws on twenty had always nearly reached him and never quite.

Nothing stuttered.

He stood at the lime line with blood running out of his nose, because the shell round his head was the hardest thing he had ever held small, and looked at the woman with the bell for a head, and she stood very still, and the bell swung, and he did not hear it, and she knew.

* * *

Then the crossbow woman fired at him.

He saw it. One bolt, out of the back of the group, fast and flat across the field at his chest while he was holding the shell round his head. And as it came, it split. Four. Twenty.

He could not put the space up. The space was round his head.

He did something else.

He did not know he could do it. He reached up, the way the woman on the air had reached up, and closed his hand on the empty cold air above the field, and *pulled*.

Rough. Wrong-handed. A copy of a copy, built in the last ten minutes by something at the bottom of his walls that had been watching her very carefully indeed. But the air came with his hand. A sheet of it. It folded, and the twenty bolts in it folded with it, and when it snapped back they were pointing the other way.

They went back across the field and thudded into the frost in a perfect ring round the crossbow woman's feet.

She lowered the crossbow.

On the ground three paces away, held by Bellion's whip, the woman who had been standing on the air stared at him with her mouth open.

"*That's mine,*" she said. "*That's—how did you—*"

*Maybe,* said the Noise, at the bottom of everything. Very quietly. Very pleased with itself.

* * *

For a moment, the field was still.

Igris and the hybrid with the blades, a pace apart, the hybrid's blades growing back slowly in the frost round their feet. Beru, panting, with the three wrong-moving women pinned under Corner's hook. Lantern with his wing down over the boy and granite dust leaking out from under it. The insect cloud drawn back from Tusk's wall of blue fire, hovering. The woman with the bell for a head, motionless. The crossbow woman with her crossbow lowered and a ring of her own bolts round her feet.

And Mau at the lime line with blood on his face, breathing hard.

It was even.

That was the thing. He had not won anything. He had not finished anything. He was holding twelve things at once, and they were holding him, and none of them could get past the other, and the sun was going down behind the western hills and twenty thousand people were watching.

At the edge of the group, the fair-haired man had stood the whole time with his arms folded and watched.

He was not grinning. He had stopped somewhere around the bell.

He started again now. Slowly. Like a man who has been waiting a long time for something, and has just seen it walk into the room.

He unfolded his arms.

"My turn," he said.
