## Chapter 17 — Borrowed Light

*When: Day 438. Dusk. The sun gone behind the western hills; the sky still light. Twenty thousand soldiers standing still. Twelve otherworlders held, or holding, in the frost.*

The fair-haired man did not have a technique.

That was the first thing Mau understood, and it was the worst thing. Everyone else on the field had had something. A shape. Blades that grew back, a sky that folded, bolts that split, a bell. Something the eyes could look at, that Ciel could count, that the Noise could watch and fold away and hand back to him rough ten minutes later.

The fair-haired man walked across the frost toward him with his split coat hanging off his shoulders, and there was nothing to look at at all.

Just a man. Very big. Moving like someone who had never once in his life met anything he could not walk through.

*Ciel.*

*I see him.* A pause. *Mau, there's nothing. It's not a technique. It's just—him. Strength. And the body round it doesn't break. That's all.* Another pause. *That's all, and it's enormous.*

* * *

The first punch broke the space.

Mau had it up. Thick, the way he had held it for Beru at the start, before the start, on the hive floor. The man did not try to get round it. He did not try anything. He drew back his right fist and hit it.

The space cracked.

Not slowed. Not stopped. *Cracked*, like a plate dropped on a stone floor, and the crack went all the way across it and the fist came through the crack, slowed, much slower, but through, and took Mau in the chest.

He went backward across the frost and hit the ground and rolled and lay there looking at the sky.

The second heart stumbled once and caught up.

"Huh," said the fair-haired man, from somewhere above him. He sounded pleased. "That was something. I felt that. What was it?"

Mau got up.

* * *

He tried everything.

The pull: the man leaned into it and kept walking. The Ruler's grip, the sliver of it, round his jaw, the way it had dropped seven soldiers in the frost: the man's head turned a quarter-inch, as if someone had tapped him, and turned back. The space again, slanted, an angle instead of a wall: the man's fist hit the angle and the angle broke too.

The man was not fast. Mau could see every punch coming. He could have told Ciel where every one would land a full heartbeat before it landed.

It did not matter. Seeing it did not stop it.

The second time he went down, he lay in the frost with blood in his mouth and felt it.

*Click.*

Under him. In his shadow. In the dark.

The wheel. One eighth of a turn. The sound of something enormous shifting one notch.

*It adapted,* said Ciel. Very carefully. *To him. His fists. That's four.* A pause. *Mau. The walls held. Barely. Don't let it turn again if you can help it.*

He got up.

The fair-haired man had stopped. He was standing a few paces off in the frost with his head on one side, looking at Mau. Not at Mau. Above him. At the place over his head where, for one moment, something had turned in the air: eight spokes, a wheel of pale light, gone again as soon as it came.

"Okay," said the man slowly. "Okay. *That's* new."

* * *

*Kamish,* said Mau.

Not out loud. Down. Along the dark, along the line that ran from him to everything that was his, across the frosted fields and the north end and the lime, to the ridge.

*One breath,* he said. *Then back to them. Please.*

On the ridge, the old dragon lifted his head off his paws.

* * *

The field heard him before it saw him.

A sound like a hill moving. Twenty thousand people turned their heads at once toward the north, toward the low ridge at the end of the north field, where a long dark shape that every one of them had taken for part of the ridge stood up.

He rose. The way old things do everything. Slowly. The long body uncoiling off the hill. The wings unfolding, vast, black against the light sky, one, and then the other, until they were wider than the house. The long neck lifting. The head coming up last, and two great cold blue lights opening in it, higher than the trees.

Then he came.

He did not fly fast. He did not need to. He came over the north field and the yard and the lime line in four slow beats of wings that blew the frost off the ground in a white wave, and the wall of twenty thousand bent away from the wind of him like grass, and he passed over Mau's head so low that the shadow of him was the whole field.

He landed between Mau and the fair-haired man.

The ground shook. He folded his wings. He brought his great head down, slowly, slowly, until the two blue lights were level with the fair-haired man's face, a few paces away.

The field was absolutely silent.

* * *

The fair-haired man looked up at him.

Mau saw his face.

He had expected fear. Every face on the field that could see Kamish had fear in it; Mau could feel it, twenty thousand people's worth of it, like heat off a stove. He had expected the fair-haired man to go pale, or step back, or reach for whatever it was that made his fists break space.

He did not.

He smiled.

Not the grin. Not the big, easy, fairground grin he had been wearing all afternoon. Something else. Slow. Real. Almost tired. The smile of a man who has walked into a room in a strange country and found, sitting at a table, someone he once knew.

"Hello again, big guy," he said.

* * *

Kamish looked at him for a long time.

Mau felt it through the line. Not words. Something older. Recognition, coming up slowly through the dark of the old dragon like a stone surfacing from the bottom of a lake.

*"You,"* said Kamish.

His voice was slow and deep, like stone moving under water. Every soldier on the field heard it.

*"You stood on the ash."*

"I did," said the fair-haired man.

*"At the end of it,"* said Kamish. *"On the ash of the last city. Small. Five of you. With borrowed light."* The cold blue eyes did not move. *"You were one of the five."*

"Fourth one in," said the man. "If you're counting." He looked up at the vast dark shape, at the wings, at the cold blue where there had once been gold. "You look different."

*"I was killed,"* said Kamish. *"By you."* A pause. *"And then raised. By him."*

The fair-haired man's eyes went, slowly, from the dragon to the boy standing behind it in the frost with blood on his face and his hands at his sides.

"Huh," he said, very softly.

* * *

He laughed.

Not loud. A short, quiet, disbelieving laugh, the kind that comes out of someone who has just understood something very large all at once.

"Kid," he said. Not to Kamish. To Mau, past the dragon's great head. "You've got Kamish."

"He keeps watch over my house," said Mau.

The fair-haired man looked at him for a long moment.

"Yeah," he said. "I bet he does."

Then he did something nobody on the field expected. He sat down. Right there in the frost, in front of the dragon, with his split coat round him and his huge hands on his knees, the way the seventh soldier had sat down an hour ago with his spear beside him.

"I'm done," he said.

The herald turned its hooded head, sharply, toward him.

"*You were paid—*"

"I was told," said the fair-haired man, without looking at it, "that there was a monster out here keeping people in a box. I came to see." He looked up at Kamish. "I already killed that one once. Didn't take." He looked past Kamish at Mau. "And the kid hasn't killed anybody. I've been counting. Not one. Not the soldiers, not the ones in front. He's had about fifty chances." He shrugged. "That's not a monster. That's somebody having a really bad day."

* * *

*"Small king,"* said Kamish. Quietly. In Mau's ribs. *"May I go back now? I have a house to keep."*

"Go back," said Mau. "Thank you."

The old dragon lifted his head.

He looked once more at the fair-haired man sitting in the frost. Something passed between them that Mau could not read. Then Kamish unfolded his wings, and the frost went up off the field in a white wave again, and he rose, slowly, the way old things do everything, and went back over the lime and the yard and the north field, and lay down on the ridge with his head on his paws, as if he had never moved.

Under him, sixty people in a long low room had felt the hill move twice, and did not know why.

* * *

The fair-haired man was still sitting in the frost.

So was the seventh soldier, ten paces off, with his spear beside him.

So was the woman who had stood on the air, held by Bellion's whip. So was the crossbow woman, with her bolts in a ring round her feet. The hybrid with the blades stood a pace from Igris with his blades growing back and did not come again. The boy with the granite was still under Lantern's wing. The insect cloud hung behind Tusk's wall and did not move. The woman with the bell for a head stood very still.

The boy in grey stood behind all of them with the small girl at his sleeve, staring at Mau with an expression Mau could not read at all.

For a moment it was over.

He could feel it. The field, holding its breath. Twenty thousand people standing still in the dusk, and the ones who had been brought to do what the soldiers could not, held, or sitting down, or not coming again.

*It's even,* said Ciel, very quietly. *It's more than even. Mau, I think—*

Then, behind the wall, on the rise, something screamed.

* * *

He looked up.

The things on the rise had not moved all afternoon. The tall thin shapes with their heads at wrong angles. The squat ones. The ones like smoke. The enormous thing at the very back with too many legs. They had stood behind the wall of soldiers, waiting, the way dogs wait behind a fence.

Nobody had let them through.

They were coming through anyway.

He saw the front rank of the wall begin to break, not forward, not at him, *apart*, men and women turning round with their spears and seeing what was coming down the rise behind them and scattering out of its way. Something tall and pale and wrong pushed through the gap. It had a face. It had several. They were all smiling.

*Devil,* said Ciel. Very fast. *I know that one. Mau. That's the faces one. We've seen it before.*

The herald had not moved. It stood in the frost in its grey hood, and did not give any order, and did not try to stop them.

"*The soldiers were never going to be enough,*" it said again. Quietly. To no one. "*Nor were you.*"

On the rise, the enormous thing with too many legs began, slowly, to walk.
