## Chapter 18 — The Things Behind

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-20)
**Volume:** 32
**Reading order:** 18
**Continuity state:** Day 438, dusk into dark. *When: Day 438. The last of the light. The wall of twenty thousand breaking apart from the back.*

The soldiers had done what he asked.

That was the problem. They had stood still. All afternoon, twenty thousand of them, spears grounded, in their ranks, because a boy at a white line had said *please* and seven men had dropped in the frost without him moving.

They were still standing in their ranks when the things came down the rise behind them.

* * *

He saw it from the lime line.

The back of the wall breaking. Not forward. Apart. Men and women turning round with their spears and seeing what was coming and trying to get out of its way, and running into the ranks in front of them, who had not seen yet, who were still facing him, still standing still, still doing what he had asked.

Something tall and pale went through them like a scythe through wheat. Not cutting. Pushing. Bodies going down in the frost on either side of it, trampled, and the thing not looking down at them, not looking at anything, all its faces smiling the same smile, coming.

Behind it, the demons. The tall thin ones with their heads at wrong angles, loping. Behind them the squat ones and the smoke ones and the others he had no names for. And behind all of it, on the rise, the enormous thing with too many legs, walking.

They were not coming for the army.

They were coming for him. And the army was in the way.

* * *

"*Turn round,*" said Mau.

The Legion turned round.

Ninety-eight soldiers in a line a hundred paces long between the lime and the field, shields locked, spears level, facing him all afternoon, facing the wall. At his word they faced the other way. And then, at his next word, they ran.

Not at the things. Into the wall. Through the gaps in the breaking ranks, ninety-eight dark faceless soldiers going into twenty thousand frightened ones, and where they went, they stopped. And turned. And locked shields again. Not one line. A dozen. Small walls of black shields, spread through the army, between the ordinary soldiers and what was coming down the rise.

The men and women who had come across the country to kill him found themselves standing behind his soldiers' shields.

He saw a young man in a too-big helmet look at the dark shield in front of his face, and at the faceless soldier holding it, and then back over his shoulder, across the field, at Mau.

* * *

The demons reached the first shields, and Mau said it.

Not loud. He did not need loud. He had said it on the north road, at nightfall, to three of them, in a voice level and empty and almost kind, and one of them had run home with a warm young voice full of words. He said it now in his own voice, tired, with blood on his face.

"Nobody move."

The demons stopped.

Not all at once. The front ones first. The tall thin loping ones, at the edge of the first shields, and the instant his voice reached them, they stopped, in mid-stride, the way a dog stops at a word it was beaten for. Then the ones behind them. A ripple going back up the rise.

They knew.

He saw it in their faces. They had heard the story. Not the herald's story, not the one with the *if* cut out. The other one. The true one. From one of their own, in a warm shaking voice, word for word, at the edge of a camp in the dark: *he stood in the road and the woods lay down and four of us became three, and he crouched in front of me and told me to say it back.*

They did not move.

*The devils won't,* said Ciel. Very fast. *Mau, they don't learn like that. They're not afraid of you. They're made of being afraid.*

* * *

The faces devil did not stop.

It came through the stopped demons as if they were trees, toward the nearest wall of black shields, where forty ordinary soldiers stood behind four of Mau's, and it was smiling with every face it had, and the soldiers behind the shields were screaming, and as they screamed it grew.

*Fear,* said Ciel. *It eats fear. Theirs. They're terrified, and it's getting bigger.*

He could not take their fear away.

He could stop it arriving.

He did what he had done with the bell. Not a wall. A shell. Round the devil itself this time, all of it, every face, a hand's breadth from its pale skin: a space in which everything it reached for was a little further away. And a little further. And a little further than that.

It reached for the shields.

It did not arrive.

It pushed. It leaned its whole tall pale weight on the shell and pushed toward the forty screaming soldiers and the shields, and it did not get any closer, and it did not get any closer, and every face on it went on smiling and the smiles began, slowly, to look less sure.

His nose was bleeding again. Both nostrils now.

*You can't hold that and anything else,* said Ciel.

*I know.*

* * *

He did not have to hold anything else. Not that part.

At the next wall of shields along, where the second devil was coming, something pale and bright went up over the ordinary soldiers like a dome of frost.

He did not do it.

He looked. The little girl. The small pale girl in the dress much too big for her, with her hair in two great coils, standing at the back of the group of otherworlders with one hand up and her face perfectly calm, and over thirty soldiers she had never met, a shield of pale light, and the second devil hitting it, and the shield holding.

She had not been asked. Nobody had told her anything.

Beside her, the boy in grey was not looking at the devil.

He was looking at Mau.

At the boy at the white line with blood running out of both nostrils, holding a devil in a shell so it could not reach forty soldiers who had come to kill him, with ninety-eight shadows spread through an enemy army holding shields over the enemy. The boy in grey's face had gone through something. Mau could not tell what. It had been one thing all afternoon and now it was something else.

"*Betty,*" said the boy. Very quietly. "*Betty, he's—*"

"*I know, in fact,*" said the little girl, without lowering her hand. "*I suppose I can see it too.*"

* * *

The enormous thing reached the field.

It came down off the rise at the back of everything on its too many legs, slowly, the way the old dragon did everything, and it was bigger than Kamish. Not taller. Wider. A great low body like a hill on legs, and the legs going on and on, and the ground shaking under each one of them. Soldiers ran from it. Demons ran from it. The stopped ones broke and scattered out of its way.

It was coming straight at the lime line. At him. At the house.

"*Lantern,*" said Mau. "*Bellion.*"

The dragon came down out of the darkening air onto the thing's back. Not with fire. With weight. The whole of him, wings spread, claws in, pressing. The thing staggered. Did not stop.

Bellion's whip went out across the field and round three of its legs at once and pulled. The Grand Marshal leaned back against it like a man holding a horse. The thing staggered again. Did not stop.

He reached for the hand that was not there.

Not a sliver this time. He could not hold a sliver; he was holding the faces devil in a shell with everything he had, and his head was splitting, and the second heart had started to stumble. He reached anyway. Further into it than he had ever gone. Into the rough unfinished piece of the Ruler's will that the Noise had folded away on the hundredth floor, and took as much of it as he could hold without letting go of the shell, and closed it round the thing's legs.

It stopped.

Twenty paces from the lime. Held. Lantern on its back, Bellion's whip round its legs, and a grip that was not there round all the rest of them, and the great low body heaving, straining, and not coming any closer.

And under him, in his shadow, the wheel began to turn.

He felt it start. Not a click. A long slow grinding, like something enormous trying to shift its weight.

*No,* said Ciel. *Mau, no, not again, the walls—*

* * *

He did not let it turn.

He did the other thing instead. The one he had learned on forty, in the dark, with an ant king coming at him on three limbs, by seeing how it was built.

Blue in the left hand. The pull. His own marble, the size of a thumbnail.

Red in the right. The push.

He did not aim at the thing. He did not aim at anything on the field. He lifted his hands, still holding the shell round the devil and the grip round the legs with whatever was left, and he brought blue and red together, and he aimed straight up.

Into the sky above it.

* * *

It made no sound. It never did.

A line of something that was not light and not dark went up out of his hands, past the great heaving body, past Lantern on its back, up into the dusk. Into the low grey clouds that had come over at sunset. And through them.

Where it went, there was nothing.

A clean round tunnel through the clouds, as wide as a house, going up and up, and through it, suddenly, for everyone on the field to see, the first stars.

Twenty thousand people looked up at a hole in the sky.

So did the devils.

* * *

They were made of fear.

That was what Ciel had said. They ate it and grew on it and were made of it. And every soldier on the field, every demon, every otherworlder, every frightened thing for a mile in every direction, looked up at the round black hole in the clouds with the stars showing through it, and was afraid.

Not of the devils.

Of him.

He felt it change. The fear on the field turning, all at once, like a flock of birds turning in the air. Away from the pale smiling thing pushing at a shell it could not get through. Away from the enormous thing straining at his grip. Toward the boy at the white line with his hands still raised and blood all down his face, who had just put a hole in the sky.

The devils had nothing left to eat.

The faces devil stopped pushing. All its smiles went out at once, like lamps. It drew back out of the shell. And then it turned and went, back through the stopped demons and up the rise and over it, faster than it had come.

The enormous thing tore its legs out of his grip, and Bellion let the whip go because Mau told him to, and Lantern lifted off its back, and it turned, slowly, the whole great low hill of it, and went back up the rise the way it had come, the ground shaking under each leg, and over, and was gone.

The demons went after them. Every one. Without a sound.

* * *

He lowered his hands.

The field was very quiet.

Twenty thousand ordinary soldiers, standing among ninety-eight dark shields, looking at him. A dozen otherworlders, sitting in the frost or standing very still. The boy in grey and the small girl with her hand still up and a pale dome of light still over thirty strangers. The herald in its grey hood, alone in the middle of the field, not moving.

Nobody dead. Not one. He had counted, with Ciel, the whole time. Trampled, some. Bruised. Six with sore jaws. A boy under a dragon's wing with granite dust in his hair. Nobody dead.

Somewhere at the back of the wall, a horn blew. Long. Low. And the wall of twenty thousand began, slowly, to turn round.

*It's over,* said Ciel. Her voice was not level at all. *Mau. It's over. They're going.*

He stood at the lime line and swayed.

Then he felt something in his coat pocket.

Warm. Two of them. Round, and flat, and warm through the cloth against his hip, as if they had just come out of an oven.

He did not take them out. He put his hand over the pocket, and stood there in the dark, and found that he was laughing again. Very quietly. Through the blood.

"*Well done,*" said a light, amused voice that nobody else on the field could hear. "*Now help them.*"
