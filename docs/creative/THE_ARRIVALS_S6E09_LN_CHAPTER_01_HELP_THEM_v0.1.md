## Chapter 19 — Help Them

*When: Day 438, full dark. The hole in the clouds closing slowly. Stars through it. Twenty thousand people turning round in the frost.*

*Help them,* she had said.

He stood at the lime line with his hand over his pocket and the two warm cookies under it, and looked at the field.

At twenty thousand people turning round in the dark. At the trampled ones, lying in the frost where the devils had gone through them, some moving, some not yet. At the ones with sore jaws. At the boy under Lantern's wing.

At the herald, in the middle of it all, in its grey hood, standing very still.

"Back," he said.

* * *

They went.

Igris. Beru. Tusk last, the blue fire in his skull going down. Kaisel out of the air. Bellion, coiling the whip. Corner, letting go of the three wrong-moving women, who scrambled away across the frost. Roof. Lantern, lifting his wing off the boy, who lay there in the granite dust and stared up at the stars and did not move.

He kept the soldiers.

And he put the wheel away. He felt it, in the dark under him, still half-turning, still grinding, wanting to learn something. He told it no. Not with words. The way you put a hand on a dog's back. It settled.

He let go of the shell. There was nothing in it any more.

He let go of the hand that was not there.

He stood at the lime line with nothing up, nothing held, nothing ready, and walked out into the field.

* * *

*Mau,* said Ciel. *You've got nothing. No space. Nothing.*

*I know.*

*If something—*

*I promised,* said Mau. *Help them. You can't help someone with your hands full.*

Ciel did not answer.

* * *

He did not know how to help twenty thousand people.

He helped one. Then the next.

The first was a woman with her shoulder out of its socket, sitting in the frost holding her arm against her chest, white with the pain of it. He knelt down beside her. She flinched away from him so hard she nearly fell over.

"I won't hurt you," he said. "I promise. I know how to do this. Somebody taught me."

Shoko had. In the clinic, on a winter afternoon a long time ago, with Stark as the patient, because Stark had put his shoulder out on the woodpile. *Brace here. Turn it out. Slow. Then up. You'll feel it go.*

He braced. Turned it out. Slow. Then up.

He felt it go.

The woman gasped. Then she sat there in the frost, moving her arm, staring at him.

"Keep it still," said Mau. "A sling, if you've got one. Don't lift anything for a week." He stood up. "Sorry."

He did not know why he said sorry. It came out.

* * *

He sent the soldiers to carry the ones who could not walk.

Ninety-eight faceless shadows going through the dark field picking up trampled men and women as carefully as Taihei picked up eggs, and carrying them to the fire.

There was a fire. He had not lit it. Tusk had, before he went down: a ring of blue flame in the frost at the edge of the field, not burning anything, just there, warm, like a hearth with no wood in it. The soldiers laid the hurt ones round it. Some of them screamed when a shadow picked them up. Some of them stopped screaming when they felt the fire.

Someone was carrying a man beside one of the soldiers. Mau looked.

The fair-haired man. With a soldier with a broken leg over his shoulder, as easily as a sack of flour, walking alongside one of Mau's shadows doing the same.

"Kid," said the fair-haired man, as he went past. "You know you're nuts, right?"

"I made a bet," said Mau.

"With who?"

"Someone who likes cookies."

The fair-haired man looked at him for a long moment. Then he laughed, and went on toward the fire with the soldier over his shoulder.

* * *

The others watched.

The otherworlders. Some of them had gone already, slipping back into the turning army in the dark. Some had not. The man with the blades had pulled his cord again and was a man again, tired, with a dark coat and no blades at all, sitting on an upturned shield watching Mau set a second shoulder. The woman who had stood on the air was standing on the ground now, which seemed to offend her. The crossbow woman had gone, with her three companions.

The boy who had fired granite out of his head was sitting in the frost where Lantern had left him, and a single soldier of Mau's stood beside him, not guarding him, just standing there, the way the soldiers stood.

And the boy in grey. With the little girl.

They had not left. They stood at the edge of the firelight, watching him go from one hurt person to the next with nothing up, nothing held, kneeling in the frost. The boy's face had stopped doing what it had been doing all afternoon. It was just tired now. And very young.

"He's helping them," said the boy. To nobody. "He's actually—"

"*In fact,*" said the little girl, very quietly, "*I suppose he is.*"

* * *

The young soldier in the too-big helmet was near the back of the field.

Mau had seen him at the start. In the front rank. Shaking, with his spear, when Mau said *please*. He had stood still all afternoon. He had stood still when the devils came, until a banner pole went over in the stampede and came down across his leg and pinned him to the frost.

He was lying there now, half under it, with his helmet over one eye, trying not to cry.

Mau knelt down beside him.

"I'm going to lift it," he said. "It'll hurt for a moment. Then it'll stop."

The boy looked at him. Very young. Younger than Yuka. His eyes were brown and frightened and wet.

"Okay," he whispered.

Mau got his hands under the pole.

It was heavy. Iron-shod, the old kind, the banner frozen stiff on it. He had no space, no pull, no hand that was not there. He had let go of all of it. He lifted it with his arms, the way anybody lifts anything, and the boy gasped as the weight came off his leg, and Mau set the pole down in the frost beside him, carefully.

"There," he said. "See? It's—"

He looked at the boy's face.

The boy's eyes were not brown.

* * *

They had been brown a moment ago. Wet and frightened and brown and very young.

They were something else now. Not a colour. A depth. As if somebody had opened a door behind the boy's face, very quietly, and was standing in the doorway, looking out.

Looking at him.

*Mau,* said Ciel. Suddenly. Sharply. *Mau, something's—*

A hand came up.

The boy's hand. But not the boy moving it. He could see that. The boy's face did not change at all; it was still frightened, still wet, still very young, and his hand came up off the frost on its own as if somebody else was wearing it, and two fingers touched Mau's forehead.

Gently. Like a parent checking for fever.

*MAU—*

Everything went white.

* * *

Not pain. There was no pain at all.

White. Like snow. Like the inside of a cloud. Like the white place at the end of the Trial, with no edges, where a light voice had spoken to him.

He felt something go.

Not out of him. *From* him. The way you feel a drawer opened in a room behind you. Something going through his things. Quickly. Carefully. Knowing exactly what it was looking for. Picking things up and putting them down and picking up others and taking them, and putting other things in their places, small things, wrong things, so neatly he would never know there had been anything there before.

He tried to hold on.

He did not know what he was holding on to.

Somewhere very far away, a voice. Light, amused, the one that had offered him a cookie. Not amused now.

"*He cheated,*" said the Goddess.

Sharp. Furious. The first time he had ever heard it anything but light.

Then nothing.

* * *

The young soldier in the too-big helmet sat up in the frost.

His eyes were brown. Wet. Frightened. He did not know why he was sitting up. He did not know why the boy who had lifted the banner pole off his leg was lying face down in the frost beside him, not moving.

He did not remember anything since the pole came down.

He looked round. At the field. At the fire of blue flame with the hurt people round it. At the soldiers of shadow standing still where they had been, suddenly, all at once, the way puppets stand still when the hand goes out of them.

He got up. His leg hurt. He could walk on it.

He ran.

* * *

They all did.

It went across the field like wind across grass. Someone saw the boy at the white line go down, face first in the frost, and not get up, and shouted, and then everyone saw. The shadows not moving. The blue fire flickering lower. The monster lying in the field with nothing up.

And the horn at the back of the army blew again, long and low, and twenty thousand people who had been turning round slowly began to run.

The otherworlders went with them. The man who had been a hybrid, off his upturned shield and into the dark. The woman who had stood on the air, up her invisible stair and gone. The boy with the granite. The herald, in its grey hood, walking, not running, east, without looking back.

The fair-haired man was the last.

He stood over Mau for a long moment in the light of the dying fire. He looked down at him. Then at the dark field, emptying. Then east.

"Kid," he said. Very quietly. "What did they do to you?"

He did not wait for an answer. Nobody was there to give one. He went, too.

* * *

The boy in grey did not go at once.

He stood at the edge of the firelight with the small girl at his sleeve and looked at the boy lying face down in the frost.

"Subaru," said the little girl. "*We should go, I suppose.*"

He did not move.

"*Subaru.*"

He went.

But he looked back. Three times. Across the frost, at the white line and the blue fire and the boy who had shielded the army that came to kill him, lying in the dark with nobody beside him.

* * *

The field was empty.

The fire of blue flame went lower, and lower, and went out.

The soldiers of shadow stood where they had been when the hand went out of them, all over the dark field, spears upright, not moving, waiting for a voice that did not come.

Mau lay in the frost by the lime line with his face in the cold, breathing.

In his coat pocket, against his hip, two cookies. Still warm.

* * *

Under the ridge, in the long low room, in the light of one lamp, sixty people sat against the timber walls and did not sleep.

Above them, through the hill, the slow vast breathing they had listened to all day stopped.

Then the whole hill moved, as the old dragon lifted his head.

*"The small king has fallen,"* said Kamish.

His voice came down through the earth like stone moving under water, and every person in the room heard it.

*"He is breathing. He is alone. Something was in his head that was not his."* A pause. *"I cannot leave a house I was asked to keep."*

Frieren was on her feet.

The note was still in her hand. *Don't come out until I do.*

She looked at it once.

Then she put it in her pocket, and went to the fold in the hill behind the thorn, and pushed it open, and went out into the dark.

Every person in the room went after her.
