*When: Day 438, late night. The hole in the clouds has closed. Ninety-eight soldiers of shadow stand motionless all over the dark field where the hand went out of them. The blue fire is out.*

He woke up cold.

That was the first thing. Cold all down the front of him, where the frost was, and the taste of earth and blood in his mouth. He lay there for a moment with his face in the ground, not knowing why.

Then he got up.

Nobody helped him. There was nobody there. He got his hands under himself and pushed, and sat up, and then stood, swaying a little, in the dark field by the white line, with his head full of a soft high ringing like the inside of a bell after it has stopped.

He looked round.

Soldiers. His soldiers. All over the field, in the starlight, standing very still, spears upright, as if they were waiting for something. He did not remember telling them to stand there.

He did not remember a lot of things, just then. It was like waking from a nap in the afternoon, when you do not know for a moment what day it is.

He put his hand in his coat pocket.

* * *

Two cookies.

He took them out. Round. Flat. Golden at the edges. Still very slightly warm, which was impossible, out here, in the frost, hours after—

After.

It came back all at once. The field. Twenty thousand people. The fair-haired man asking if he could take all of them. A light amused voice out of nowhere. *A cookie. A really good cookie.* Make it two. *You are not allowed to kill nobody, and you need to help them afterward.*

He laughed.

Out loud, alone in the dark field. He held up the two cookies in the starlight and laughed, because he had done it. He had done it. Twenty thousand, and nobody dead, and a goddess had paid up.

He remembered that perfectly.

* * *

Someone was running toward him across the field.

He turned.

A small figure. Coming fast, not running, *floating*, low over the frost, faster than anyone ran, with long pale hair loose behind her and a staff in one hand. An elf. He knew her. The elf from the east room. The one who read all the time.

She came down out of the air ten paces from him and did not stop. She came at him across the frost with her arms already reaching, and her face—

He did not know what her face was doing. He had never seen it do that.

She reached for him.

He stepped away.

* * *

He did not think about it. His body did it. One moment she was there, her arms coming round him, and the next he was not; he was four paces to the left, standing in the frost, and her arms closed on nothing.

It was easy. It was the easiest thing in the world. A little step sideways through nothing, the way Yuka did it, the way Yuka had always done it, the thing Umaru's registry called her *teleport division*. He had watched her do it a thousand times. He could do it in his sleep.

He did not know why he had never been able to do it before.

He did not know why the elf was looking at him like that.

"Mau," she said.

She came again. Slower. Her hands out. He stepped away again. Not unkindly. The way you step away from someone at a market who has mistaken you for somebody else. Four paces. Then four more, when she followed.

She stopped.

She stood in the frost with her hands still half out, and did not follow again.

"Sorry," said Mau. He meant it. She looked so stricken. "Do I know you?"

* * *

Somewhere very far away, behind something, like a voice through a closed door in another part of a house, someone was saying his name.

*Mau. Mau. Mau. That's Frieren. Mau, that's—*

He did not hear it. Or he heard it the way you hear a sound in a dream and fold it into the dream without waking. A voice behind a wall. He frowned, a little, and then forgot it.

The elf did not answer him.

She was standing very still. Her face had gone perfectly blank, the way a lake goes blank when the wind stops. She was looking at him the way he had once seen someone look at a room after a fire.

"I know you," he said, to be kind. "You're the one in the east room. You read a lot." He smiled at her. "Sorry. I didn't mean to jump. You came at me fast."

She did not say anything at all.

* * *

Then he saw Yuka.

She was coming across the field behind the elf, with the others: a lot of people, the people from the house, coming out of the dark from the direction of the ridge, running, some of them, and some of them walking slowly as if they were afraid of what they were walking toward. And there in the middle of them, between an old man and an old woman, with her coat half off her shoulders and her face white—

Yuka.

His whole chest went warm.

He did not think about it. He stepped. One little step sideways through nothing, across forty paces of frosted field, and he was in front of her, and she stopped so hard her grandmother walked into her.

"Yuka," he said. "Did you see?"

She stared at him.

"Did you see me fight?" he said. He could not stop smiling. "All of them. Twenty thousand. And nobody died. Not one. I promised." He held out his hand. "And I won *two cookies*."

He put one in her hand.

"One's for you," said Mau. "Because you love sweets."

* * *

Yuka looked down at the cookie in her hand.

Golden at the edges. Still very slightly warm.

She looked up at him.

He was so happy. She had never seen him look like that. Not on the roof, not in the yard, not at the long table on the best of nights. He was standing in the frost in the middle of a dark field with blood dried down his face from both nostrils and his coat torn and earth on his chin, beaming at her as if she were the only thing on the field worth looking at.

"Oppa," she said. Her voice did not work properly. "Oppa, what—"

"I know," he said. "Isn't it stupid? A cookie." He laughed. "She was so offended when I said that's all."

Behind him, ten paces away in the frost, the elf from the east room had not moved.

Yuka looked past him. At Frieren.

At Frieren's face.

* * *

"Mau?"

A small voice. At his elbow.

He looked down. A girl. Small, in a coat too big for her, with her hands behind her back and a folded paper sticking out of her pocket, looking up at him with very serious eyes.

He knew her too. Of course he knew her. She lived in the house. She was always in the fort, with the others. She had a list. She was always with Yuka, or near Yuka, or near—

"Hey," said Mau warmly. He crouched down so his face was level with hers. "You're Yuka's friend, right? From the fort?"

The girl did not answer.

She stood there with her hands behind her back and looked at his face for a long time. Very carefully. The way she looked at the map on the long-room wall every morning, checking that the pins were where they had been.

They were not where they had been.

"Yes," said Ori, at last. Very quietly. "From the fort."

He smiled at her, and ruffled her hair, and stood up.

She did not move when he did it. She stood very still with her hands behind her back, and when he turned away she took the folded paper out of her pocket and looked at it, the list, his list, the one with *Lantern* and *Roof* and *the ones that ride* on it, and she put it back, and did not cry, because there was nobody to count for.

* * *

The house stood round him in the frost.

All of them. Sixty people, and more, in the dark field where twenty thousand had been, looking at him. Shoko with her bag. Gojo with his blindfold up and his bare eyes on the middle of Mau's head, very still, his face doing the thing it had done once before, in the clinic, looking at a root he could not see past. Rimuru, grey again, with Diablo's hand on his shoulder, saying nothing, his eyes shut, listening to something down the thread that was not answering. Fern. Maki. Yuta. Taihei with a lamp.

Mau looked round at them all.

He knew them. Every one. Their faces, their names, where their rooms were, what they ate. He had lived with them. He was fond of them. They were kind people, the people of this house, and they had let him stay, and he was grateful.

"It's all right," he said. "Everyone's all right. Nobody died. They've all gone home." He smiled round at them. "Sorry if I scared you."

Nobody said anything.

He did not understand why nobody said anything.

* * *

Yuka was holding the cookie.

She had not eaten it. She was holding it in both hands, the way Ichika held her cup, and she was looking at Frieren.

Frieren was looking at nothing.

She had not moved from where she had stopped. Ten paces off, in the frost, with her staff in one hand and the other still half raised, the way it had been when her arms closed on nothing. The note was in her pocket. *I told you first.* Her face was perfectly calm.

Fern went to her. Put her arm round her.

Frieren did not seem to notice.

"He doesn't know me," she said. Very quietly. To nobody. In a voice so level it did not sound like anyone's. "Fern. He looked at me and he didn't know me."

* * *

Over on the ridge, at the end of the dark north field, an old dragon lay with his head on his paws and two cold blue eyes open, watching.

*"Small king,"* said Kamish. Very quietly. Down the line. In Mau's ribs.

Mau heard that.

He turned his head toward the ridge and smiled.

"Yes?"

The old dragon was silent for a long time.

*"Nothing,"* said Kamish at last. *"Come home. It is cold."*

"Coming," said Mau.

He put his hand in his pocket and found the other cookie there, still warm, and was glad, because Yuka had its twin. Then he turned toward the house, and the lamps Taihei had lit in the long-room windows, and started walking.

The house walked with him.

None of them said anything.

Behind them, all over the dark field, ninety-eight soldiers stood motionless in the frost, waiting for an order that did not come, until at last, one by one, without being told, they went down into the ground, and were gone.

*End of Volume 32.*
