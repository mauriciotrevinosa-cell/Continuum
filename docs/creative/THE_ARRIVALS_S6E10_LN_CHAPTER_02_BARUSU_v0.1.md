*When: Day 440, first light. Frost. Anko on the red stake at the river bend, with her watch, the way she used to sit before the night shift, because the night shift has not been called since the field.*

Anko saw them first. She always did.

Two figures on the east road, coming over the low hill where twenty thousand had come two days before. Small. Slow. One tall, one very short, the short one holding on to the tall one's arm.

She did not ring anything. She took the pencil from behind her ear and wrote on the back of her watch-sheet, *Day 440. First light. Two. East road. Walking.*

Then she climbed down off the stake and went to find somebody.

* * *

She found Mau.

He was already up. He was at the pump in the yard, washing his face in water so cold it hurt, humming. He looked up when she came round the corner of the barn and smiled at her the way he smiled at everyone now, warm and friendly and a little polite.

"Morning, Anko," he said.

She looked at him for a moment.

"Two people," she said. "East road. Walking."

He was at the lime before she finished the sentence.

* * *

It was the boy in grey.

He came up the road to the white line slowly, like someone who had walked all night and was not sure, every step of it, that he should be walking. His grey clothes were filthy. His face was grey with tiredness. The small girl was on his arm, in her dress much too big for her, with the hem dragging through the frost and her two great coils of pale hair coming down at the sides, looking at everything with her very old eyes.

They stopped at the lime.

Mau was standing on the other side of it with his hands in his coat pockets.

"Hey," he said. "You came back. I'm glad. Are you hurt?"

The boy stared at him.

"*Am I*—" He stopped. "You were *lying in the field*. Face down. I saw you. You weren't moving." He took a breath. "And you're asking if *I'm* hurt?"

"I'm fine," said Mau. "I just fell over. I was really tired." He smiled. "You're the one who walked all night."

The boy looked at him for a long time with an expression Mau could not read.

Then he looked past him. At the yard. At the house beyond it, grey in the first light, with its long-room windows lit and smoke coming out of the kitchen chimney.

"Rem," he said. "And Ram. You said they were here. You said they were safe." His voice cracked. "I want to see them."

* * *

She came out of the kitchen door with washing over her arm.

Rem. In her plain dress, with her sleeves rolled up and a basket of wet shirts on her hip, the way she came out every morning to hang the washing before the frost was off the line. She was not looking at the lime. She was looking at the line, and the pegs, and the sky to see if it would rain.

Then she looked up.

She stopped.

The basket did not fall. She held on to it. But she stopped in the middle of the yard with the basket on her hip and stood there in the frost and looked at the boy at the white line, and her face did something nobody in the house had ever seen it do.

"*Subaru-kun*," she said.

Very quietly.

The boy made a sound like something breaking.

* * *

He crossed the lime.

Nobody stopped him. Mau stepped aside. The boy went past him across the frosted yard at a stumbling run, and the small girl let go of his arm and stayed where she was at the white line, and the boy reached Rem and stopped a pace away from her, as if he did not dare go closer, as if she might not be real.

"Rem," he said. "Rem. *Rem*."

"I'm here," said Rem.

"You were *gone*." He was crying now. He did not seem to know it. "You were peeling potatoes. You were peeling potatoes and then you were *gone*, and the knife was on the floor, and I looked, I looked for *six days*, in the forest, in the stream, everywhere, and Ram looked, and then Ram was gone too, and I didn't—I thought—"

"I know," said Rem.

"It's been *weeks*—"

"It's been more than a year," said Rem. Gently. "For me."

He stared at her.

She put the basket down. Carefully, on the frost, beside her feet. Then she stood up and looked at him, at his grey filthy clothes and his grey face, and her eyes were wet, and she was smiling, and she did not reach for him and she did not step away.

"You look terrible, Subaru-kun," she said.

* * *

"*Barusu.*"

Ram had come out behind her.

She stood in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded and one pink eyebrow raised, looking at the boy in the yard the way she looked at a dinner that was crude, barely edible, and a disgrace.

"Ram," said the boy. "*Ram*—"

"You walked," said Ram, "twenty thousand people to our door."

He stopped.

"Because a hood in a tent told you a story." She did not unfold her arms. "You saw a monster who keeps people in a box. You did not think: *Rem is very strong, and Ram is very rude, and nobody has ever kept either of them anywhere they did not want to be kept.* You did not think anything at all." She looked at him down her nose. "You are, and Ram says this with the full weight of more than a year of wondering whether you were still alive, a complete idiot."

The boy opened his mouth.

"*Complete*," said Ram.

He closed it.

* * *

"We live here," said Rem.

She said it simply. Not to argue. The way you tell someone where the kitchen is.

"This is our house. I have a room. Nee-sama has a room, beside mine. My name is on my door in ink." She looked at him. "Nobody keeps us. We can go anywhere we want. We don't want to go anywhere."

"But—" He looked from one of them to the other. "But home. The mansion. Roswaal. Emilia-tan's waiting, everyone's—I came to bring you *home*—"

"Home," said Ram, "is where Rem is."

She unfolded her arms at last.

"And Rem is here."

* * *

The boy stood in the frosted yard and did not seem to know what to do with his hands.

Rem looked at him for a long moment.

When he said *Emilia-tan*, something had moved in her face. Small. Quick. Mau, at the lime, had seen it, and did not know what it was. Not sadness, exactly. Something older and quieter than that. Then it was gone, and she was smiling again, a small kind smile, the kind you give someone you are glad is alive.

"Of course she's waiting," said Rem. "She would be."

She picked up the basket of washing again.

"Stay for breakfast," she said. "Taihei made porridge. And there's pear bread." She looked past him, at the white line, where the small girl in the big dress was still standing in the frost. "Both of you."

"*In fact,*" said the small girl, from the lime, with great dignity, "*Betty is hungry, I suppose.*"

* * *

He stayed.

He went in through the kitchen door behind Rem, slowly, looking at everything: the long room, the long table, the map on the wall with its pins, the people at breakfast who had all stopped eating to look at him. Ram walked behind him with her arms folded, the way a guard walks behind a prisoner. Betty came last, holding up the hem of her dress so it would not drag on Momo's clean floor.

At the door, he stopped.

He turned round and looked back at the yard. At Mau, still at the lime line with his hands in his coat pockets, watching them go in with a pleased, friendly smile.

Gojo was standing by the barn. He had been there the whole time, with his blindfold up.

The boy hesitated. Then he crossed to him. Quietly. So that nobody at the lime would hear.

"You're the one with the eyes," he said. "Right? On the field. I saw you looking at him."

Gojo did not answer.

"I saw something," said the boy. Very low. "Last night. Two nights ago. Whenever. I looked back. Three times, from the edge of the fire. He was helping a kid with a pole on his leg. A soldier. Young." He swallowed. "And the kid's hand came up. On its own. I don't know how I know that. It just—it didn't move like a person moving it. And the kid's eyes went—" He stopped. "They went *deep*. Like somebody opened a door behind them." He looked at Gojo. "And then he touched his forehead. Just two fingers. And your guy went down."

Gojo was very still.

"You saw that," he said.

"I saw that." The boy looked over at the lime, where Mau was crouched now, talking to Ichika, who had come out with her cup. Smiling. Easy. Kind. "I don't know what it means. I thought you should know."

Gojo looked at Mau for a long time.

"Yeah," he said at last. Very quietly. "Thank you."

The boy nodded once, and went in to breakfast.

Gojo stood by the barn with his blindfold up and his bare eyes on the boy at the lime line, and on the tidy place in his head that something had gone in and out of, and did not move for a long time.
