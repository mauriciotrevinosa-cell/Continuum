*When: Day 441, mid-afternoon. The long room half empty. The witches at the end of the long table with tea, as they are every afternoon now. Rem hanging the second wash. Subaru has been in the house for a day and a half.*

He had slept for fourteen hours.

Rem had put him in the little room at the end of the west wing that nobody used, with a blanket and a jug of water, and he had lain down meaning to think, and woken up with the sun on the other side of the sky and Betty asleep at the foot of the bed in her enormous dress like a cat.

He had eaten. Taihei's porridge, and then Momo's pear bread, and then a second helping of the porridge because Taihei had looked at him and put it down without asking. He had sat at the long table among sixty strangers who all knew Rem's name, and watched Rem laugh at something the girl with the hood said, and watched Ram tell a man named Stark that his chopping was a disgrace, and watched them both walk through this house as if they had lived there all their lives.

And he had thought.

That was the problem. He had always been very good at thinking. He had thought himself out of a hundred deaths. He thought now, and by the afternoon of the second day he had thought his way, very carefully, step by step, to the wrong answer.

* * *

It was a spell.

It had to be. Rem and Ram did not *live* in places. Rem and Ram lived at the mansion, with Roswaal, with Emilia-tan, with him. They did not have rooms with their names on the doors in ink. They did not laugh at girls in hoods. Something had been done to them.

And the people who did things to people were sitting at the end of the long table drinking tea.

He had seen them at breakfast. Seven women in a row, all different, all wrong in some way that made the back of his neck prickle. A woman in black with a book. A woman with dirt on her hands and a dreamy face. A tall pale one with very long hair who never moved. A small one in the middle with her hands open on the table and a chair that wobbled when she shifted.

And at the end of the row, quiet, with a card in front of her on the table, white, with one word on it in crayon—

He knew her.

He had known her the instant he saw her. He had felt it in his chest like a hand closing. The silver hair. The violet eyes. The way she sat, very still, looking at nothing.

The Witch of Envy.

She had loved him. In his world, in the dark between deaths, in the shadow that came every time he tried to tell anyone, she had held him and said it over and over and over. *I love you. I love you. I love you.* He had hated it. He had been terrified of it.

He was not terrified now.

He was thinking.

* * *

He walked up to the end of the long table at three in the afternoon.

Betty came with him. She had not wanted to. She had said *Subaru, I suppose this is a very bad idea, in fact*, and he had said *trust me*, and she had come, holding his sleeve, with her face set.

The witches looked up.

All seven of them. At once. The way a row of cats look up when a dog walks into the room.

He did not look at the others. He looked at her. At the quiet one, at the end, with her card.

"Satella," he said.

She looked at him.

Her face did not change. Not at all. She looked at him the way she looked at the window, or the wall, or the far end of the yard. Her hands were folded on the table in front of her, on either side of the card.

ALWAYS, said the card. In a child's crayon.

"I know who you are," said Subaru. He put both hands on the table and leaned on them, the way he had leaned on tables in a hundred rooms in his world, in front of nobles and knights and cultists, when he had worked something out and was about to win. "And you know who I am. Don't you."

She did not answer.

"You love me," said Subaru.

The long room went quiet. Not all of it. The part close enough to hear. Rem, outside in the yard at the washing line, had stopped with a wet shirt in her hands, looking in through the window.

"You told me," said Subaru. "Over and over. Every time. In the dark." He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. "So you'll do what I say. That's how it works, isn't it? You love me, so you'll help me." He leaned closer. "Rem and Ram. Whatever this house has done to them. Undo it. Take them home. You can do that. I know you can." He smiled. The old smile. The one that had got him through a hundred doors. "For me."

* * *

Satella looked at him for a long time.

Then she looked down at the card on the table between her hands.

She did not pick it up. She looked at it, the way you look at something someone gave you once that means more than it should. Then she looked up again, past Subaru, out of the window, at the yard, where a boy with his coat open was crouched by the pump with a small girl and a cup of water, laughing.

She did not say anything at all.

"Satella," said Subaru.

Nothing.

"*Satella*. I'm talking to you."

* * *

A chair scraped.

The small one in the middle. The one with her hands open on the table and the chair that wobbled. She put down her teacup, carefully, in its saucer, without a sound. Then she stood up.

She was not tall. Standing, she was barely taller than Betty. She came round the end of the long table, unhurried, her open hands at her sides, and stepped between Subaru and the quiet one with the card, so that Subaru had to look down at her, and Satella was behind her.

She looked up at him.

Her eyes were very old. Older than Betty's. Older than anything in the room.

"No," she said.

Subaru blinked.

"Who are—"

"The first," said the small woman. "Of us. In your world, they had a name for me. It was for someone smaller." She did not smile. "You may call me Pride, since you are so fond of names. It suits the conversation we are about to have."

Behind her, down the long table, the other witches had put down their cups. The woman in black had closed her book. The woman with the dirt on her hands was smiling, dreamily, the way you smile at weather you have been waiting for.

"Sit down, boy," said Pride. "You have walked a long way to say something very foolish. The least we can do is tell you why."

* * *

Subaru did not sit down.

Betty's hand tightened on his sleeve.

"*Subaru,*" she said, very quietly. "*I suppose you should sit, in fact.*"

He looked down at her. At her very old eyes in her very young face, looking up at him. Not angry. Something worse than angry.

He sat down.

Outside, at the washing line, Rem put the wet shirt back in the basket, very carefully, and wiped her hands on her apron, and came in.

Ram was already standing in the kitchen doorway with her arms folded. Nobody had seen her arrive.

At the end of the long table, Satella had not moved. She sat with her hands folded on either side of her card, and looked out of the window at the boy by the pump, and her face was perfectly still.

But her fingers, very slightly, had closed round the edge of the card.
