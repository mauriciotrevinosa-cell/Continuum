## Chapter 4 — What the Witches Said

*When: Day 441, mid-afternoon, continuous. The witches' end of the long table. Rem in the kitchen doorway with her apron on. Ram beside her with her arms folded. Betty holding Subaru's sleeve.*

It was the woman in black who began.

She did not put her book away. She kept it open on the table in front of her, one hand flat on the page, the way someone keeps a finger in a book they intend to go back to.

"You were the exception," said Echidna.

Subaru looked at her.

"In your world." She said it pleasantly, as if they were discussing the weather. "A boy from somewhere else. The only one in four hundred years. Everyone turned to look at you. The witches in the dark thought about you constantly, because there was nothing else like you in the whole of creation." She turned a page, without looking at it. "You thought we only thought about *you*."

"I didn't—"

"You did," said Echidna. "It's all right. In that world, it was even true." She looked up at him with her pale curious eyes. "Look round."

He looked round.

At the long table. At sixty people. A girl with a guitar. A pale quiet man who made terrible coffee on purpose. A man with white hair and a blindfold. An elf. A slime who was a boy. A girl with a hood. An old woman in a high seat with her mouth set. A tired doctor with a cigarette she never lit.

"Every one of them," said Echidna, "came from somewhere else." She closed her book at last. "Here, you are not the exception. Here, you are ordinary." She smiled, very slightly. "It's quite restful, once you get used to it."

* * *

"And she did not love you," said Pride.

She had sat down again. Not in her own chair. On the bench, beside Subaru, close, with her open hands in her lap, so that she was speaking to him at the level of his shoulder. Satella sat at the end of the table behind her, perfectly still.

"She told me," said Subaru. His voice was not steady. "Over and over. She—"

"She said it," said Pride. "That is not the same."

She did not look round at Satella. She did not need to.

"We are witches," said Pride. "We are not people who happen to be called witches. We are the sins, boy. Each of us. Walking about with faces on." She tilted her head. "What is envy?"

He did not answer.

"Wanting what someone else has," said Pride. "That is all it is. That is all it ever was." She looked at him with her very old eyes. "You gave love away to everyone. To the half-elf. To the maids. To the little spirit on your sleeve. To strangers in the street. You threw it about like a man throwing coins at a festival." She paused. "And she watched you do it, from the dark, every time. And she envied it. Every coin you threw to somebody else."

The long room was very quiet.

"She did not love you," said Pride. "She envied the love you gave everyone else. She wanted the same. She did what she did to you because she wanted it, and because she could not have it, and because she is what she is." She looked down at her open hands. "You were so sure you were the centre of it, you never once asked what she was looking at."

* * *

Subaru stood up.

He had to. He could not sit still under it. He stood at the end of the long table with Betty still holding his sleeve and his face white and his hands shaking, and he looked at the row of witches, and at Rem in the doorway, and at all of them.

"You don't know," he said.

Nobody answered.

"You don't know what I did. What I went through." His voice was going up. He could hear it. He could not stop it. "I *died*. Do you understand? Not once. Over and over. Fifty times. More. I stopped counting. I died for them. For Rem. For Emilia-tan. For Ram. For everyone. Every time something went wrong, I died and went back and tried again, and nobody knew, nobody *could* know, I couldn't tell anyone, and I did it *anyway*—" He was crying again. "So don't sit there and tell me what I am. You don't *know*. None of you know what it's like."

* * *

"Mau died four hundred and thirty-seven times."

It was Rem.

She had not moved from the kitchen doorway. She stood there with her apron on and her hands folded in front of it, and she said it quietly, the way she said everything, the way you tell someone where the kitchen is.

Subaru turned and looked at her.

"In a trial," said Rem. "Before any of us came. Four hundred and thirty-seven times. He told us. At breakfast, by accident, and then in the afternoon, properly, because he chose to." She did not look away. "He counted them. Every one. He still knows the number."

"Rem—"

"And he never once asked anybody for anything," said Rem. "Not for that. Not for anything. Not to be thanked. Not to be understood. Not to be loved more for it." She paused. "He doesn't wear it. He didn't want us to know. We found out because he couldn't help it."

The long room was absolutely silent.

"I'm not saying it to hurt you, Subaru-kun," said Rem. Gently. "I'm saying it because you said *nobody here knows what it's like*." She looked past him, out of the window, at the yard, where a boy with his coat open was walking toward the barn with Yuka, talking, his hands in his pockets. "Someone here does. He's right there. He just never said it to win anything."

* * *

Subaru did not say anything.

He stood at the end of the long table and looked out of the window at the boy in the yard. The boy who had lain face down in the field two nights ago. Who had shielded the army that came to kill him. Who had asked, at the white line, at dawn, *are you hurt?*

"And he is not a saint," said Echidna, from her place. Mildly. "Let us be fair, since we are being honest."

Pride nodded.

"Our treasure," said the woman with the dirt on her hands, dreamily. Daphne. "Every second week. For more than a year. He came and sat with us, and we asked him questions, and he answered. He never once complained."

"He forgot once," said Pride. "An appointment. And we told him he owed us two hours instead of one. And he said *okay*." She paused. "That is all he said. *Okay.*"

"He went quiet," said Echidna. "Once. Badly. He decided something alone and told nobody, and it hurt the people who love him." She opened her book again. "And then he came back, and told everyone, and said he was sorry, and stopped doing it." She turned a page. "That is harder than never failing. Most people would rather never fail than have to do that."

"You would have made it a drama," said Pride. To Subaru. Not cruelly. "You would have thrown yourself into it and made everyone watch." She looked at him with her old eyes. "He made it a breakfast."

* * *

Then Satella spoke.

Nobody in the house had heard her speak more than a dozen words in a year. She spoke now, from the end of the long table, without moving, in a voice so quiet the whole room leaned to hear it.

"I envied it," she said.

Subaru turned.

She was looking at him. Not at the window. At him. Her violet eyes, steady, with nothing in them he recognised. No hunger. No dark. Just a woman at a table, looking at someone she used to know.

"The love you gave everyone else," said Satella. "Pride is right. I envied it. Every coin." She looked down at the card between her hands. ALWAYS, in crayon. "I took something from you, so that you would always come back to me. To the dark. To the place where I was waiting." She was quiet for a moment. "I don't need it any more."

"What—"

"Here," said Satella, "people envy *me*." She almost smiled. "I have a card. And a house. And a boy who gave me a family's love when I asked for the other kind, and meant it." She lifted her eyes to his. "I don't need you to keep coming back."

She held out her hand across the table.

Not to touch him. Palm up. Open. As if she were giving something back.

"It was mine," said Satella. "Your return. I'm done with it." She closed her hand, slowly, on nothing. "You can have your death back. It's yours. Only one. Like everyone."

* * *

Subaru felt it go.

He did not know what it was until it was gone. A weight he had carried so long he had stopped knowing it was there. A cold hand on the back of his neck that he had stopped feeling years ago. A door behind him that had always been open, that he had always known he could fall back through, every time.

Shut.

He sat down on the bench very suddenly, because his legs would not hold him.

"I can die," he said. To nobody. In a small, strange voice.

"Yes," said Pride. "Like everyone."

"I can't go *back*."

"No," said Pride. "You'll have to get it right the first time. Like everyone." She looked at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she put one of her small open hands on his arm. Just for a moment. "Most people manage. You'll find it's not as bad as you think."

* * *

He sat there for a long time.

Betty climbed up onto the bench beside him and leaned against his arm, the way she had leaned against it in the dark at the edge of the camp, and said nothing at all, which for Betty was the kindest thing she knew how to do.

Rem came out of the kitchen doorway at last. She sat down on his other side. She did not touch him. She sat there.

Ram stayed in the doorway with her arms folded. But she had stopped looking at him as if he were a disgrace. She looked at him, now, as if he were a dinner that had been badly made, but might, with a great deal of work, someday be edible.

* * *

Pride stood up.

She went back to her own chair, the one that wobbled, and sat in it, and picked up her teacup, which had gone cold, and drank from it anyway.

Then, without looking at Subaru, to the table in general:

"Our treasure is not himself this week."

The long room went still again. Differently.

"That is not your business, boy," said Pride. "It is ours. And the house's." She set the cup down. "But since you are going to be living here, and since you were the last one to see him before he fell, you may as well know that we have noticed."

Down the table, at the window, Daphne had turned her head.

She was looking out at the yard. At the boy walking back from the barn with Yuka, laughing at something she had said, his hands in his pockets, easy, kind, friendly. The way he had walked past the witches' end of the table that morning, and the morning before, with a polite nod, and gone on.

Daphne's dreamy face had stopped being dreamy.

She was frowning.
