*When: Day 444. The day after Yuka's eighteenth. The paper lanterns still up, crooked, unlit. Umaru has not taken down the EIGHTEEN sheet. Mau slept on the long-room bench again.*

Armin put the sheet on the table at breakfast.

He did not say anything first. He unfolded it beside his bowl, the back of his arithmetic with the small careful writing on it, and turned it round so the people nearest him could read it, and waited.

Fern read it first. Then Shoko. Then Maki, leaning over Fern's shoulder.

*Day 442. He asked who Ciel is.*

Nobody said anything.

At the far end of the table, Mau was helping Ichika cut her bread.

* * *

"I saw something tidy," said Gojo.

He said it to the table. Not loudly. He had his blindfold down for once. He was looking at his porridge.

"On the morning after. In his head. Something went in and came out again and put everything back very neatly." He did not look up. "I said it to Shoko. I didn't say it to anyone else, because I didn't know what it meant."

"I saw a hand," said the boy in grey.

Everyone looked at him. He had been at the long table for three days and had not said a word at breakfast in any of them. He sat between Rem and Betty with his spoon in his hand, very pale.

"In the field," said Subaru. "He was lifting a pole off a soldier. A kid. And the kid's hand came up. Not like the kid was doing it. And his eyes went—" He stopped. "Deep. Like somebody opened a door behind them. And he touched Mau's forehead, two fingers, and Mau went down." He looked at the table. "I told him." He nodded at Gojo. "On the first morning."

"He asked who Ciel is," said Armin quietly. "And he told me how he came here. He said Yuka was here first. He said the gate had been looking for him. He said he woke in the woods and walked until he found the settlement, and then the Red Gate, and that's where he and Yuka got close." He looked down at the sheet. "He didn't say anything about the Trial. Or the coins. Or the Muslin. Or the tower."

He did not say *or Frieren*. He did not have to.

Frieren was sitting very still at her place, with her cup in both hands.

* * *

"He's like Taihei," said Maki.

Taihei, at the stove, turned round.

"Not now," said Maki. "When you came. Your first month." She did not look away from him. "You were polite to everyone. Grateful. You said thank you for everything. You tried not to take up room. You slept where nobody would mind and you asked before you sat down." She looked down the table, at Mau, cutting Ichika's bread into small squares, saying something that made Ichika giggle. "That's him. Since the field. He's being a good guest."

Taihei did not say anything.

He looked at Mau for a long time. Then he turned back to the stove.

"Yes," he said. Very quietly. To the porridge. "That's what that is."

* * *

Fern asked him.

She waited until he had finished with Ichika's bread. Then she put her hand flat on the ledger, closed, the way she did when she was going to say something that would go in it, and said, across the long table, gently:

"Mau."

He looked up. He smiled.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Why don't you treat us like family?"

The long room went quiet. All of it. The wave, from one end to the other.

Mau looked at her. He did not seem upset by the question. He seemed to be thinking about it, honestly, the way he thought about everything.

"I like all of you," he said. "Really. You've been so good to me. This is a really good house." He looked round the table, at all of them, warmly. "But I haven't known you very long." He paused. "I don't want to act like I belong somewhere before I do. That's not fair to anyone." He smiled at Fern, a little apologetically. "Is that weird?"

Fern did not answer.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she opened the ledger, found the page with his column on it, crooked as ever, every entry since the beginning in her small exact hand, the check, the days, the floors, the points, everything. She looked at it.

She closed the ledger again without writing anything.

"No," she said. "It's not weird."

* * *

Frieren found him in the yard after breakfast.

She did not run at him this time. She did not reach. She had learned that. She came round the corner of the barn where he was splitting kindling with Stark and stopped ten paces away, on the frost, with her hands at her sides, and waited until he looked up.

"Mau," she said.

He put the axe down.

"Hi," he said. Carefully. He had stopped smiling quite so easily. Something in the way she stood made him careful.

"I'm Frieren," she said.

He nodded. "I know. The east room. You read."

"Yes." Her voice was very level. "I read. I've read for a thousand years." She did not move. "And for a year and a half, I've read in the east room. With you. At the reset hour, I put my foot on your shin and say *one*, and you say *still here*." She paused. "And then I put my ear on your chest and say *two*, because you have two hearts now, and I'm the one who counts them."

Mau looked at her.

Stark had stopped splitting kindling. He stood very still with the axe in his hands, looking at the ground.

"I'm sorry," said Mau. Gently. "I don't—"

"You told me first," said Frieren. "Every time. You said you would. You put it on a note under the ridge." She took the folded note out of her pocket. She did not unfold it. She held it. "*I told you first.* That's your hand."

She took a step toward him.

He stepped back.

* * *

Not far. Not the little step through nothing, four paces at once. Just a step back, on the frost, the way you step back from someone at a market who has stood too close.

She stopped.

"Please," said Mau.

He said it kindly. That was the worst thing. He said it the way he had said it to twenty thousand soldiers at the white line. Tired. Kind. Meaning it.

"I'm sorry. I can see this matters to you. I can see you're—" He stopped. "But I don't know you. I don't. And you keep coming toward me like I'm someone else, and it's making me—" He looked for the word. "Uncomfortable." He swallowed. "Could you please stay away? Just for a while. Please."

Frieren stood on the frost with the note in her hand.

She did not say anything.

She did not cry. Not in the yard. Not in front of him. She looked at him for a long time, with her face perfectly still, the lake with no wind on it.

Then she nodded, once.

She turned round and walked back round the corner of the barn, toward the house, not fast, not slow, with the note held in her hand at her side.

Stark put the axe down very carefully on the chopping block. Then he went after her.

* * *

Mau stood in the yard by the woodpile.

He felt bad. He did not know why he felt so bad. She was a stranger. A kind one, clearly, and sad about something, and she had got him mixed up with somebody, and he had asked her, as gently as he knew how, to give him some room.

That was all.

He did not know why his chest hurt. Both hearts. The warm one, and the cold one half a step behind.

He picked up the axe again and split a piece of kindling, badly, and then another.

* * *

In the long room, Ori was at the table with her list.

She had the new page open. *THE GAME. (He doesn't know me.) (He winks.) (Rules: ?)* She had been writing rules under it for two days. Small ones. Guesses. *Maybe he wants me to find a clue. Maybe the clue is in the fort. Maybe Yuka knows.*

She had watched the yard from the window. She had seen Frieren walk away from the woodpile.

She looked at the page for a long time.

Then she took her pencil and drew one straight line through the whole of it, very neatly, the way she crossed things out on the shadow list when they were done.

Under it, she wrote:

*Not a game.*

She did not cry either. She folded the list and put it in her pocket and went and sat on the floor just inside the east room, by the connecting door, where she could hear Frieren breathing on the other side, and did not count anything.

* * *

That afternoon, Daphne came to the clinic.

Shoko was there alone, writing in her own ledger, the one with his numbers in it. She looked up.

The witch stood in the clinic doorway with dirt on her hands from the fields and her dreamy face not dreamy at all.

"I would like an hour," said Daphne. "With him. Here. Soon." She looked at Shoko. "Before he decides he doesn't want anyone looking."

Shoko put her pen down.

"You know what it is," she said.

Daphne was quiet for a long moment.

"I know what it looks like," she said. "Where I come from, there were things that ate memories. Names. Whole lives." She looked at her dirty hands. "I was one of the things they were afraid of." She lifted her eyes. "Something has been eating in that boy, doctor. Very carefully. And I would like to know what it left behind."
