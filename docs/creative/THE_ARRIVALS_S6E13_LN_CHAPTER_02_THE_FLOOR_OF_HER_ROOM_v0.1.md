## Chapter 8 — The Floor of Her Room

*When: Day 444, night. The house gone to bed early and quietly, the way a house goes to bed after a hard day. The east room door shut. The connecting door to Ori's room shut too, for the first time anyone can remember.*

The bench was wrong.

He had slept on it three nights and it had been fine. Narrow, hard, with his coat under his head and Taihei's blanket over him and the stove ticking down in the corner. Fine.

Tonight it was wrong.

He lay on it in the dark with the long room empty round him and the paper lanterns hanging crooked over his head, and could not stop seeing the elf's face in the yard. The way she had nodded, once, and walked away with that folded paper in her hand. The way she had not cried.

He did not know her.

He did not know why it felt like he had done something terrible.

He got up.

* * *

The linen was in the big cupboard at the turn of the stair. He knew that; he had helped Taihei put the winter blankets in it on the first-frost day. He took two. Thick ones, wool, smelling of cedar. He tucked them under his arm and went up the stairs in the dark, past the doors, and did not stop at the east room.

He stopped at Yuka's.

He knocked. Softly. Two and one, the way he always did.

He did not know why he knew to knock two and one.

* * *

She opened it in her nightclothes with her hair down and her face blank with sleep, and then not blank at all.

"*Oppa*?"

"Hey," he said. He held up the blankets. "Sorry. The bench is awful. Is it all right?" He was already stepping past her into the room, the way you step into a room you have been in a hundred times. He looked at the floor by the window, the space between her bed and the wall, and started unfolding the first blanket. "Same as always. Am I taking up too much floor?"

Yuka stood in the doorway with her hand still on the door.

"Same as always," she said.

"Since the Red Gate." He spread the second blanket on top of the first and sat down on them and looked up at her, smiling. "Is it? Too much floor?"

She looked at him for a long time.

He did not know. She could see he did not know. He thought this was ordinary. He thought he had done this every night for months, ever since some Red Gate she had never been in with him, and that she would say *no, it's fine*, and get back into bed, and they would talk until they fell asleep, the way they always did.

They had never done it. Not once.

"No," said Yuka. "It's not too much floor."

She shut the door.

* * *

They talked.

She got back into bed and lay on her side facing the window, facing him, with the blanket pulled up to her chin. He lay on his back on the floor with his hands behind his head and looked at the ceiling. The window was a square of stars. Neither of them lit a lamp.

He talked about the beach. The sea. The fish. The old woman at the stall who had winked at him and he did not know why. He talked about the army, the bell, the man with blades for arms who had grown them back and back, Igris saying *good, then I will not run out*. He laughed. He said Umaru was going to audit the receipt book and find *fish, two, very good* and send him to Ministry jail.

She laughed too.

It was easy. That was what frightened her. It was the easiest thing in the world, lying in the dark talking to him, and it was a thing they had never done, and he talked to her the way you talk to the person you talk to every night.

* * *

"Can I tell you something?" said Yuka.

It was very late. The stars in the window had moved. He had gone quiet for a while and she had thought he was asleep.

"Mm."

"I've never told anyone here." She looked at the ceiling too, now. "Not my grandparents. Not Maki."

He turned his head on the blankets to look at her.

"My brother," said Yuka.

"Tsurugi," said Mau.

She went very still. Then she remembered. He knew his name. He had always known; Mau knew everybody's family. That part had stayed.

"Tsurugi," said Yuka. "He—" She stopped. "Things are complicated. With him. He thinks nobody chose him. Ever. Not our parents. Not our grandparents. Not anyone." She pulled the blanket tighter. "And there's a coin. Somewhere. Someone's going to use it, one day, and he's going to walk through that door, and he's going to see me here." Her voice was very small. "With all of this. With a family. With a house that chose me." She swallowed. "And I'm afraid of what I'll be when he does. If I'll be someone he can stand to look at. Or if I'll just be another person who got chosen when he didn't."

She had never said it out loud.

Mau did not say anything for a long time.

"You'll be his sister," he said at last. "That's all. That's what you'll be." He looked at the ceiling. "And if he can't stand to look at you at first, you'll wait. You're good at waiting. You waited for me." He smiled in the dark. "And then he'll look."

She did not answer.

She did not ask him what he meant by *you waited for me*. She was afraid of what he would say.

* * *

She did not remember getting out of bed.

That was the thing she would think about afterward, again and again. She remembered talking. She remembered him going quiet, his breathing slowing, the way breathing slows when someone drifts off in the middle of a sentence. She remembered lying on her side looking at the dark shape of him on the floor by the window.

And then she was on the floor.

Beside him. On top of the blankets, not under them, in her nightclothes, lying on her side with her back to the wall and her face a hand's breadth from his shoulder. She had her own blanket, dragged down off the bed. She did not remember dragging it.

He was asleep.

* * *

The reset hour came.

She knew it by the stars, and by the quiet. Every night since she had come to the house, at this hour, somewhere down the corridor, a light had gone on behind the east room door, or a murmur had come through the wall: two voices, very low. *One. Still here. Two.* Everybody in the west wing had heard it, some nights, if they were awake. Nobody ever said anything about it.

Tonight there was nothing.

He did not wake. He did not stir. He lay on his back on her floor with his hands on his chest and slept straight through it, breathing slow and even, the warm heart and the cold one half a step behind, for the first time since she had known him.

He did not remember the Trial. He did not remember why he woke.

So he did not wake.

* * *

She lay there.

Very close. Not touching. She could feel the warmth coming off his shoulder through the cold air between them. She could hear both hearts if she held her breath.

And something in her chest that she had kept very small and very quiet for a long time, so long she had almost stopped knowing it was there, sat up.

She felt it. Her heart going. Too fast. In the dark, a hand's breadth from him, with his face turned a little toward her in sleep, the scars from the shrine silver on his cheek in the starlight.

She shut her eyes.

"*Please,*" she whispered. So quietly that not even she could hear it, almost. "*Please, no.*"

He did not wake.

"*He's Oppa,*" said Yuka, to nobody, to the dark, to the thing in her chest. "*He's Oppa. He's Frieren's. He doesn't know. He doesn't know anything.*" She pressed her face into her blanket. "*Please, no.*"

The thing in her chest did not lie down again.

She did not move away either.

She lay on the floor of her own room beside him until the window went grey, with her heart going and her eyes open, and did not sleep at all.

* * *

Down the corridor, in the east room, Frieren lay alone on her side of the bed.

At the reset hour she had put her foot out, the way she always did. She had found nothing. She had said *one*, very quietly, to nobody.

Then she had lain there and listened, the way she had listened every night for a year and a half, for a voice through the wall. Any voice. *Still here.*

There was nothing.

She did not know where he was sleeping. She had not let herself go down to the long room to look.

Through the connecting door, shut tonight for the first time, she could not hear Ori breathing.

She lay in the dark with the folded note in her hand, and counted nothing, and waited.
