*When: Day 445. Grey morning, no frost. Yuka has not slept. Mau slept very well. Umaru finally took the EIGHTEEN sheet down and pinned it inside the Ministry book.*

She said it without thinking.

That was how it went wrong. She had not slept. She came down to breakfast with her eyes heavy and her hair still down, and he was already at the long table, in the place beside her grandmother that had become his, and he looked up and smiled at her and moved along the bench to make room.

She sat down.

"Morning, Oppa," said Yuka.

The way she had said it every morning since Glue. Without thinking. The safest word in the world.

* * *

He looked at her.

Kindly. And a little confused, for a moment, the way you look at someone who has called you by the wrong name and you are trying to work out whether to correct them.

Then his face cleared.

"Oh," he said. "Right." He smiled. "Tsurugi."

Yuka stopped with her spoon halfway to her bowl.

"You were talking about him last night," said Mau. "You miss him." He nodded, as if that settled it. "Of course you do. *Oppa*." He said it gently, the way you would say someone else's word for someone else. "Are you going to bring him?"

Down the table, Maki had stopped eating.

"Because I've got—" Mau was already reaching into his coat. The inside pocket on the left. Where a man keeps something he means to keep close. "Do you want my coin yet? Or not yet?" He felt in the pocket. "It's yours if you want it. You know that. Whenever you're ready. If you want to use it for him, I—"

He stopped.

He felt in the pocket again.

* * *

It was not there.

The bullet was. Small and cold, where it had always been. But nothing warm beside it. He frowned. He felt the other inside pocket. The outside ones. He stood up from the bench and patted his coat all over, the way you do when you have lost your keys, and then sat down again, puzzled.

"Huh," he said. "That's weird."

Nobody said anything.

"I always keep it there." He looked at the coat. "The warm one. It's always—" He laughed, a little embarrassed. "I must have left it somewhere. In the clinic, maybe. When Shoko had my coat off." He looked at Yuka. "Sorry. I'll find it. It's yours whenever you want it."

Yuka did not move.

At the end of the long table, by the window, in the place where he always sat when he came in from the kilns, Kaneki had gone very still.

He was holding one of the good cups in both hands. He did not drink from it. He looked down at it, the way he had looked down at it on the night in the summer when the boy across the table had put something warm in his palm and closed his fingers over it and said *for her*, and Kaneki had not been able to say anything at all.

He did not have it any more. He had used it, in the summer, on the seventh night, the way it had been given to him to use.

It was sitting beside him now. Touka, with her hand flat on the table very near his. And asleep upstairs, in a bed too big for her, with her wooden cup on the shelf beside it, filled exactly to the line.

He looked at Mau patting his coat and laughing at himself, and then at Touka, and then down at the cup again.

He did not say anything. He did not know what he could possibly say.

* * *

"It's fine," said Yuka.

Her voice came out very steady. She did not know how.

"I don't need it," she said. "Not yet." She looked down at her porridge. "Not yet, Oppa."

"Okay," said Mau warmly. "Whenever you're ready."

He went back to his breakfast.

She sat beside him and did not eat hers, and did not say anything else, and under the table her hands were holding each other so hard her knuckles were white.

She had stopped correcting him.

She did not know when. Sometime in the night, maybe. Sometime on the floor of her room in the dark, with the reset hour going past and him not waking. She had stopped saying *that's not true*, and *you don't remember*, and *you were never there*. She had stopped saying anything at all.

She did not know if that was kindness or cowardice.

She did not know if it was something worse.

* * *

Daphne asked after lunch.

She did it properly. Shoko was with her, in the clinic doorway, with her coffee. The witch stood in front of Mau in the yard, where he was mending a hinge on the barn door with Wakana, with her dirty hands folded and her face very calm.

"I would like an hour," said Daphne. "With you. In the clinic. Just to look." She smiled, dreamily, the old way, though Shoko could see it cost her something. "I look at things. In the fields. Seeds. Roots. I'm told I'm good at it."

Mau smiled back.

"That's kind," he said. "But I'm fine. Really. Shoko checked." He looked at Shoko. "You checked."

"I checked your body," said Shoko.

"Right. And it's fine." He turned back to the hinge. "Thank you, though."

"Just an hour," said Daphne. "Only looking. I won't touch anything."

And something happened in his face.

He did not know what. Shoko saw it. A flicker. Very fast. Gone almost before it came. Not anger. Something under anger. The look an animal gets when a hand comes near something it has hidden.

"No," said Mau.

Then he heard himself say it, and looked surprised, and laughed, and was kind again.

"Sorry," he said. "That came out wrong. I just—" He stopped. He did not seem to know how to finish. "I don't really want anybody looking. Inside. I don't know why." He looked at the hinge. "Is that weird?"

"No," said Daphne softly. "It's not weird at all."

She looked at Shoko.

Shoko looked back.

Neither of them said anything until he had gone off round the barn with Wakana to find a better screw. Then Daphne said, very quietly, to the yard:

"It doesn't want to be found."

"What doesn't?"

"Whatever it put in his place," said Daphne. She looked at her dirty hands. "It's sitting where he used to be. And it's comfortable there." She was quiet for a long moment. "It doesn't want to leave."

* * *

Yuka watched from the kitchen window.

She had seen Daphne go up to him. She had seen the flicker in his face, even from there. She had seen him say *no*.

She knew what Daphne was. Everyone in the house knew what the witches were. She knew what *an hour, just to look* meant, and she knew that if the witch of gluttony looked inside him, she would find whatever had been done, and she would start to undo it.

And the boy who had taken her to the sea on Kaisel two days before, who had eaten the other half of his cookie with her at sunset, who had lain on her floor last night and told her *you'll be his sister, that's all*, would go back to being Frieren's.

And would not remember the sea.

She stood at the kitchen window with her hands holding each other so hard they hurt, and hated herself for the thing that had just gone through her chest when he said *no*.

Because it had been relief.
