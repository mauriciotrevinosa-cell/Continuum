## Chapter 11 — Coats

*When: Inside the gate. The first day, and the second, and the third. No sun, only a wrong-coloured light that dims and comes back. Outside, an hour has become something else. Nobody inside can know what.*

It was cold.

That was the first problem, and it was a very ordinary one, and he was almost grateful for it. Yuka was shivering in her coat before they had walked a hundred paces across the old stone, and his own coat had a tear in the back from the field that the wind came through like a knife.

"Shop," said Yuka, through her teeth.

He opened it.

* * *

It came up in his window the way the notice had said. A list. Things, with prices beside them in the tower's script. Food. Rope. Lamps. Potions in small bottles, very expensive. Blades. Clothes.

And beside the list, for the first time in the window, a little box of his own. *Inventory.*

He looked into it.

Crystals. Dozens of them. The rough pale dungeon crystals the house had been collecting since the tower opened, the ones Senku studied and Umaru kept a ledger of, the ones that bought potions and tools in the old dungeon's shop. He had been carrying the house's spare stock in his shadow for weeks, in case of an emergency. He did not remember being asked to. He just knew they were his to carry.

"They're the house's," he said. "Not mine."

"It's an emergency," said Yuka.

"It's an emergency," he agreed.

He bought two coats.

Proper ones. Long and heavy, lined with something soft and grey, with hoods. Boots. Gloves. A lamp that burned without oil. Bread that did not go stale, and dried meat, and a pot. He bought a blanket, and then looked at Yuka, and bought a second one.

Then he took Umaru's receipt book out of his pocket, the one from the beach, and wrote every single thing down. *Coats. Two. From the house's crystals. Will repay.* He showed it to Yuka.

"She's going to audit this," he said.

"She's going to audit this so hard," said Yuka.

She put on her coat. It came down to her ankles. She pulled the hood up and her face disappeared inside it, and she looked at him out of it, and he laughed, and she laughed, and for a moment it was just two people in a cold place in good coats, and nothing else.

* * *

The points were harder.

He had held them since the end of summer. A hundred and thirty-four. He did not remember why, exactly. He knew there had been a reason. A sheet on a wall that said *DON'T TOUCH ANYTHING*. The window growing.

*Because the Noise was making room,* said Ciel. *In your window. For the rest of you. You held them so nothing went in before the room was finished.* A pause. *It's finished now. It finished all at once, before the field. It's safe to spend some.*

He did not remember the room being finished. He believed her anyway.

But the gate did not care about reasons. The ice arrow had made that clear. Whatever was up on that wall did not wait for anybody to finish thinking.

"I have to spend them," he said. "Some of them. We need everything we can get in here."

Yuka looked at him out of her hood.

"Half," she said.

"Half?"

"Only half." She said it very firmly. "Or Umaru will be mad."

He looked at her.

He did not know why that made his chest hurt. It was a joke. It was the kind of thing she said. But the way she said it was like she was holding on to something, a rope across a gap, and the rope was a girl with a hood who kept a Ministry book and would be mad.

"Half," he said.

They chose together, sitting on a fallen block of old stone in their new coats with the shop closed and the window open, arguing about every point. Endurance, because of the cold and because Shoko would want it. Agility. A little into the new fields, the ones beside his cursed energy and his mana, that had filled in the overload and that he did not remember filling. Sixty-seven points. He felt them go in, one by one, and the cold got a little further away.

Sixty-seven left. Held.

"Umaru would be proud," said Yuka.

"Umaru would have made a sheet."

"Umaru would have made *three* sheets."

* * *

He sent the soldiers out at what passed for dusk.

Ten of them. The ten in front. Up out of the old stone in their rows and out across the ruin in fours, the way they had gone out past the ridge. He shut his eyes and turned their pages, one by one, and said what he saw out loud, and Yuka sat beside him on the block and listened.

Walls. Towers, broken, rimed with ice. A frozen river. A courtyard full of statues that were not statues; things, frozen where they stood, a long time ago. And at the far end of the ruin, a tower that was not broken. Tall and pale and whole, with a dark gap high up in it.

"That's where it is," he said. "The arrow."

He opened his eyes.

Igris was standing in front of him.

He had not called him. He had not said the name. The knight had come up out of his shadow on his own, silently, and was standing a pace away on the old stone with his long sword sheathed at his hip and his helm bowed.

*"My liege,"* said Igris.

Mau stared at him.

*"Forgive me,"* said Igris. *"I was not summoned."* He did not lift his head. *"This is a cold place, and a strange one, and the lady is not used to it. Permit me to keep the first watch."* A pause. *"I would consider it an honour."*

Yuka had gone very still beside him.

"You—" Mau stopped. "Sure. Yes. Thank you, Igris."

The knight bowed. Deeper. Then he turned and walked to the edge of the firelight, where the old stone fell away into the dark ruin, and stood there with his back to them, looking out, the way he stood at the north end at home.

Mau looked at his back for a long time.

"They don't usually do that," he said quietly. "Come up on their own. Talk without being asked."

Yuka did not answer.

She was looking at the knight too. At the way he stood. At the way he had said *the lady*, and looked, for one moment before he turned away, not at Mau, not at the ruin, but at her.

As if he knew exactly who she was. And exactly who she was not.

* * *

They found the guardian on the third day.

It was in the whole tower, as he had seen. A tall pale thing, thin as frost, with a bow made of ice and arrows that grew from its fingers. It stood at the top of the tower in a room with a wide window and shot at anything that moved in the ruin below, and it never missed, and it never stopped.

They did it carefully.

Lantern went up first, wings wide, to draw its eye; the arrows broke on his dark scales like hail. Igris went up the tower stair. Yuka went with Mau, because she would not stay behind, and when the thing turned from Lantern to the stair and drew, it was Yuka's rope that went across the room, low, and caught its ankle, the way it had caught the guardian's on a roof under a grey lid of sky, a long time ago, on the tenth floor.

He did not remember that. He thought it was the first time she had ever done it.

He finished it. The pull, at the right moment, and Igris's sword, and the thing went over the edge of its own window and shattered on the ice below like a dropped glass.

*Level 1 cleared.*

*Level 2: open.*

He stood at the window of the tower, breathing hard, and turned round, grinning, to say something to Yuka.

She was not looking at the window. She was looking at him.

* * *

She had been looking at him all day.

All three days. Since the ice arrow. Since he pulled her against his chest without thinking and turned so that his back was where her head had been.

She knew what was happening.

She was not stupid. She was eighteen and she had been in the house a long time and she had watched people love each other there, slowly, the way the house did everything. Eren and Mikasa. Momo and Okarun. Fern and Stark, pretending not to. Frieren and Mau.

She knew what it looked like when someone looked at you the way he looked at her now.

He did not mean anything by it. Not the way it would mean something if he remembered. He thought she was his. His Red Gate. His floor of her room. His sea. In the history that something had written in him, she was the one he had got close to, and he looked at her the way you look at that person, and he did not know he was doing it.

And she did not know what to do with the part of her that liked it.

* * *

That night, by the oil-less lamp, with Igris at the edge of the light and the second level's door standing open somewhere in the dark, she lay in her new coat under her new blanket and could not sleep, and thought the thing she had been trying not to think for three days.

He had loved Frieren. Before. Completely. Everyone in the house knew it. You could see it from the yard.

And now Frieren was gone from him. Not dead. Just gone, taken out of him like a page torn out of a book. And without her, he had turned, the way a plant turns, toward the next nearest light.

Which was her.

*So is this what would have happened,* she thought, *if Frieren had never been there?*

*Is this what it would have been?*

She lay very still under the blanket and hated herself so completely for thinking it that she could not breathe for a moment.

Across the lamp, Mau was asleep, on his back, with his hands on his chest.

Down in his shadow, under the old cold stone, something very old and very large lay curled in the dark, awake, with its two blue eyes open, listening to a girl not sleep.

It did not say anything.

Not yet.
