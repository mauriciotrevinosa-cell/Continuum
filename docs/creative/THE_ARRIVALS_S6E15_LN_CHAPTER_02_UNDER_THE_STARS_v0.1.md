*When: Inside the gate. Some days in; she has lost count. The second level, a high moor of dark heather and standing stones. For once the sky has stars that look almost like stars.*

The scouts had been out since dusk.

He sent them in fours across the moor, the way he always did, to hunt for the guardian, which had not shown itself in three days. They found nothing but heather and stones and wind, and every hour he shut his eyes and turned their pages and said *nothing* out loud, and Yuka, beside him, said *nothing's good*, the way Anko said it.

Igris stood at the edge of the firelight with his back to them.

The sky was full of stars.

They were not the house's stars. *The Party* was not there, or *The Hundred and First*, or the one Umaru had written on the back of an invitation card. But they were stars, real-looking, close and cold and very many, the first sky inside the gate that had looked like a sky at all.

Yuka lay on her back on the blankets and looked at them. The little lotus rested against her wrist inside her cuff. She had kept wearing it.

She had sat closer to him tonight than she meant to. She had not decided to. She had sat down after the meal, the way she always did, and somewhere in the evening she had ended up with her shoulder against his, and had not moved.

He had not moved either.

* * *

Her heart was a mess.

That was the only word she had for it. Not fear. Not happiness. Not guilt. All of them, at once, in a tangle she could not pull apart, and under all of them, the thing that had sat up in her chest on the floor of her own room and would not lie down again.

He turned his head and looked at her.

She looked back.

The stars were in his eyes. The scars from the shrine were silver on his cheek. He was looking at her the way he had at the sea, when the water shone and she had turned inside his arms, and she knew what that look was now, and she knew she should look away.

She did not look away.

He kissed her.

* * *

It was not long. It was careful, the way he did most things that mattered: a question more than anything. When he drew back he was already opening his mouth to say *sorry*, she could see it, the apology coming up into his face before the kiss was even over.

She did not let him say it.

She did not think. That was the thing she would remember, afterward, again and again. She did not think at all. Her hands were in the front of his coat and she pulled him back and kissed him, and whatever it was in both of them that had never done this before, that neither of them had a name for, took hold.

She stopped thinking about Frieren.

She stopped thinking about the house, and the floor of her room, and the reset hour, and the coin, and Daphne at the barn door saying *just an hour*. She stopped thinking about anything except that he was close and she wanted him closer, that she had wanted it for longer than she had let herself know, and that here, under strange stars, in a place nobody could follow, there was nobody to see.

His coat came off. Then his shirt. She did not remember which of them did it.

Her hands went to his back.

* * *

And she felt them.

All of them.

Under her palms, all at once. The thousand thin lines from the shrine, raised and silver, everywhere, as if he had walked through rain made of knives. The three long ridges across his back from the hive, from the first time Beru opened him. The pucker on his shoulder where the jaws had closed. Twice. The tight shiny place on his side where a spear had gone in on eighty. The burn on his chest, from the hundred and first floor, a thin pink line.

And his hand, against her face. The left one. The two fingers that would not close, curled stiff against her cheek, because an old dragon's true breath had taken them, on seventy, when he stood alone in it so nobody else would have to.

She knew every one.

She knew where each of them had come from. She had been in the yard for every one. She had been at the long table when Shoko came out of the clinic and said *he'll live*. She had heard the whole house hold its breath.

He had taken every one of them for them. For her. For Frieren. For the whole house. So that none of them would ever have to stand where he stood.

And he did not remember a single one.

* * *

She started to cry.

She did not mean to. It came up out of her all at once, the way it had nearly come up at the sea, and this time she could not stop it. Her hands stayed on his back and she cried, with her face against his shoulder, the good one, and could not breathe.

He stopped.

At once. Completely. His hands went still. He drew back a little, just enough to see her face, and his face was frightened.

"Yuka," he said. "Yuka, I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Did I—" He stopped. "I thought— If I got it wrong, I'm sorry. I'll stop. We'll stop. I didn't mean to—"

"No," said Yuka.

She could barely say it.

"No. It's not—you didn't—" She shook her head against his shoulder. "It's your hand."

He looked down at his hand. The left one. The two fingers.

"My hand?"

"Your hand," said Yuka. "And your back. And—" She was crying too hard. "I remembered. All of it. Everything. Where they're from." She pressed her face against him. "I remembered everything."

* * *

He did not say anything for a long time.

He held her. Not the way he had been holding her a moment ago. The other way. The way you hold someone who is crying. One hand on the back of her head, the bad one, gently, and his chin on her hair, and the stars above them, and Igris at the edge of the firelight with his back to them, very still, looking out at the moor.

"I know there's stuff missing," he said at last. Very quietly. Into her hair.

She went still.

"I'm not stupid." He was quiet. "Everyone at the house looks at me like I'm somebody else. The elf. The little girl with the list. The doctor. The witches." He breathed out. "And I've got all this." He moved his scarred hand, a little, against her hair. "And I don't know where any of it came from. I just have it. Like I've always had it."

She did not say anything.

"I know there's pieces of me missing," said Mau. "I can feel the shape of them sometimes. Like a tooth that's gone." He paused. "I don't know what they are."

He drew back, a little, so he could see her face.

"It's all right," he said. "We don't have to do anything. Really. Not tonight. Not ever, if you don't want." He wiped her face with his thumb, the good hand. "We can just talk. Will you tell me?"

"Tell you what?"

"Where they're from," he said. "The ones you remember. If you want to."

* * *

So she told him.

She did not tell him everything. She could not; she did not know everything, and some of it was not hers to tell. But she told him about the tower.

Floor by floor. Lying on the blankets under the strange stars with his coat back on and hers round both of them, with her head on his shoulder and his arm round her, she told him.

The guardian on the roof, on ten. The knight on twenty, who was Igris, at the edge of the firelight right now. The bear on thirty, and the collarbone. The hive on forty, and Beru, and the shrine, and his hands burning black. The wyvern on fifty and the barb through his thigh. The shaman, the old dragon, seventy, alone, and the true breath. The Legion. The Grand Marshal. The hundredth floor. The hundred and first, and the Architect, and the thing put into his chest that beat half a step behind his own heart.

He listened to all of it.

He did not interrupt. Sometimes, when she said a thing, his hand moved on her shoulder, very slightly, as if something had touched it from the inside. Once, when she said *the hive*, he went very still, and stayed still for a long time.

She did not tell him about Frieren.

She did not have to.

Because the elf from the east room was in every floor of it. Waiting at the screen. Floating over the bridge. Kneeling in the frost. Crying outside the clinic door. Standing at the edge of the hive with a spell in her hand she was not allowed to fire. You could not tell the story of the tower without her. Yuka tried. She could not.

When she finished, he was quiet for a long time.

"The elf," he said at last.

Yuka did not answer.

"Frieren," said Mau. Slowly. As if trying out a word in a language he did not speak. "She was—" He stopped. "There was something. With us. Wasn't there."

"Yes," said Yuka. Very quietly. "There was."

He looked up at the strange stars for a long time.

"Okay," he said.

That was all. *Okay.* The way the witches said he had said it once, when he owed them two hours instead of one.

And then he turned his head and pressed his mouth, very lightly, against her hair, and did not let go of her.

She understood, then, lying there, that it did not change anything for him. He knew now. He knew he had had something with the elf, before. And it did not matter, to the boy holding her under the stars. That was before. That was a story someone had told him about a stranger.

He was here. With her.

She did not know if that was the most beautiful thing anyone had ever done for her, or the most terrible.

She thought it was probably both.

* * *

He fell asleep before she did.

She lay with her head on his shoulder and listened to both hearts and looked at the stars, and did not sleep, and did not move.

Inside him, Ciel had seen all of it.

She had felt his heart go when he kissed her. She had felt Yuka's hands find the scars. She had heard him say *okay*. She did not say anything. There was nobody in the gate to say it to, and he had asked her to wait, and she had promised.

And she thought, very carefully, the way she thought about everything, about what would happen when they were out. If she told Rimuru. If Rimuru told Frieren. If Frieren—

She stopped thinking about it. Not yet.

Then, very quietly, from the dark under them, from his shadow on the moor, a voice she had never heard speak to her.

Slow. Deep. Like stone moving under water.

*"Little one."*

She went still.

*"Do not be afraid,"* said Kamish. *"I am very old, and I have seen a great many things, and I do not speak often. But I have been listening."* A pause, long as a breath. *"He is still him."*

Yuka shut her eyes.

*"That is the cruel part,"* said the old dragon, very gently. *"Not that something has been taken. That nothing has. Everything he is, is still here. His kindness. His foolishness. His way of standing where the fire is so others need not."* A pause. *"It is only pointed at the wrong story."*

She did not answer. She could not.

*"You stopped,"* said Kamish. *"Tonight. When you felt what he carries. You stopped."* Another long pause. *"Remember that you did. You will need to, later."*

Then the voice was gone, and there was only the wind over the heather, and the knight at the edge of the light, and the boy asleep under her cheek with his scarred hand curled against her shoulder.

She lay there until the strange stars faded, and did not sleep, and remembered that she had stopped.
