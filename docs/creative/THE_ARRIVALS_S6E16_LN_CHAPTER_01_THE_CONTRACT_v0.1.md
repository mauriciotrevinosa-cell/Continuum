## Chapter 13 — The Contract

Mau remembers, from somewhere he can't place, that a loan can be made: one thing, to one person, and his body carries the weight. Without Ciel or Rimuru he builds it clumsily by feel, and the Noise, watching, lets it hold. He lends Yuka the pull. She hates it, then she's good at it. They train every day and fight every guardian together until they move like one person, "inseparable," the shadows say. Igris corrects her footwork, respectfully; Beru calls her "little queen" once and is told off by Igris. Then she asks him the question that matters: the Ten Shadows. She has the technique. *When: Inside the gate. Many days. Four levels: a drowned forest, a city of bells with no people in it, a desert of black glass, a long stair under the sea. Outside, nobody inside can know how long.*

They did not talk about it.

They did not pretend it had not happened, either. In the morning, on the moor, he woke with her head on his shoulder and lay very still for a long time so as not to wake her, and when she did wake and looked at him, he smiled, the way he smiled at her now, and said, "Morning," and she said, "Morning," and went red, and that was all.

They ate. They packed the blankets. They went to find the guardian.

It was a great grey thing of standing stone that walked across the moor in the night like a hill getting up, and they beat it together at dusk on the fourth day, his pull and her rope and Lantern's weight, and the door to the next level opened in the side of a barrow, and they went down into it, and did not talk about the night under the stars at all.

But she did not sit apart from him any more. And he did not let go of her hand on the stair.

* * *

The loan came to him on the drowned forest.

He did not know where it came from. That was the strange thing. It came into his head the way the knock on her door had come, two and one, the way the word *Ciel* had come: from somewhere he could not reach, already shaped.

*You can lend something. One thing. To one person. And you carry it.*

He knew that. He did not know how he knew.

He did not know who had taught it to him. He had a feeling, very faint, of a small grey boy on a bench, and a girl with a hood writing lines on a sheet, and an old woman with a pencil saying *saw it myself*. Then the feeling went, the way a dream goes when you open your eyes.

"I think I can lend you something," he said.

Yuka looked up from the fire.

* * *

He did it badly.

He had no one to help. He did not know there should have been someone. He sat across the fire from her with his eyes shut and felt for the thing in him that let things go *through*, the thin line from the place behind his eyes, and tried to lay it across the space between them like a string between two cans, and could not, and tried again.

*Let it,* he thought. Down. At the dark under the walls. At the thing that had said *maybe*. *Please. Let it hold.*

He felt something watch him.

He felt it consider.

Then the string was there. Between them. Thin and clumsy and wrong-handed and real.

"What are you lending me?" said Yuka.

"The pull," said Mau.

* * *

She hated it.

She said so, at length, for two days. It felt wrong. It felt like writing with someone else's hand. It felt like a voice that was not hers coming out of her mouth. She put it out in front of her, the small hard knot of drawing-in, the way he showed her, and it went wild and pulled her own feet out from under her and dropped her in the drowned forest's black water up to the waist.

He laughed so hard he had to sit down on a root.

She threw a fish at him.

On the third day she put it exactly where she meant to, behind the drowned guardian's knee, a long pale thing like a heron made of rot, and it went down in the water, and Mau's soldiers had it, and she stood on a root with her hand out and her mouth open.

"I did that," she said.

"You did that," said Mau.

He felt it in his body. The weight. The way your arm feels when you have held something out for a long time for someone else. A dull ache behind the eyes. His.

He did not mind at all.

* * *

They trained every day after that.

In the city of bells, in the empty squares, with the bells ringing on their own in the towers above. In the desert of black glass, at night, when the glass went cold. On the long stair under the sea, with the water held back on either side by something neither of them understood.

He lent her the pull, and then, carefully, other things. The space, a little: the thin shell, the angle. The sky-grab, once, rough, and she nearly folded the desert in half and he made her give it back. She taught him her footwork. The way of moving she had learned at home, long before she ever came to the house, from a grandmother who was old there and young here, that made every step land where the next one wanted to start.

*"Your weight is too far forward, my lady,"* said Igris, from the edge of the training ground, the first time he saw her try a turn. Very formally. *"Permit me."*

He showed her. Slowly. A knight's turn, with the weight back and the blade low. She did it. He bowed.

*"Better,"* said Igris. *"Much better."*

By the time they reached the city of bells, they fought the guardians together without speaking. He would move and she would already be moving. She would throw her rope and his pull would already be there to bend it round. They did not plan it. They did not need to.

*"Little queen,"* said Beru, once, peering up out of the shadow on the black glass after the desert's guardian went down in pieces, as she stood with her hand out and her hood back and her face fierce. *"You fought very well beside my King—"*

*"Beru,"* said Igris.

It was only his name. Said very quietly. With something in it like a door closing.

Beru looked at the knight. At Mau. At Yuka.

*"Forgive me,"* said Beru. *"The little one. I meant the little one."*

He went down into the dark and did not come up again for a day.

Yuka stood on the black glass and looked at the place where he had gone, and did not say anything. Mau did not understand what had happened. He laughed, and said Beru always got titles wrong, and went to look at the guardian's remains.

She did not laugh.

* * *

It was on the stair under the sea that she asked.

They had stopped for the night on a landing. The water stood up on both sides of the stair like walls of green glass, with fish moving in it, and pale things further back, and the stair went down and down between them into the dark.

"Oppa," she said. She had stopped flinching when he answered to it. "The Ten Shadows."

He looked at her.

"You've got a copy," said Yuka. "The Noise made it. From watching me. From watching—" She stopped. "From watching someone, a long time ago, in a fight." She looked at the green water. "And you've got one. Tamed. The dog with the one white ear."

"Ear," said Mau.

"Ear." She almost smiled. "And I've got mine. My dog. The white one." She looked at her hands. "And there's eight more. In both of us. Untamed. Waiting." She was quiet. "And at the bottom, him."

He did not have to ask who.

"I've never been able to," said Yuka. "Not one more. Not since the dog. My grandfather said I wasn't ready. Gojo said—" She stopped again. "Gojo said the rule. You call it. You subdue it. Alone. If anyone helps, it doesn't count. The ritual's void."

"I know the rule," said Mau.

He did not know how he knew it. A memory of a dark field, maybe, and Igris beside him, and a man with bare eyes saying *help voids it*. It came and went.

"So I can't," said Yuka. "Not on my own. I'm not strong enough. And you can't help me, because that voids it." She looked at the water. "And you can't do yours alone either, not safely, because they'd kill you. And I can't help you."

* * *

He thought about it for a long time.

He looked at the string between them. The loan. The thin clumsy wrong-handed line from the place behind his eyes to hers, carrying the pull, the weight of it on his body.

"What if we're not two people?" he said.

She looked at him.

"The rule says alone," said Mau slowly. "One summoner. If anyone else helps, it's void." He held up his hand, where the line was, that only they could feel. "But what if there's a contract? Like this one. What if the ritual looks at us, and because of this, it doesn't see two people?" He paused. "It sees one."

Yuka stared at him.

"That's—" she said. "That's not—"

"Is it?"

She did not answer.

She looked at the line between them. At the boy across the landing, with the green water standing up behind him and fish moving in it, and his scarred hand up between them, and his face serious and hopeful at the same time.

"Gojo would kill you," she said.

"Gojo's not here," said Mau. He grinned. "Nobody's here. That's sort of the point."

From the dark of his shadow on the stone landing, a slow deep voice, like stone moving under water:

*"It is a very foolish idea,"* said Kamish.

They both jumped.

*"I have seen many foolish ideas,"* said the old dragon. *"Some of them worked."* A pause. *"Try it with the smallest one first."*
