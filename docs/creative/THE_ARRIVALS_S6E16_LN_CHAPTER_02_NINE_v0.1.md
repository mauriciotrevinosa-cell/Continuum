## Chapter 14 — Nine

*When: Inside the gate. Many days. A forest of white trees with no leaves, a canyon full of wind, a field of black rain, a marsh under a green moon. Somewhere outside, a house is waiting.*

They started with the rabbit.

Kamish had said the smallest one first, and the old dragon was rarely wrong about anything. So on the first night in the forest of white trees, at the bottom of the canyon of wind, with the guardian of the level not yet found and their scouts out hunting it, they cleared a circle in the pale leaf-litter and stood in it.

Yuka in the middle. Mau a pace behind her, inside the circle. The string between them, the loan, thin and clumsy and real.

"If it doesn't work," said Yuka, "it's void, and it goes back down angry, and I don't get to try again for a long time."

"I know."

"And if it doesn't work, you'll have helped, and that's—"

"I know," said Mau. "Try."

She put her hands together.

* * *

The shadow under her feet went deep.

Not her shadow; the other one, the one under the one everybody had. She opened it the way her grandmother had told her, a long time ago, in a world where her grandmother was old. She said the words.

Something came up.

Small. White. Many of them. Rabbits, a dozen, two dozen, pouring up out of the dark at her feet like water out of a spring, and they did not look at her the way her dog looked at her. They looked at her the way wild things look at you before they bolt. And then they bolted. All at once, in every direction, round her and past her and over her feet, and one of them went straight for her face with its teeth bared.

Mau's hand went out.

The space. A shell, the size of a face, a finger's width from hers.

The rabbit hit it and bounced off.

And the notice came up.

They both saw it. He saw it in his window; she saw it in the air in front of her, pale, in the tower's script, the way the tower's notices had always come for him.

*Ritual in progress.*

*Summoner: 1.*

They looked at each other.

"*One*," said Yuka.

"One," said Mau.

The rabbits had stopped, all of them, at the edge of the circle, as if the notice had frightened them too.

* * *

She tamed them.

It took an hour. It was the hardest hour she had ever spent. Rabbits are not strong, but there were so many of them, and they did not want to be anybody's, and they were fast. She caught them one at a time with her hands and her rope and the pull he had lent her, and pinned them, and held them, and did not let go, the way her dog had needed her not to let go, a long time ago, when she was small.

He did not help.

He stood inside the circle a pace behind her and watched, and twice put the space between her and a rabbit going for her eyes, and once, when four of them went for her ankle at once and she went down, he struck: the Ruler's grip, the smallest sliver, round all four, and they flew apart and came back slower.

Each time, the notice did not change.

*Summoner: 1.*

When the last rabbit stopped fighting and lay still in her hands, and then in the circle, and then sank down into her shadow and was gone, and she felt it there, small and white and many and hers, the notice changed at last.

*Ritual complete.*

*Rabbit Escape: tamed.*

She sat down in the white leaf-litter, all at once.

"It worked," she said. In a very small voice.

"It worked," said Mau.

From his shadow, peering up over the edge of the circle, two cold blue lights.

*"It is a very foolish idea,"* said Kamish's slow voice from somewhere much deeper. *"And it worked."* A pause. *"I have seen that before. Not often."*

* * *

After that, they learned the rules.

Not all at once. One ritual at a time, over many days, the way the house had learned everything: by trying, and writing it down, and arguing about it at the fire. Yuka kept a book. She had bought it from the shop with the house's crystals and written *receipt: one book* in Umaru's receipt book, very small, and every night she wrote the rules in it in her careful hand.

*1. With the contract, the gate sees us as one. (Summoner: 1.)*

*2. Inside the circle, he can hit it. It doesn't void. (Tested: rabbits, Nue.)*

*3. He can't take over. If he does the subduing, it voids. (Tested: nearly, with the Toad. Didn't. Close.)*

*4. If I'm in real danger, he can step all the way in. It still counts. (Tested: the Serpent. I don't want to talk about the Serpent.)*

*5. Same for him. Same rules. I coach. I lend him what I know.*

*6. Nobody else. (Igris asked. Igris was very polite about it. No.)*

* * *

They did his too.

That was the part she was proudest of, later. Not her own nine. His.

He had the copy. The Noise had built it from watching, a long time ago, and it was rough and strange and wrong-handed, the way all his copies were, and the shikigami in it were not like hers. They were his. They came up out of his shadow looking the way he looked at things: a little sideways, a little smeared at the edges where the world could not decide where they ended, like Ear.

She stood a pace behind him inside the circle and told him what she knew.

"Nue's fast in the air but it can't turn tight. Go under it. Under. *Under*, Oppa—"

"The Toad's tongue comes from the left. Always the left. It's stupid. Use that."

"Don't look the Ox in the eye. Don't. It charges what it looks at. Look at its feet."

He listened. He did what she said. Sometimes he did something else, something he made up on the spot, the way he had made up the bell-shell on the field, and twice that worked better than what she said, and she was annoyed both times, and wrote it in the book anyway.

And she lent him things back. Not through the string; the string only went one way, from him to her. Through her mouth. Through her footwork. Through the rope she threw across a circle in a canyon full of wind, to catch a Great Serpent's tail so he could get his arms round its head.

*Summoner: 1.*

Every time.

* * *

Round Deer came on the field of black rain.

They were both hurt by then. The Ox had caught her hip, and the Serpent had nearly had his arm, and the wind in the canyon had opened the scars on his back like old seams. They called the deer half hoping it would kill them quickly.

It did not try to kill them.

It stood in the black rain, pale, with its antlers like branches of light, and looked at them. And when Yuka had subdued it, which took a very long time because it would not fight, only walk away, again and again, until she ran after it and caught it by the neck and held on, it turned its head in her arms and breathed on her hip.

The pain went out of it.

She stared at it.

Then it turned, and breathed on Mau's back, and the opened scars closed.

"*Oh*," said Yuka.

Mau's Round Deer, tamed an hour later, was smaller than hers, and smeared at the edges, and its antlers flickered. But when it breathed on her, the ache in her ribs from the black rain went too.

They sat in the black rain and laughed until they could not breathe, because they could heal themselves now, and they were going to need it.

* * *

Agito came last of the nine.

In the old stories, Yuka said, in her world, Agito was not a shikigami on its own. It was something you made by putting others together. A fusion. You could not tame it; you built it out of what you had.

It came up out of her shadow on the marsh under the green moon whole, by itself. A tall thing, armoured, with a face like a mask and too many arms, and it looked at her the way all the others had, and it was not made of anything else. It was its own.

"That's not right," said Yuka. "That's not how—"

"Maybe it is here," said Mau.

She looked at him.

"Everything's different here," he said. "Isn't it?"

She tamed it. He tamed his. It was the hardest of the nine by far, and when it was over they lay in the marsh mud under the green moon side by side with Round Deer breathing on both of them, and did not get up for a long time.

* * *

That night, Yuka opened the book and wrote the list.

*Mine: the dog (white). Rabbit Escape. Nue. Great Serpent. Toad. Max Elephant. Round Deer. Piercing Ox. Tiger Funeral. Agito.*

*His: Ear. Rabbit Escape. Nue. Great Serpent. Toad. Max Elephant. Round Deer. Piercing Ox. Tiger Funeral. Agito.*

She looked at it for a long time.

Then, under it, she wrote the last line, and drew a box round it, the way Umaru drew boxes round things on her sheets.

*Last: him.*

She did not have to write his name.

They could both feel him. Every night. Under all the others, under the dogs and the deer and the bird and the serpent and Agito, at the very bottom of the dark, something enormous and still, waiting, with eight spokes turning very slowly over its head.

"Last," said Yuka. Very quietly.

Across the fire, Mau was looking at the green moon.

"Last," he said.

Below them both, in the dark, the wheel turned over once, slowly, like something hearing its name.
