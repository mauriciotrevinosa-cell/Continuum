*When: Inside the gate, the last level. A plain of white stone to every horizon, flat as a table, under a sky with no colour at all. Nothing alive. No wind. Two people, a fire, and the shadows.*

There was nothing on the last level.

No ruin, no forest, no stair. A white stone plain, flat to every horizon, and a sky that was not grey or blue or any colour, just *sky*, and silence. The scouts went out across it in fours and came back with nothing. No guardian. No walls. No door.

"It's waiting," said Mau.

"For what?"

He looked at her.

She knew. She had known since the green moon. She could feel him, at the bottom of everything, under the nine, enormous and still, with his eight spokes.

"For us," said Yuka.

* * *

They did not hurry.

There was nothing to hurry for. Nobody to protect. Nobody waiting on a step. Nobody who could be hurt by anything they did on the white plain except themselves, and they had a deer that breathed scars closed now.

They sat by the fire for a whole day and made a plan.

It was a stupid plan, Yuka said. It was simple, and stupid plans were often simple, and simple plans were often the ones that worked. Kamish agreed with her about that, from the dark, in his slow voice.

"He adapts," said Yuka. "That's all he does. You hit him once, the wheel turns, and he learns it. Whatever you hit him with, it won't work twice." She drew the wheel in the white dust with her finger: eight spokes. "So you don't hit him twice. You hit him once, with something so big he doesn't get to learn it."

"Kamish," said Mau.

*"I am listening,"* said Kamish.

"Your breath. The real one. The one you held back on seventy." He paused. "Can you hold it for a while? Build it?"

The old dragon was silent for a long moment.

*"I can build it,"* he said. *"It takes time. While I build it, I am a very large thing standing still."*

"Then something has to make him look somewhere else," said Yuka.

Mau looked at the edge of the firelight, where the knight stood.

*"My liege,"* said Igris, before he could ask. *"It would be my honour."*

"And something has to stand in front of whoever called him," said Yuka. "Because the first thing he'll do is come for the summoner."

From the shadow by her feet, two cold blue lights peered up.

*"Little rope,"* said Beru. Very quietly. Very steadily. *"I will stand in front of you."*

She looked down at him.

*"I stood in front of no one, once,"* said Beru. *"In a hive. I ate everything that came to me. Then a king held me while I ate him, and did not let go."* The blue lights did not waver. *"I have learned to stand in front of people. I am good at it now."*

* * *

"They're mine," said Mau. "They come when I call. Not you." He looked at the string between them. "Unless I lend them."

"Can you?"

"I don't know."

He tried.

Not the pull, this time. Not the space. Something bigger and stranger. He felt for the line that ran from him to everything that was his, the dark branch, the cup, the knight and the ant and the old dragon lying in it, and he took three threads of it, very carefully, and laid them along the string to her.

He felt the Noise watch.

He felt it consider, for a long time.

Then he felt it let go.

Yuka gasped.

"*Oh*," she said. She had her hand flat on her chest. "They're—I can feel them. Oppa, I can *feel* them."

*"My lady,"* said Igris, from the edge of the light, and bowed to her, deeply.

*"Little rope,"* said Beru.

*"Small king's little one,"* said Kamish, slowly, as if trying it out, and something in his deep voice sounded almost amused.

* * *

He hung the Purple last.

Not to fire. To hold. Blue in his left hand, red in his right, and he brought them together slowly, slowly, until they touched, and then he did not let them go. He held it in the air above the white plain, a ball of something that was not light and not dark, humming, enormous, the colour of a bruise on the inside of the world, and his arms shook with it.

"That's in case," he said. Through his teeth. "And to look at. He'll look at it."

"How long can you hold that?"

"Not long. Go."

* * *

She called him first.

She stood on the white stone with her hands together and opened the deepest part of the shadow under her shadow, the bottom of it, where he had been waiting all this time, and said the words her grandmother had told her never, ever to say.

The plain went dark.

He came up out of the stone at her feet the way the old dragon did everything: slowly. Huge. Pale. Muscled like something carved out of a mountain. Wings on his head that were not wings. A sword where his right arm should be. And over his head, turning, very slowly, the wheel. Eight spokes. Pale light.

He looked at her.

*Ritual in progress.*

*Summoner: 1.*

He moved.

Straight at her. The way Yuka had said he would. Not at the dragon. Not at the knight. At the one who had called him, faster than anything that big had any right to be—

Beru was there.

Black chitin and long arms and the antennae laid back, between Yuka and the sword, not attacking, not eating, just standing, and the sword came down on him and he took it on both crossed forearms and his knees cracked the white stone and he did not move.

*"Not her,"* said Beru, very quietly. *"Not today."*

The wheel turned. One spoke.

"*Igris*," said Yuka.

The knight came in from the left with his long sword going round, and the great pale head turned toward him, and away from Yuka, and up, for one heartbeat, at the humming purple thing hanging in the colourless sky over all of them.

One heartbeat.

"*Kamish*," said Yuka. "*Now.*"

* * *

The old dragon breathed.

Not the tide. The sun. The true breath, the one he had held back on seventy, built for a long minute while a knight and an ant king and a ball of purple bought the time for it. It came out of his great dark throat white, and went across the white plain, and took the pale shape at Yuka's feet, all of it, the sword and the wings that were not wings and the wheel, before the wheel could turn a second time.

When the white went out, there was nothing on the stone.

Then something sank into Yuka's shadow. Slowly. Something enormous and still. And over her head, for a moment, faint, turning: eight spokes.

*Ritual complete.*

She sat down on the white stone, all at once.

* * *

He did his a minute later.

He let the Purple go first, up, into the colourless sky, where it made no sound and left a round black hole with nothing on the other side of it. Then he called.

His was smaller. Smeared at the edges, the way all his were. Its wheel flickered. But it came straight at him the way hers had come at her, and Beru was in front of him, and Igris went in from the left, and it looked up, one heartbeat, at the hole in the sky where the Purple had gone.

Kamish had built his breath again. Faster this time.

*Ritual complete.*

Yuka, on the white stone with her own wheel turning faintly over her head, had counted the whole time, under her breath, the way Ori counted at the reset hour.

"Two minutes," she said. Her voice came out cracked. "Both of them. Two minutes."

* * *

They laughed.

He did not know who started. One of them made a sound and then the other did and then they were both on the white stone, on their backs, under a sky with a round black hole in it and nothing on the other side, laughing until they could not breathe, until it hurt, until Yuka was crying and he was crying and Round Deer came up out of both their shadows at once, worried, and breathed on them.

"Do you know," said Yuka, when she could talk. She was lying with her head near his, looking up. "Do you know how many people have done that? Ever?"

"No."

"One," said Yuka. "Before today. In the whole history of the Ten Shadows. One. And it was *Sukuna*." She started laughing again. "So we're the second and third. And if you leave him out, because he cheated, he was wearing someone else, then we're the *first*. The first ever. With the technique."

Mau turned his head on the stone and looked at her.

"You're the first," he said.

"We both—"

"Mine's a copy," said Mau. "The Noise made it from watching. It's not mine. Not really." He smiled at her. "You're the first. The first one ever. Yuka."

She stared at him.

He leaned over, on the white stone, and kissed her. Quickly. The way you take something you are not sure you are allowed.

* * *

She wanted to say something.

He could see it. She opened her mouth and her face did something complicated, and he knew, the way he was beginning to know things about her, that what she was going to say was *Oppa, we can't*, or *Oppa, you don't know*, or *Oppa, there's someone*.

He said it first.

"I know," said Mau.

She stopped.

He sat up. He sat on the white stone with his arms round his knees and looked out across the plain at nothing, at the colourless horizon, and was quiet for a long time.

"I know I had a past," he said. "With someone. Before." He did not look at her. "You told me. In the tower story. The elf. Frieren." He said the name carefully. "I know there was something."

Yuka did not move.

"And it's—" He stopped. "Since you told me. Things come back. Sometimes." He looked at his hands. "Not much. Pieces. A cold foot. On my leg. At night. Someone saying *one*." He swallowed. "A bench. Somewhere cold. Three people standing in front of me so I couldn't go up a stair. Somebody writing *together* on a board." He shook his head. "I don't know what they are. They come and they go."

The wheel over Yuka's head had faded. Over his, very faintly, it turned once.

"And I don't know what would happen," said Mau, "if they all came back."

He turned his head and looked at her at last.

"I don't want to remember," he said.

She went very still.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I know that's—I know it's wrong. I know there's someone at that house who—" He stopped. "But I'm so happy. Here. With you. I've never been this happy. I don't think I have. I don't *remember* being this happy." He laughed, a little, and it cracked in the middle. "And I'm scared that if I remember, I'll lose this. I'll lose you. I'll turn into someone who doesn't—" He stopped again. "I don't want to lose this."

* * *

Yuka did not say anything.

She sat on the white stone beside him, with her knees up, with the place where her wheel had turned still warm over her head, and looked at the colourless sky with the round black hole in it.

She thought about the cold foot. About who it belonged to. About the word *one*, said every night at the reset hour in the east room, through the wall, for a year and a half.

She thought: *he's starting to remember. And he's choosing not to.*

She thought: *and he's choosing me.*

She did not know which of those was the worst thing she had ever heard, or the best.

She put her head on his shoulder.

"Okay," she said.

Very quietly. The way he said it. The way the witches said he had once said it about owing them two hours.

And under them, in the dark, in both their shadows, two great still shapes lay with their wheels slowly turning, and an old dragon lay awake beside them, and did not say anything at all.
