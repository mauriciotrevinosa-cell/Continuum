*When: Inside the gate, the last level, the next day. The round black hole in the colourless sky has closed. Something under the white stone has been moving all night.*

It came up at noon.

Or what passed for noon. The colourless light was brightest, and the white plain was very still, and then the stone in the middle of it, a long way off, cracked.

He felt it through the scouts first. Then through his feet.

Then he saw it.

A giant. White stone, like the plain. It came up out of the ground the way a man stands up out of a bath: slowly, streaming dust, all shoulders and knees, until it stood taller than the tower on the first level, faceless, with its long arms hanging. Where its feet touched the plain, the plain was not there any more. Not broken. *Gone*, the way things were gone where the Purple went.

*Guardian: the Lock.*

*Defeat it to open the gate.*

"That's the door," said Yuka. "That's the actual door."

"Yeah."

"It unmakes things."

"Yeah," said Mau. "I saw something like it once. On a hundred and one." He did not know how he knew that. A grey long thing, smiling, drawing lines through the world. It came and went. "Don't let it touch you."

* * *

"Wait," he said.

She looked at him.

The giant was a long way off. It was walking toward them, slowly, across the white, with the plain vanishing under each step.

"There's a thing I know," said Mau. "That you don't. About the wheel."

"I know about the wheel—"

"You know about *him*," said Mau. "In the stories. I've had a piece of the wheel in me all summer. Since a fight in a field, a long time ago. It turned four times." He paused. "I didn't know that until you told me about the tower. But I can feel it. The notches. I know how it turns."

She was quiet.

"Tell me," she said.

* * *

He told her. Sitting on the white stone, with the giant walking toward them, slow, a long way off.

"It works two ways," said Mau. "He gets hit, and the wheel turns, and *he* adapts. That's the first way. That's what everybody knows. That's what makes him unbeatable." He drew the eight spokes in the dust with his finger, the way she had the day before. "But now he's yours. And you've got a wheel too. Over you. Like the one I've got." He touched the dust. "So the second way: when he adapts, *your* wheel can turn with his. You can learn it too."

Yuka stared at the spokes.

"So here's what you do," said Mau. "You don't keep him out. You don't fight with him the whole time. He's too big, and if he goes down all the way, you lose him." He tapped the dust. "You call him. You let him take the hit. Whatever it is. Fire. A blade. That thing's hands. He takes it, and you don't let it finish him. Even one piece of him left is enough. He adapts." He paused. "And then you send him back. Fast. Before it hits him again."

"And then?"

"And then your wheel and his sync up," said Mau. "And you know what he knows. You can stand in it. And you put him away, and you use the others. The dogs. Agito. The deer, to heal." He smiled a little. "Or my things, if we've got a contract running."

She looked at the dust for a long time.

"Think about it," said Mau. "Every time. Before you call him. What do you want him to learn? What can he take without going down? Then call him." He looked up at the giant. "Not before."

"Where did you learn that?" said Yuka quietly.

He thought about it.

"I don't know," he said. "It's just true. Isn't it?"

* * *

They did it.

The giant reached them in the middle of the afternoon. It lifted one long white arm and brought its hand down on the plain where they were standing, and they were not standing there: he had stepped, through nothing, the way she did, and taken her with him.

Where the hand came down, the plain was gone. A hole with no bottom.

"Now," said Mau.

She called him.

He came up between them and the giant, pale and huge, with his wheel turning over his head, and the giant's hand came down again, and Mahoraga took it.

On his crossed arms. The way Beru had taken the sword.

It unmade him. Not all at once. From the hands in. His arms, his shoulders, the pale stuff of him going to nothing where the white hand pressed—

"*Back*," said Yuka.

He went. Down into her shadow, half of him gone. Fast.

Over her head, the wheel turned.

*Click.*

Over his, a heartbeat later, as his own Mahoraga came up and took the next blow and went back down: *click*.

Yuka stood on the white stone and felt it. The shape of what the giant did. Not a blow. A line, a drawing-through, the world being told *not here*. She felt her wheel learn it. She felt the next time it would not quite work.

"*Oh*," she said.

* * *

After that it was not a fight. It was a lesson, and they were the ones teaching.

The giant reached for them and the reaching did not quite arrive. Not stopped, the way his shell stopped things: *adapted*, the white hands closing on air a finger's width short of where they should have closed, the way the claws on twenty had always been a finger's width short.

"Dogs," said Yuka.

Her white dog came up out of her shadow, and Ear out of his, and they went for the giant's ankles from opposite sides, two shadows, one with a white ear up, and the giant turned to find them and the plain under its turning feet went to nothing and it staggered.

"Agito," said Mau.

It came up. Both of them, his and hers. Not a fusion, not something built from the others. Its own: armoured, too many arms, a face like a mask. It went up the giant's back like a man climbing a wall and its many hands found the place at the back of the stone neck where the white was thinnest.

"Kamish," said Yuka. She did not need to say anything else.

The old dragon had been building since the giant stood up.

* * *

The breath went across the white plain like the sun coming up.

The giant stood in it for one long moment, white in white, and then it was not standing. It was going. From the neck down, the way it had made the plain go: the whole tall shape of it coming apart into dust and then less than dust, until there was nothing in the middle of the plain but a hole with no bottom, and a wind, for the first time on the last level, rushing into it.

*Guardian: the Lock. Defeated.*

*Level cleared.*

*All levels cleared.*

*The gate will open.*

* * *

She felt it then.

In the quiet afterward, with the wind dying down and Round Deer breathing on both of them and the old dragon lying down again with a sound like a hill settling. In her shadow. Under the dog and the rabbits and the serpent and the deer and the great still shape with its wheel.

Something new.

Small. Black. Curled up. Asleep.

She had not called it. She had never seen it before. But she knew what it was at once, the way she had known her white dog the first day the technique woke in her: not a stranger. *Hers.*

The other dog. The black one.

The technique gives them together. Everyone with the Ten Shadows starts with both: a white dog and a black, the pair, the first thing it hands you. Her grandmother had told her so, a long time ago, in a world where her grandmother was old. And Yuka had only ever had one. She had always thought something was wrong with her. That she had been given half.

She had not been given half. The other had been there the whole time. Asleep, behind a door in her shadow that had never once opened.

It was open now.

"Oppa," she said. Very quietly. "Something's in my shadow. Something I've always had, and never had."

He looked at her. Then down at her shadow on the white stone.

He frowned.

Down at the bottom of his walls, very faintly, he felt something that had said *maybe* to him in a frosted field. Watching. Pleased with itself. Quiet.

"I think it came through the contract," he said slowly. "The thing in me. The one that watches. It was in the string the whole time." He looked at her. "It must have reached you. And left that."

"It's mine," said Yuka. Her voice shook. "Not to tame. It was always mine. It was just asleep." She looked at him. "Something opened the door."

He looked at the small black shape in her shadow that only she could feel.

"Then wake it up when you want," he said. "It's been waiting long enough."

She put her hand flat on the stone over it, gently, the way you put your hand on a sleeping animal.

Under her palm, in the dark, the black dog stirred, and lifted its head, and knew her.

* * *

Then they took everything.

The gate was full of it. That was the other thing about a cleared gate: everything it held came loose. Crystals, piles of them, in the ruins of every level, in the bell towers, in the black glass. Bodies of the guardians they had beaten, the ice archer, the stone walker, the drowned heron, the things in the desert, all lying where they had fallen, not yet raised. Weapons. A bow made of ice that did not melt.

Mau called them up.

All of them but Tank, still in pieces from the field. The soldiers, ninety-eight, the two places in the last row empty. Corner. Roof. Tusk. Kaisel. Bellion. Lantern. And sent them out across every level he could reach, and they came back loaded, carrying, dragging, crystals in their arms and on their shields, bodies over their shoulders, the bow, everything.

Yuka watched them come back in rows across the white plain and laughed.

"Umaru's going to need a bigger book," she said.

* * *

The gate was opening.

They could feel it. A pressure in the colourless air, the way the frame felt before it took you. Somewhere in the middle of the white plain, where the giant had stood and the hole was, a light was coming up. Red, then less red. Then the grey-white of a transfer.

Home.

They stood side by side on the white stone and looked at it.

He took her hand.

She let him.

"Oppa," she said.

"Mm."

She did not look at him. She looked at the light.

"When we get back," said Yuka. "We hide it. All of it." Her hand tightened on his. "What we did. In here. The—" She stopped. "All of it. We don't tell anyone."

He looked at her.

"We talk about it," said Yuka, "only when it's you and me." She swallowed. "Nobody else. Not anyone. Only us."

He did not ask why. He thought he knew. He did not know that he was wrong about why.

"Okay," he said.

She held his hand very tightly, and they walked toward the light together, and behind them, in rows, his shadows followed, carrying everything.

In his shadow, the old dragon said nothing.

In hers, a small black dog lay curled beside a white one, awake now, for the first time.
