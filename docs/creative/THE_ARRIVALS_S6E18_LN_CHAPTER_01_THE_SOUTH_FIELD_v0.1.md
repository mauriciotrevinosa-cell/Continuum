*When: Day 447, evening. A day and a half since Anko wrote "One hour. (He said.)" on the board. The paper lanterns have been taken down from the long room. Nobody has eaten properly since yesterday.*

The hour had become a day and a half.

Anko had known at the end of the first hour. She had been standing at the board with her watch open and her pencil behind her ear, the way she stood every time anyone went in, and the hour had come and gone, and the frame had stayed dark.

At the end of the second hour, she had gone and got Yuta and Maki.

They had not left since.

* * *

They sat on the bench in front of the frame, the two of them, the way they had sat on the back step most evenings that autumn, with a blanket over both their knees and their shoulders touching. They did not talk much. Maki had her spear across her lap. Yuta had his sword. Neither of them looked away from the dark frame for long.

At dawn, Mai had come out of the house with three cups of something hot and given them one each and kept one, and sat down on the end of the bench, and stayed.

"She's eighteen," Maki had said, at some point in the night. To nobody. "She's eighteen, and she's in a Red Gate, and he took her in there, and he doesn't—"

She had not finished.

Yuta had put his hand over hers on the spear.

"He'll bring her back," he had said. "Whatever he remembers. Whatever he doesn't." He looked at the frame. "That part of him didn't go anywhere. You saw him in the field."

Maki had not answered.

She had not let go of the spear either.

* * *

The frame lit at dusk.

Grey-white. The ordinary light. Not red.

Maki was on her feet before it finished glowing. Yuta was beside her. Mai stood up so fast her cup went over in the frost. Across the yard, Anko had already turned round from the board with her watch in her hand.

Shadows came out.

Not Mau. Not Yuka. Shadows. A whole pack of them, coming out of the frame in rows, faceless, dark, and every single one of them was carrying something. Soldiers with their arms full of crystals, the rough pale dungeon crystals the house traded with, so many that they spilled and rolled across the dry ground in front of the frame like hail. Soldiers with things over their shoulders, long things, pale things, bodies: a tall frost-thin archer with a bow in its dead hand, a heron made of rot, a great grey thing of standing stone that took four of them to carry. Lantern, with a bow made of ice in his claws that steamed in the evening air and did not melt.

They kept coming.

They went past Maki and Yuta and Mai in their rows and laid everything down in the yard, carefully, in heaps, the way the soldiers laid down the hurt in the field, and went back for more.

By the time the last of them came out, half the house was in the yard, staring at the heaps.

"*What*," said Umaru, from the kitchen door, faintly, "*is all of that*."

* * *

Then they came out.

Together. Through the grey-white light, side by side, and Maki saw their hands, for one moment, just as they stepped through: holding. Then not. They let go on the last step, so smoothly that nobody who had not been looking for it would have seen.

Maki had been looking for it.

They were talking. That was the first thing anyone heard. Not *we're back*, not *sorry*, not anything about a day and a half. Mid-sentence. Yuka, with her hood back and her face fierce and bright, in a long grey coat nobody had ever seen, saying:

"—no, you went *under* it, I *told* you to go under it, that's why it worked—"

"I went *round* it," said Mau. "Under would have been stupid. It was a *serpent*—"

"Under was in the *book*—"

They both stopped.

They had seen the yard. The heaps. The house standing in the frost, staring at them. Yuta and Maki in front of the frame with their weapons in their hands and their faces doing something Yuka had never seen them do.

Yuka's face changed.

"Grandma," she said. "Grandpa."

Maki did not say anything at all.

She crossed the dry ground and put her arms round her granddaughter, coat and all, and held on, hard, with her face against Yuka's hair, and did not let go for a long time. Yuta stood behind her with his hand on Maki's back and looked over her shoulder at Mau.

Mau looked back.

"Sorry," said Mau. "It was a Red Gate. It wasn't supposed to be. It was supposed to be the garden." He paused. "I brought her back."

"Yes," said Yuta. Very quietly. "You did."

* * *

Yuka pulled back out of her grandmother's arms.

She was not crying. She was eighteen, and she had stood in front of a Mahoraga with Beru in front of her and Kamish building the sun behind her, and she was not crying. Her face was bright, and fierce, and something else under that, something that Maki saw and did not have a name for.

"You won't believe it," said Yuka.

"Believe what?"

"What we did." She looked at Mau. He grinned. "We were going to do it at the debrief. Properly. But—" She looked at the house, the whole house, standing in the yard round the heaps of crystals and bodies. "Come to the south field. All of you. Now. Before anyone asks anything."

"Yuka—" began Shoko.

"*Please*," said Yuka.

* * *

They went to the south field.

All of them. The whole house, in the dusk, across the frosted grass, past the stack of twisted oak, to the south field where Eren and Mikasa went to see the yellow flowers, the second flowering long since over now. They stood along the edge of it in their coats with their breath going up white, and in the middle of the field, Mau and Yuka stood side by side, a few paces apart.

They had planned it. That was clear at once. They stood the way you stand when you have practised something.

Frieren stood at the very edge of the field. A little apart from the others. With Fern beside her, and Ori beside Fern, holding her hand.

She was watching Mau.

He did not look at her.

* * *

"Dogs," said Yuka.

They put their hands together, at the same moment, in the same shape.

Out of Mau's shadow came Ear. Black, the size of a pony, smeared a little at the edges where the world could not decide where it ended. One black ear flat. One white, and up.

Out of Yuka's came her white dog. Clean and pale and close at her side, the way it always was.

And then, beside the white dog, out of Yuka's shadow, something else.

Black. Smaller than Ear. Clean-edged, like the white one. It came up out of her shadow at the same moment as the white, side by side, the pair, and sat down in the frost at her other side, and looked up at her the way the white one always had.

"Yuka," said Maki. Very strangely. "Yuka. You've got *two*."

"I always had two," said Yuka. She put her hand on the black dog's head. It leaned into it. "The technique gives you both. I only ever had one, and I thought I'd been given half." She looked at her grandmother. "He was asleep. Something opened the door."

Maki stared at her.

* * *

Then the others.

One by one. Side by side. The same shape of hands, at the same moment, and out of both their shadows, together, the same thing.

Rabbits. A flood of them, white, pouring out across the frosted grass round their feet, and Ichika, at the edge of the field, shrieked with delight.

Nue. Two great birds, wings wide, going up into the dusk with lightning crackling along their feathers.

The Great Serpent. Two of them, rising out of the ground like trees, enormous, swaying, and somebody in the house said a word that Seiko would have slapped them for, and Seiko did not slap anyone, because she was staring too.

The Toad. Max Elephant, and water pouring off its trunk onto the field. Round Deer, pale, with antlers of light, and it walked to the edge of the field and breathed on Taihei's hands, scarred from a year of stoves, and Taihei looked down at them and did not say anything at all.

The Piercing Ox. Tiger Funeral.

Agito. Two of them, armoured, too many arms, each its own, side by side in the dusk.

Nine. Each. Side by side.

Then Yuka and Mau lowered their hands, at the same moment, and every shadow on the south field went down into the frost, and the field was empty again except for the two of them.

* * *

The house stood along the edge of the field and did not say anything.

Gojo had his blindfold up. He had had it up since the dogs. He was looking at the two of them in the middle of the field with an expression nobody in the house had ever seen on his face, not on the hundred and first floor, not in the clinic looking at the root.

"Nine," he said. Very quietly. "Each. *Each.*" He did not look away from them. "That's not possible. You can't. The rule—"

"We found a way round the rule," said Yuka.

"Nobody finds a way round that rule."

"We did," said Mau. He grinned. "It's in her book. You can read it."

Gojo opened his mouth. Then he shut it again.

Along the edge of the field, people were starting to move. To breathe. Somebody laughed, shakily. Ichika was asking if the rabbits could come back. Umaru had her chalk out and was writing on the back of her hand because she had no sheet. It was over; that was what everyone thought, Maki thought it, Yuta thought it, the whole house thought it. Nine each. Impossible. Over.

Then Mau looked at Yuka.

And Yuka looked at Mau.

And they both, at the same moment, smiled.

* * *

They put their hands together.

Not the same shape as before.

Gojo saw the shape, and all the colour went out of his face at once.

"*No*," he said. "*No, no, don't, you can't call him, you'll*—"

The south field went dark.

Behind Yuka, out of her shadow, something rose. Slowly. Huge and pale, muscled like something carved from a mountain, with wings on its head that were not wings and a sword where its arm should be. And over its head, turning, very slowly: a wheel of pale light. Eight spokes.

Behind Mau, out of his: the same. Smaller. Smeared at the edges, the way all his things were. Its wheel flickered. But it was there, and it was his.

Two Mahoragas. Standing in the south field in the dusk, behind two people, with their wheels turning over their heads.

They did not move. They did not attack. They stood behind their summoners, the way Igris stood behind Mau at the north end, waiting.

Nobody along the edge of the field breathed.

* * *

Mau looked at Yuka.

Yuka looked at Mau.

It had worked. All of it. The plan they had made by the fire, the routine they had practised on the white plain, the order, the timing, the moment when everyone thought it was over. It had worked exactly the way they had planned it.

She started laughing first.

Then he did. In the middle of the south field, in the frost, in the dusk, with two Mahoragas standing silent behind them and their wheels turning and the whole house standing at the edge of the field in total silence, the two of them looked at each other and laughed, helplessly, the way you laugh when something impossible has gone exactly right.

At the very edge of the field, a little apart from the others, Frieren stood with Ori's hand in hers.

She watched them laugh.

She did not say anything. She did not move. Her face was perfectly still.

Ori, beside her, looked up at her face.

Then Ori looked back at the middle of the field, at the boy laughing with Yuka under a wheel of pale light. And held Frieren's hand harder.
