# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 33
## Chapter 19 — The Things in the Yard

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Codex, 2026-10-22)
**Volume:** 33
**Reading order:** 19
**Source episode:** S6E19
**Continuity state:** Day 448, first morning after the return. The recovered bodies, crystals and weapons are still physically in the yard. Umaru and Anko begin an inventory. Mau reconstructs Tank without ceremony, then extracts the ice archer, stone walker and drowned heron; desert remains need further sorting. His displayed named capacity has expanded from twenty to forty. The ninety-eight soldiers remain separate; the two unmade on 101 do not return. Ciel can speak to him; no private romantic information becomes public.

*When: Day 448, early morning. Frost in the yard, kitchen smoke over the eaves. Somebody has put a bucket under the drowned heron. It has not helped.*

The bucket was full.

Anko stood over it with her pencil behind her ear and considered the dark water spreading across the boards underneath. She moved the bucket six inches to the left. Water dripped past it on the right.

"That thing," she said, "is taking the piss."

"It isn't doing anything," said Umaru.

"Then it's very good at it."

Umaru crouched beside the slate she had brought out from the Ministry. She had divided it into four columns before breakfast: crystals, weapons, bodies and *ask*. The fourth was already larger than the other three. Anko had written *bucket* in it.

Beyond the heron lay the stone walker, in sections too large to get through the workshop door, and the tall thin archer, carefully arranged on an old sail. One of its hands still gripped something that was no longer there. Lantern stood beside the ice bow, holding it upright in his claws while the morning sun touched it without melting it.

Mau came out carrying two mugs.

"Which one of you wanted the—"

"Me," said both of them.

He looked into the mugs. One contained tea. The other looked as if someone had washed a brush in it.

"Umaru," he decided, offering her the second.

She took it without looking away from the slate. Anko accepted the tea and looked at him over the rim.

"You slept?"

"Yes."

"You ate?"

"Not yet."

"Then we're sorting, not seeing how much of the yard you can put in your trousers before breakfast."

He glanced down at his shadow, then at the heaps.

"That would be a lot of trousers."

"The Ministry is not buying you bigger ones," said Umaru.

Yuka came through the kitchen door with half a piece of bread in her hand and the other half already in her mouth. Her coat was folded over one arm. A thread hung from its cuff where something on the last floor had caught it.

"Grandma says if anyone raises the heron before she gets back, she's going to be annoyed."

"Why?" asked Mau.

Yuka swallowed. "She wants to see it."

"Your grandmother's got a funny idea of a morning out," said Anko.

* * *

They began with the things that could be counted without touching them.

Weapons on the dry sail. Crystals in the crates Kaneki brought over from the workshop, with folded cloth in the corners so they would stop running through the gaps. The bow on its own, because nobody knew yet whether its cold would damage anything left beside it. Lantern refused to set it down until Anko showed him a padded length of board and asked him to lay it there.

He did it with the care of someone putting a child to bed.

"Not yours, then?" she asked him.

The great claws opened and closed once.

Mau looked at the bow. "You can try it later. Somewhere that isn't here."

Lantern went very still, apparently satisfied.

Umaru labelled the board. Then she crossed out *Lantern's new bow* and wrote *ice bow, carried by Lantern*. Anko pointed at the correction with her pencil.

"That's why we do this before he gets attached."

Beside the larger bodies, they uncovered the things from the desert. Some were long, articulated pieces of dark glass. Others were fragments with sand fused into them. Yuka knelt and turned one over, frowning.

"This was moving when it came at us. But I don't know if these two were one thing or two."

"So don't write two," said Anko.

Umaru changed the entry to *desert remains — unsorted*. Mau helped Yuka separate a hooked length from a coil without letting either scrape her fingers. He knew the movement before she made it; she lifted her hand and his was already supporting the other end.

"Those weren't together," she said.

"No. That came off the thing under the glass."

Anko looked from one to the other, then bent to see what they were holding. "Can either of you tell me which bit had a mouth?"

They considered it.

"It wasn't a very helpful mouth," said Mau.

Yuka laughed into her bread.

They put the two pieces in separate trays.

* * *

Tank was easier.

Mau had not expected that, or rather he had expected the old difficulty without knowing why: a great weight to haul through a small opening, his breath trapped somewhere under it. He stood at the edge of the dry boards, where the yard gave way to bare earth, and felt for the bear.

There he was. Broken, stored. Still his.

*Reconstruction,* said Ciel, quietly. *The cup, not the other two. I'll watch.*

He nodded before remembering that nobody else had heard her.

"Tank," he said to the others. "I'm going to put him back together."

Anko moved the slate away from the patch of ground he was looking at.

The shape was already there in his mind: shoulders, back, the tremendous lowered head. He held it, and let what was missing find its places.

Something drew from the cup. He felt the level fall, distinctly, without the old struggle to keep the pieces aligned. The dark at his feet thickened, rose, and shook itself into a bear.

Tank sneezed.

The bucket fell over.

"Oh, brilliant," said Anko.

Mau blinked at him. He had been bracing for more. Tank put his nose against Mau's palm, heavy and cold, and Mau rubbed the flat place between his eyes.

"Morning."

The bear sat on the edge of the sail.

"Not there," said three people at once.

He got up. The archer's left leg slid off the boards.

"Other side, Tank."

He went to the other side.

Umaru looked at the bear, then at Mau. "How much?"

He checked before answering. "It took some. Not nothing. It was just easier to keep hold of it."

*You held the pieces without pulling them apart again,* Ciel said. *You lost less of the work doing it.*

He repeated that, roughly. Umaru wrote it down.

"And standing there?" she asked, pointing at Tank.

"Nothing. Same as before."

The last two empty places in the soldiers' row were something else. He felt them when he looked for the legion, an absence at the end of a shape he still expected to be complete. There were no pieces to gather there, no pattern waiting for his hand.

He did not reach for them again.

* * *

Maki arrived with Yuta and a bowl of porridge she insisted Yuka finish while they were talking. Yuka tried to put it on a crate. Maki put it back into her hands.

"You can describe the terrible bird while you eat."

"It wasn't a bird."

"It's got a beak."

"So has a kettle," said Yuka.

Yuta looked at the heron. "I'd rather not have tea out of that one."

They gave the bodies space. Kaneki moved the last crate clear and stood with Anko on the dry side of the puddle. Mau crouched beside the archer and opened the window.

He stopped.

Umaru noticed. "What?"

"The places."

"What places?"

"Forty. It says forty now."

She got up so fast the slate nearly went into the water. "It was twenty."

"I know."

He let her see the field through the bracelet, deliberately selecting it rather than spilling the whole window into the morning. Ten occupied places. Forty available in total. Farther down, the soldiers in their own category: ninety-eight.

Umaru read it twice.

"When?"

"I didn't check that part after the last level."

"So we know it's changed," said Anko, "and we don't know exactly when."

Umaru wrote *first checked Day448* beside the new number.

Mau could feel a broadening down where the old registrations sat. Room he had not noticed because he had been looking at other things. The cup was still the cup. Making a place was not filling it, and filling it would still take something from him.

"Not forty new ones," Yuta said, reading over Umaru's shoulder.

"Forty total," she said. "Not the soldiers. Not the dogs and the other technique. Different list."

Maki watched Mau rather than the numbers. "And you don't have to use all of them today."

"There aren't forty bodies."

"That wasn't what I said."

He glanced up, smiled a little, and looked back at the archer.

* * *

He did not need a speech.

The feeling beneath the broken shape was clear enough. A watcher above the ice, a bow drawn before the traveller saw the tower. He had felt its aim turn on him. Now the thing it had been was still caught in the pieces, strong enough to answer.

"Arise."

The frost on the sail drew inward. Black ran between the pale fragments. An arm lifted, whole, and a long narrow figure stood before him without the bow, its empty fingers curled as if it could still feel a string.

Mau rose with it.

"Rime," he said, after a moment.

It lowered its head.

Umaru wrote the name. The cup had dropped again. Ciel told him where it stood; he waited until she had finished before turning to the stone walker.

That one resisted differently. Not refusing. Heavy in a way that had nothing to do with the four soldiers who had carried it. He had to hold the whole shape instead of thinking of it as broken sections. When it came up, its stone shoulders barely cleared the eaves.

"Cairn," said Mau.

"Cairn stays away from the gutter," said Kaneki.

The great head turned toward the gutter, apparently trying to identify its new enemy.

Mau put a hand on its arm. "That. Don't hit it."

It folded itself lower.

The heron was last. Its shadow rose out of the puddle with a noise like wet cloth lifted from a basin. The puddle stayed behind; nothing more dripped into it. It stood on one leg, long head lowered toward Mau, and waited.

"Reed."

Yuka looked at him over her empty bowl. "You named that horrible thing Reed?"

"What would you call it?"

She considered the hooked beak. "Bucket."

Anko laughed so hard she had to put her tea down.

* * *

Three new places filled. Thirteen in the first list now. Ninety-eight in the second. The shikigami did not appear in either, and when Mau sent the three back down, none of the cup came back with them.

Umaru checked that too. Anko made him describe the difference between sending a shadow away and paying to raise a new one, then put a line under the entry so nobody would charge him twice on paper.

The desert trays stayed where they were. Something in one of them had not answered when he touched the pieces; they would sort it properly before trying anything else. There was no body of the last guardian, no white giant waiting under the sail. The list ended with what they actually had.

Momo opened the kitchen door.

"If you're finished bringing things back from the dead, the porridge is going cold."

"Tank first," said Anko. "He isn't sitting on my list."

Mau turned.

Tank was lying on the dry side of the yard with the slate between his paws. Umaru was kneeling beside him, trying to persuade one paw to move off the corner without losing everything she had written.

"He likes the Ministry," said Yuka.

"The Ministry," said Umaru, tugging, "is considering sanctions."
