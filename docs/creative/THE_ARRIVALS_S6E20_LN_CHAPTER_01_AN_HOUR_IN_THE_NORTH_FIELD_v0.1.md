# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 33
## Chapter 21 — An Hour in the North Field

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Codex, 2026-10-22)
**Volume:** 33
**Reading order:** 21
**Source episode:** S6E20
**Continuity state:** Day 448, late morning. Mau initiates a private conversation with Rimuru after learning Ciel told him. They notify the house before going to the north field. Mau explains his fear of losing his present feelings; Rimuru refuses to lie or pretend the theft is harmless, and asks him to stop treating Frieren as the cause. They leave with a partial agreement, affection and unresolved anger. Nobody else hears their conversation; full memory restoration does not occur.

*When: Day 448, late morning. The north field is damp where the frost has gone. Anko knows where they are and that they want an hour. Neither of them has brought a weapon.*

Rimuru walked with his hands in his pockets.

He had left the blanket on his chair before they went, and taken his coat from the peg. The weariness had gone with him. Diablo had stood too, at once.

"Just us," Rimuru had said.

The demon had looked at Mau, then at his master, and sat back down.

Now they were beyond the last workshop, and Mau was trying to remember whether he had ever walked beside Rimuru without someone else between them. He knew that they had. There were little pieces: a bowl, a fence, somebody laughing at a bird. But trying to place the pieces brought a different picture over them, smooth and plausible, of Rimuru showing a new arrival where to put his things.

He looked away from it.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"Not much."

"Because of what Ciel told you."

Rimuru stopped walking.

They had reached the low bank where the field rose toward the trees. The ground was dry beneath the branches. Mau sat on a root with enough room beside him for another person, and waited.

Rimuru did not sit.

"I should have spoken to you," he said.

"I'm speaking to you."

"Earlier. Before you went in. I knew something was wrong. We all knew something was wrong, and I let you go because I thought an hour—"

"You didn't turn the gate red."

"I know that."

"Then don't start with that bit. Please."

Rimuru looked at him. For a moment the expression was so familiar that Mau expected to know the next sentence before it came. He did not.

Rimuru sat on the other end of the root.

* * *

"I'm not angry at her," Mau said.

Rimuru kept looking at the field. "Ciel."

"Yes. She was carrying something she couldn't tell anyone, and I kept asking her to wait. She told you. I understand."

"She thought you might not."

"She asked me if I wanted her to explain to you that I wanted to talk. I thought I'd manage that part myself."

Rimuru almost smiled. It did not stay.

"What do you want me to say?" he asked.

Mau rubbed his hands together. They were cold from the washing court.

"What you actually think. Not the thing you spent all night making safe to say."

Rimuru bent and picked a piece of dead grass off his shoe. He rolled it between two fingers, then let it go.

"I think you know enough to know why she was frightened."

Mau did not answer.

"I think Yuka knows more than you do. I think she's eighteen and in love and terrified, and none of those things stop this from hurting people. And I think you've been letting tomorrow be the day you have to look at that."

"I have looked at it."

"You asked her to wait. Twice."

"I asked her to wait."

"Those sound different from inside your head. From outside, everyone is still waiting."

The words landed harder than Mau had expected. He looked at his hands.

"I didn't ask her to lie."

"No," said Rimuru. "You didn't."

They sat without speaking for a while. A shadow crossed the far edge of the field, one of the watchers going home by the tree line. Neither called it closer.

"Can I try to tell you something?" Mau asked at last.

Rimuru turned toward him.

"I'm not trying to make it all right. I just don't know how to explain it without sounding as if I am."

"Try."

* * *

"You tell me I loved her," said Mau. "Everyone does. And I know you aren't all making it up. The things in my room, the way people stop when I walk in, the way she looks at me. I can see something happened."

"It did."

"I know. But I don't know it the way you know it."

He paused, trying to find words that were not pictures of things he could barely reach.

"When you say I was happy, I hear you telling me about somebody you knew. When I try to remember being him, something else turns up in the place. A day I came here. Things people said. It fits until I put it beside what you're telling me. Then it doesn't. But the feeling doesn't change just because I've spotted the wrong date."

Rimuru had stopped moving entirely.

"I kissed Yuka," said Mau. "I wanted to. I still want to. Nobody put her face in front of me and said I had to. She makes me laugh. I want to hear what she'll say about things. When she gets hurt, I feel it before I can think about why."

"Mau—"

"I know how that sounds. With Frieren waiting. I know. But that's the bit that's true inside me now. And I don't know what getting the rest back does to it."

He swallowed.

"You all want me back. I don't know which part you mean. If I remember everything and look at her and there's nothing there any more—" He stopped. "She's the one who lives through that. Not a wrong date. Her."

Rimuru looked down at the root between them.

"And Frieren's living through it now," he said.

Mau shut his eyes.

"I know."

"I don't say that to cancel what you feel."

"I know."

Rimuru let him have the silence. He had asked for the explanation, and now that he had it he could not make it small enough to put beside a simple instruction.

"Were you happy?" Mau asked, very quietly.

Rimuru looked up.

"With me. Before. Was it only terrible things?"

For the first time since they had left the house, Rimuru laughed. It came out once, without any pleasure in it at first, then changed as he shook his head.

"You absolute idiot."

Mau looked at him.

"You made me live above your wardrobe."

"I know where you live."

"Yes, but you don't remember being a complete pain about it. The ladder. The door. Who could hear what through the floor." He rubbed his face. "We argued about bowls. And shifts. And whether the other one counted as an idiot if he was doing it for sensible reasons."

"Did he?"

"Always. Especially then."

Mau laughed before he could stop himself. Rimuru looked at him when he did, and the smile went from his face so quickly that Mau wished he had not.

"That," said Rimuru. "You used to do that when you knew I'd had a bad day. You'd ask me something stupid and make me explain it until I heard how stupid it was."

Mau opened his mouth, then closed it.

"I'm not asking you to do it now," Rimuru said. "I just remembered."

* * *

They moved off the root when the shade got cold.

They walked along the field instead of back toward the house. Rimuru picked the muddy side without noticing; Mau went round the puddles. After a while Rimuru said, "I don't think remembering is meant to take you away."

"You don't know that."

"No. I don't know what this thing has done well enough to promise you anything. But what it gave you is a story, Mau. A story about us. You did live here. You weren't sent to take over somebody else's place."

Mau stared at a broken seed head beside the path.

"It doesn't feel like that."

"I believe you."

They went a few more steps.

"I don't know whether I'm angrier at you," said Rimuru, "or at the thing that made this a choice you thought you had to make."

"You can be angry at both."

"I'm finding that out."

Mau stopped beside the fence. A narrow strip of old cloth had caught on the bottom rail and was moving in the wind. He reached down and freed it before it tore.

"What happens when everyone knows?" Rimuru asked him.

"I don't know."

"They will."

"Yes."

"This isn't something two people can hide in a house like ours forever."

"I'm not thinking about forever."

"I think you need to think farther than the next time somebody walks through the door."

Mau folded the strip in his hand. "I don't know how to cross that bridge without her being the first person everyone sees on it."

Rimuru did not ask which her. He did not have to.

"I won't tell a lie for you," he said.

"I don't want you to."

"If somebody asks me something I know, I won't stand there and tell them they imagined it."

"Then don't."

Rimuru waited, as if he expected another condition.

Mau shook his head. "There isn't a clever bit after that. If someone asks, tell them."

"And until then?"

He looked toward the roofs beyond the field. The kitchen chimney, smoke thinning in the late morning. A rectangle of yellow at the upstairs window where somebody had hung something to air.

"You told Ciel you couldn't carry it alone," he said. "Or she told you. It doesn't matter. You can talk to each other. I'm not going to tell you which parts of it you're allowed to feel."

Rimuru looked at him for a long moment.

"But you still don't want her to tell Frieren."

Mau's grip tightened on the cloth.

"Not like that. Not everything she saw in the gate, suddenly, when Frieren hasn't even heard me say I was wrong to ask her to stay away."

"Then say it."

"I will."

* * *

They had turned back toward the house, but were still out on the field, when Rimuru spoke again.

"I don't want you to pretend you love Frieren."

Mau looked at him.

"That isn't what I'm asking. Nobody gets anything from a performance." Rimuru's voice was tired again. "I want my friend back. And I want him to stop making her stand there as if she's done something shameful by wanting to speak to him."

Mau felt heat go up the back of his neck.

"I thought she wanted something I couldn't give her."

"She does. That doesn't make her dangerous."

"I didn't say—"

"You told her to stay away."

Mau stopped.

Beyond the field the workshop door was open a little way, a dark rectangle in the sun. From here neither could hear what was happening inside. Mau looked at it, then back at Rimuru.

"Yes," Mau said. "I did."

Rimuru waited.

"I'll talk to her," said Mau. "Not to ask her to wait forever. Not to tell her I'll be different tomorrow. To say that part."

"All right."

They went the rest of the way together. Near the yard, Rimuru stopped to scrape mud from his shoe with a stick. It was hopeless; the more he scraped, the more he found underneath.

Mau held out a hand.

Rimuru looked at it, then at his face.

"For balance," said Mau.

"I know what a hand is for."

He took it anyway, leaning on Mau while he worked the mud out of the sole.

At the kitchen door Anko looked at them over her mug.

"You were fifty minutes."

"Do we get ten back?" Mau asked.

"No refunds."

Rimuru let go of his hand and went inside. He had not forgiven him. Mau had not asked him to. They could sit at the same table now, and when Kaneki put down a fresh pot, Rimuru moved the cold cup aside instead of staring into it.
