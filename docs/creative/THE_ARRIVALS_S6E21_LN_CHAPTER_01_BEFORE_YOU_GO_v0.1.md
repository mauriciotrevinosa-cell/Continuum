# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 33
## Chapter 23 — Before You Go

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Codex, 2026-10-22)
**Volume:** 33
**Reading order:** 23
**Source episode:** S6E21
**Continuity state:** Day 448, afternoon. Mau stops Frieren from leaving the long room and asks to speak privately outside. He apologises for asking her to stay away, recognises she is not the cause of the theft and admits fragments returned in the gate after Yuka told him about the tower. He cannot promise recovered love or full memory. They agree ordinary conversation need not be forbidden. Frieren later tells Fern there may be a possibility, without claiming restoration. Yuka sees the invitation but does not hear the private conversation.

*When: Day 448, afternoon. Lunch has been cleared. Frieren has been on the same page long enough for the light to move off it.*

Yuka saw him stop.

She was carrying her mended coat upstairs when Mau came through the other doorway. He had a cup in his hand. Frieren sat in her chair at the reading end of the room, the open book across her knees, and as soon as she saw him she closed it.

She did not slam it. She put one finger between the pages, got up and reached for the folded cloth on the arm of the chair. She had become very practised at leaving without looking as if she was leaving because of him.

"Frieren," Mau said.

Yuka's hand tightened on the coat.

Frieren looked at him.

"Before you go. Could we talk?"

For a moment Frieren did nothing. The cloth lay half folded in her hand. Then she put it down on the chair.

"Yes," she said.

Mau looked toward the outer door. "Somewhere quiet?"

She nodded and went with him. Neither looked up the stair. Yuka remained there, with her palm against the rail, until Momo asked from the kitchen whether she was bringing the coat to dinner.

"No," she said, too quickly.

She carried it the rest of the way up.

* * *

Frieren chose the bench beside the bare garden beds.

It was out of the wind. The ground had been turned for winter in some places and left beneath a cover of straw in others. There were small stakes with writing on them, and a length of string somebody had forgotten to take down, sagging between two posts. Mau moved it away from the bench before she sat.

She watched his hand do it.

He sat far enough along that she could put the book between them if she wanted. She kept it in her lap.

"I said something to you," he began. "Before we went into the gate."

"I remember."

"I know you do."

He had rehearsed the next part coming out of the house. Now all the sentences sounded like a way of asking her not to be angry while he explained why he had hurt her.

He put the cup down by his foot.

"I was wrong to ask you to stay away from me. You hadn't done anything to me."

Her finger moved along the closed edge of the book.

"You didn't remember me."

"That isn't something you did."

She looked at him then, directly, and he found that harder than watching her leave the room.

"I felt cornered," he said. "People kept telling me things I was supposed to feel. When you came close, I thought I was going to be asked again, and I wanted it to stop." He rubbed his thumb against the side of the cup. "So I made you the person who had to go away."

Frieren looked toward the garden.

"You were very clear."

"Yes."

He did not put anything after it.

The string tapped against the post. She reached for it, almost absently, but it was out of reach. Mau caught it and wrapped the loose end round the wood until it stopped.

"I'm sorry," he said.

* * *

She did not tell him it was all right.

He had not expected her to. He waited while she looked at the covered beds and the small neat stakes, at the places where things would come up when it was warmer. From here the kitchen sounded distant. A chair scraped inside, then somebody laughed, and the door shut on it.

"Did something happen in the gate?" Frieren asked.

"A lot happened."

"Something about this."

He nodded.

"Yuka told me about the tower. The parts I couldn't fit together. What I was going up for. Some of it."

She kept still.

"And there were pieces," he said. "Not a whole memory. More like knowing what a word is going to be before someone says it. A bench. A foot against my leg."

Her fingers stopped on the book.

"Cold," he said. "The foot. I remember that part very clearly."

He glanced at her. He had meant it almost as a joke, something small to put between the difficult things. Her eyes were on his face now, so intent that the little smile went out of him.

"Do you remember what I said?" she asked.

He breathed in, slowly.

"One."

She looked down at once.

He did not know whether she wanted him to say more. There was a reply somewhere behind the word, ready-shaped, the way his feet had known a turn in the gate. He could have said it. He did not know if saying it to her here would be a kindness or the worst thing he could do.

"I know it mattered," he said instead. "I don't remember it properly. Not what it was like to be there."

She nodded once. Her hair had slipped over one shoulder; she tucked it back with a movement he felt he should have been able to place.

"You don't have to find the rest now," she said.

He looked at her.

"You were looking for it," she said. "Just then."

"Yes."

"Don't hurt yourself trying to give me a sentence."

He sat back. The cup had gone cold beside his foot.

* * *

"But," Frieren said.

It was so quiet he almost thought she had finished another thought he had not heard.

"What?"

"There is a but."

He looked at his hands. The scars ran across them in shapes he could name more readily than the moments that had made them.

"I don't know if I'll be the person you remember."

Frieren turned the book over in her lap. There was no expression on her face he knew how to answer.

"You're the person sitting here," she said.

"I know. But the things you mean when you say my name—" He stopped and tried again. "I don't feel all of that. I know there was something with you. I'm not pretending I can't see it any more. But I can't say I'll wake up tomorrow and know how to be what you lost."

"I haven't asked for tomorrow."

"No. You haven't."

He had expected a plea. Perhaps that was why he had been so afraid to speak to her. He had expected her to ask something he would have to refuse, and she was sitting beside him holding a book, answering only the things he actually said.

"I need to understand what happened to me," he said. "And what I want to do with it. I don't want to promise something because it would make this afternoon easier."

Her mouth moved a little.

"It wouldn't make it easier."

"No?"

"I'd hear it."

He thought about that. He believed her.

"Then I won't."

They sat while a small bird worked along the edge of a bed, looking for something beneath the straw. It found a loose end, pulled with all its weight and fell backward when the strand came free. Frieren watched it get up, indignant, and try again.

"What are you going to do?" she asked at last.

"I don't know yet. Ciel's helping with what she can. Apparently I have family here who'd rather I stopped treating them like the people who showed me a spare room."

He meant to soften it. It did not sound soft when he said it.

Frieren's gaze stayed on the bird.

"You do."

"I can start by not walking out of the room whenever that gets difficult."

"Or making someone else walk out."

He looked at her.

She met his eyes. Calmly. There was hurt in it, unmistakable now that he had stopped trying not to see.

"Yes," he said. "That too."

* * *

The bird had stolen enough straw to look as if it was carrying a beard.

Frieren watched it fly badly to the fence, drop half the load and return for it. She had not moved away from Mau on the bench. She had not moved closer either.

"Can I speak to you?" she asked.

He blinked. "You are speaking to me."

"When you're in the long room. At the table. If I have something to say."

"Of course."

She nodded, looking at her book again.

The shame arrived a moment later, when he understood why she had needed to ask.

"Frieren. You don't need permission. I was wrong. I'm not putting a different rule in its place."

"All right."

"I mean it."

"I heard you."

He bent and picked up his cup. Something had got into it while it was on the ground. He looked at the small drifting fleck, tipped the tea onto the earth away from the plant stakes and wiped the rim with his thumb.

Frieren watched him.

"What are you reading?" he asked.

She looked down, as if she had forgotten the book was there.

"A preservation spell."

"For food?"

"The author spends a long time explaining that it isn't for food."

"What happens if you use it on food?"

"They don't say. They tell you not to. Seven times."

He looked at her. A smile was beginning at the edge of her mouth, very faint, not offered to make him feel better.

"You're going to try it."

"Not on anything we intend to eat."

"Does the kitchen know there's a difference?"

She closed her hand over the cover.

"Momo is very unreasonable about experiments."

He laughed. It surprised them both.

He did not fall in love with her sitting on the bench. Nothing opened cleanly inside his head. The hurts they had spoken about were still there when she stood, and he could not make himself into a promise by carrying her book.

But she did not have to leave first. They walked back across the garden together, and when they reached the door he held it while she went in.

* * *

Fern found her in the long room a little later.

The book was open again. Frieren's eyes were on it, but when Fern sat beside her she did not pretend she had been reading.

"He spoke to you," Fern said.

"Yes."

Fern waited. She had brought two cups and put one on the little table beside the chair. Frieren took it with both hands.

"He apologised," she said. "For telling me to stay away. He remembers pieces."

Fern's expression changed before she could stop it.

"Not everything," Frieren said. "He was very clear about that too."

Fern looked at the cup she had brought for herself.

"Did he ask you to keep waiting?"

"No."

"What did he ask?"

Frieren thought about the bench, the string wound round the post, his face when he realised she was asking whether she was allowed to speak.

"What I was reading."

Fern looked up.

"At the end," said Frieren.

She held the warm cup against her palms. There was a small, dangerous lightness in her chest. She knew what it was. Naming it too loudly would not make it safer.

"There may be a possibility," she said.

Fern sat beside her for a while. She did not tell her that there was, or that there was not. When Frieren finally looked at the page, Fern leaned over to see it too.

"That isn't for food," Fern said.

"I know."

"Miss Frieren."

"I know."
