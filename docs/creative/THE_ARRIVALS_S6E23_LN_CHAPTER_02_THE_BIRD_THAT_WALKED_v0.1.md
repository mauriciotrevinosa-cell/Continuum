# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 33
## Chapter 28 — The Bird That Walked

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Codex, 2026-10-22)
**Volume:** 33
**Reading order:** 28
**Source episode:** S6E23
**Continuity state:** Day 449, late afternoon into the preparations for supper. Ori and Frieren fold scrap paper in the long room and invent a target game. Mau joins by choice. They enjoy his company without restoring his missing family recognition; no private conversation is relayed, no romance is discovered and no promise of recovery is made.

*When: Day 449, late afternoon; one end of the long table, before it is needed for supper.*

The first bird fell on its face.

Ori looked under the table to see whether it had done anything more promising on the way down.

It had not. It lay between the feet of a chair, with its wings pointing in different directions.

"The left side is wrong," she said.

Frieren looked at the piece of paper in her own hands.

"Both of mine are wrong."

"You haven't thrown yours."

"I can tell."

Ori reached under the chair and recovered the bird. There was dust on its nose. She brushed it off and examined the fold that had gone round the wrong way.

The paper was from a parcel. One side was plain, the other covered in a few old marks which had nothing to do with birds. Ori had cut it into squares with scissors borrowed from the sewing basket. She had asked first, and had put the scissors back before carrying the squares to the table, because she knew what happened to people who left the sewing scissors in other places.

Frieren's book was shut beside her elbow. She had been using it to press the folds flat. Now she put her second bird between the covers and sat with her hand on top.

"Will that make it better?" Ori asked.

"It will make it flatter."

"That's not the same."

"We'll find out."

Ori sat down beside her again.

She had brought the squares because she wanted something to do with her hands. Frieren had moved the book and asked for one. They had not agreed what they were making until Ori's third fold looked a little like a wing.

Now there were five birds. One of them had no head. One had a head and very little else. The one inside Frieren's book made the pages sit crooked.

Ori put a finger against the spine.

"You shouldn't hurt your book."

Frieren opened it at once.

The bird came out very flat.

* * *

Momo let them borrow a wooden bowl until supper.

"Not that one," she said, when Frieren reached for the first. "That's for the sauce. The empty one."

Frieren looked into the bowl she had nearly taken. Then at the row of empty ones next to it.

"This one has more character."

"It has sauce."

Ori took an empty one before the argument could become more interesting. They carried it back to the table and set it at the far end, clear of the cloths laid out for supper.

The game was simple. They stood behind the bench and tried to get the birds into the bowl. A bird that struck the rim did not count. A bird that went under the table did not count. They had not yet decided about a bird which landed in somebody's hair, but agreed they would ask that person if it happened.

Frieren went first.

Her bird travelled almost the whole distance. Then it turned sharply, touched the wall and landed on the floor.

Ori went to collect it.

"Yours goes farther."

"It doesn't go toward the bowl."

"But if the bowl were over there."

"We could move it."

Ori looked back at her.

Frieren's face was quite serious.

"After you've thrown?"

"We haven't made a rule about that."

They made a rule about that.

Ori's next bird went into the bowl upside down, with one wing caught over the rim. They bent over it together, trying not to breathe on it.

"That counts," Ori whispered.

"You said the rim didn't count."

"If you hit it. This one is holding on."

Frieren considered the distinction.

"Very well."

She picked up her own bird, walked round the bench and set it in the bowl beside Ori's.

Ori stared at her.

"It has legs," Frieren said.

"You gave it legs."

"It needed them."

Ori laughed. She tried to stop before it became too loud, and then laughed again because Frieren was looking at the tiny bent corners under the bird as if she had solved a very important problem.

They made another rule.

* * *

Mau came in while Ori was writing it down.

He had washed his hands. Water had darkened the edge of one sleeve, and he was trying to push it back without getting the dry part wet. He stopped beside the table and looked at the bowl, the paper on the floor and the two of them standing behind the bench.

"Am I in the way?"

"Only if you have paper in your hair," Ori said.

He put a hand on his head.

She had not expected him to do it. For a moment she forgot the explanation. Frieren picked a square up from the table and held it out.

"We're throwing birds."

Mau looked at hers.

"Is that the bird?"

"It has legs."

"I can see that."

"They are not allowed any more," Ori said. "And you can't move the bowl. Or use magic. Or put it in. It has to fly."

He read the small sheet she had written. Then he looked at the floor, where a bird without a head was resting on its side.

"Is that one all right?"

"It can still enter," Ori said. "If it wants."

Mau smiled.

"Could I try?"

"Yes."

She said it quickly, and reached for the pile before he could change his mind. Three squares stuck together. She separated them very carefully and gave him the one with the least writing on it.

"You can make a new one."

"Do I have to follow a pattern?"

Ori shook her head.

He sat down at the end of the bench. He folded the paper in half, opened it again, looked at the crease and folded it in a different direction. Ori watched his hands. He made a narrow point and pressed it with his thumbnail. Then he looked at the birds they had made.

"I'm not sure mine's a bird."

"It isn't a person," Frieren said.

"No."

"That helps."

He folded a second wing and held it up. One side was longer than the other.

Ori could have shown him where to change it. She had already decided which fold to undo. Instead she asked him to give it a try first.

He stood behind the bench and threw.

The paper turned over once and struck him in the chest.

Frieren looked down at it.

"Yours came back."

"I think it likes me."

Ori laughed before she could decide whether she should.

* * *

They moved the bench a little closer. All three of them moved it, with Frieren lifting her end just enough that Ori did not have to drag hers. Mau held the middle and watched their feet.

He stayed.

There was no reason he had to. Nobody had asked him to keep Ori company or told him it would help with anything. He changed his wing, sent the bird past the bowl and went to get it himself. On the way back, he picked up the ones the others had lost.

He brought Ori's first bird to her.

"This one yours?"

She nodded.

He put it beside her elbow, ready for her next turn.

Frieren's bird struck the outside of the bowl. She watched it lie on the table, its feet turned up.

"The legs interfere with the balance," Mau said.

"I need them for landing."

"Not if you land in the bowl."

"I haven't."

He looked at her. Then he laughed, and Frieren's mouth moved, very slightly, before she looked down at the next square.

Ori made a mark on the sheet. She had won twice. Frieren had won once if holding on counted, which it did now because Ori had said so. Mau had not won at all.

"I'm very good at getting them back," he said, when she told him.

"That's not a score."

"It should be. Look how far this one went."

The headless bird had reached the other side of the room.

They took a little while to find it.

When they came back, Frieren had made her next bird smaller. She did not show them until it was her turn. It left her hand without wobbling and went straight into the bowl.

Ori ran to look.

It was folded into a tight square.

"That is not a bird."

"It flew."

Mau leaned over the bowl.

"She's got you there."

Ori added two more rules. Mau watched her writing and asked whether very bad birds still counted as birds. She said they did, because otherwise they would have to start again.

His next throw landed beside the bowl.

"Better," Ori said.

"I'm getting there."

She moved along the bench to make room when he sat. There was enough room without moving. She moved anyway.

* * *

Momo came for the bowl when the light at the window began to fade.

She stood with her hands on her hips and looked at the paper scattered on the floor.

"How many of those are there?"

"We can get them," Ori said.

"I know you can. That's why I'm asking."

They got them. Frieren found the headless one under Momo's shoe and asked her not to stand there. Momo lifted her foot very slowly.

Mau wiped the bowl with the clean cloth Momo gave him. Then he carried it into the kitchen, and came back for the wet patch beneath it. Ori was under the table collecting the last of the birds when she heard him say they had done a good job making up the game.

She came up too fast and bumped her shoulder on the bench.

"Are you all right?" he asked.

"Yes."

His hand had moved toward her. She saw it. She waited.

He rested it on the bench instead, steadying it while she crawled out.

She stood beside him with the birds held against her chest.

"Thank you for letting me join in," he said.

It was warm. He meant it. He was smiling at her, with the damp cloth in one hand and the crooked bird in the other.

Ori looked at the bird.

She had been pleased a moment ago. She was still pleased. The two things did not fit neatly beside each other, and she did not know where to put them.

"You can play," she said. "You don't have to ask."

"All right."

He did not sound as though he was refusing her. He simply did not know why she had said it like that.

From the kitchen, Momo called his name and asked him to take the large pot to the table. He went to help. Ori put the birds in a small pile by Frieren's book.

Frieren had not opened it.

"We need somewhere for these," Ori said.

Frieren looked round the cleared table. Then she reached under the bench for the empty parcel they had taken the paper from and opened it out.

Ori set the birds inside. Frieren lifted one corner to keep them from sliding away.

"Yours won," Ori said.

"You said it wasn't a bird."

"It wasn't."

Frieren took the small square out and unfolded one edge. She bent it back the other way, giving it a very short head.

Ori inspected it.

"That's worse."

Frieren put it in the parcel anyway.

They carried the whole untidy collection to the shelf together. When Mau came back with the pot, Ori held up the last bird before putting it away.

"This is yours."

"The one that came back?"

"Yes."

He smiled at it and went to fetch the bread.

Ori laid it on top, with its long wing folded over Frieren's bird's legs, and pushed the parcel back far enough that nobody would mistake it for rubbish.
