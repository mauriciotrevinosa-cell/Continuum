# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 33
## Chapter 35 — Lunch, Somewhere Else

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Codex, 2026-10-22)
**Volume:** 33
**Reading order:** 35
**Source episode:** S6E27
**Continuity state:** Day451, noon. Mau and Yuka keep their planned lunch outside the busy rooms, within the protected settlement; they tell the house their location and return time. Food and borrowed things come from the household, not a new shopping trip. They enjoy a quiet date and kiss privately without sex; they distinguish choosing an ordinary afternoon from agreeing to a contract. Their bond deepens without resolving concealment, restoring Mau's old feelings or making a promise of children yet.

*When: Day451, noon. A sheltered place on the inside of the western boundary, out of the path of people carrying things. The house knows where they are. They have promised to bring the bowls back.*

"That's not a basket," Yuka said.

Mau looked at what he was carrying.

"It has handles."

"So does a pan."

"I considered a pan. This seemed more festive."

It was a small wooden crate with a folded cloth lining it. Two bowls sat inside, with a covered jar, a loaf cut down the middle and something tied in a second cloth that Yuka kept trying to examine. Momo had made him take the cloth from the clean shelf instead of using his coat.

"It was clean," he said when Yuka asked.

"Your coat?"

"Mostly."

She reached for the crate. He turned away, protecting it from her.

"You get to find out when we get there."

"I've carried food with you before. Sometimes finding out late is how you discover he ate it on the way."

"One time."

"I wasn't counting."

He looked at her. She looked back, entirely innocent, and tucked her hands into the mended sleeves.

They had left separately enough that neither of them thought of it as leaving together until they reached the path. Yuka had told Maki they would eat on the western side, by the low bank, and be back well before the afternoon work needed the bowls. Maki had looked at her for a moment, then told her to take a warmer layer. Mau had put their location on Anko's small slate without being asked.

It was lunch. That was what they had said. Nobody had had to believe anything that was not true.

Yuka watched the edge of the crate knock lightly against his leg as they walked and thought about all the things a true sentence could leave out.

* * *

The place he had found was dry.

That was its chief virtue. A low rise broke the wind, and the sun had been on the grass long enough to take the damp out of it. The house roofs showed above the slope when Yuka stood, but disappeared when she sat down. Beyond the boundary the trees were losing the last of their colour. A few leaves clung stubbornly in places that did not seem any more sheltered than the empty branches beside them.

He put the crate down and unfolded the blanket.

"No legs?" she asked.

He looked at her.

"The blanket. Are we sure it isn't going to get up and run off?"

He laughed. "Ori's rules are getting very strict."

"You were playing with them the other afternoon."

"I was losing. It's different."

She sat on one corner and held the other down while he moved the crate onto it. It would have been a handsome arrangement if one of the bowls had not immediately rolled onto its side. He caught it with his knee.

"Excellent," she said. "Very elegant."

"You haven't seen the food."

He took the cover off the jar.

It was ordinary warm food, the sort the house made because people needed feeding: a thick vegetable stew, bread with a proper crust, a little sharp sauce kept separate because he had remembered how much she liked and how much he did not. In the second cloth were cooked apples in a small lidded pot, and two spoons.

Yuka looked at them.

"You got dessert."

"I made a persuasive case."

"To Momo?"

"To Taihei. Momo said we could have whatever was already there if we stopped standing in front of the stove."

Yuka laughed and took a bowl. He held the jar while she served, careful with the hot cloth around it, and she handed him the fuller bowl without commenting on it.

For a while they just ate.

* * *

It was strange hearing the house from this far away.

Not silence. The sound of a door, voices carried in pieces when the wind turned, a hammer working at something somewhere they could not see. It was possible to imagine walking back into any of the little scenes behind those sounds: somebody looking for a cup, somebody refusing a chore, somebody finishing one without having to be asked.

Yuka rested her bowl on her knees.

"Do you miss being in the gate?"

Mau tore a piece of bread off before answering.

"Which bit?"

"Not the thing trying to erase us."

"That narrows it down."

She nudged him with her elbow. He smiled, then looked out at the bare trees.

"Some of it. Knowing what we were going to do that day. Being able to ask you something without checking who else was in the room."

"We asked terrible questions."

"Yours were terrible. Mine were excellent."

"You asked if a serpent would have knees if we pulled hard enough."

"An important tactical question."

She put a piece of bread in his hand before he could defend it further.

He ate it.

"I don't miss the cold," he said. "Or waking up not knowing what a noise was going to turn out to be."

"You always looked as if you knew."

He turned toward her. "No, I didn't."

"To me. Sometimes. You'd look at a whole thing coming at us and say we should go round it, the way someone would say we should use the other door."

He considered that, then smiled without much confidence.

"I was hoping you knew what you were doing."

She stared at him.

He stared back.

Then she began laughing, and he did too, setting the bowl down because he could not trust himself with it. When she leaned into him he did not have to decide what to do with his arm. He put it round her, and she stayed there until she could breathe without laughing again.

* * *

They moved closer after the food was gone.

She sat between his knees with her back against his chest, both of them facing the trees. The blanket would not reach round their shoulders as well as under them. She tried for a while, lost a corner and made him move so she could retrieve it.

"You're sitting on it."

"You're sitting on me."

"That's different. I meant to."

He lifted just enough for her to pull the cloth free. She settled again, satisfied, with his hands loose in her lap. The two stiff fingers of his left hand lay against her wrist instead of closing with the others. She shifted so they would not be pressed awkwardly beneath her sleeve.

He looked down at what she had done.

"Thank you."

She touched his knuckles once. Did not ask him to explain them.

"When we went to the sea," she said, "you made the waves shine."

"You thought the old woman was laughing at us."

"She was laughing at you. There's a difference."

He smiled against her hair.

"What about the waves?"

She had to think about how to ask it.

"Would you have done it if I hadn't been impressed?"

His hands moved a little in her lap. "Made them shine? Probably. It looked good."

"That's not—"

"I know."

She looked over her shoulder. He was watching her now, not the trees.

"I wanted you to see it," he said. "If you hadn't liked it, I'd have wanted to find something you did."

"Not everything has to be a spell."

"I brought apples."

She laughed softly. "You did."

"Very advanced apples. Hours of study."

She turned inside the circle of his arms until she could face him. There was a red mark on his wrist from the crate handle. A crumb at the corner of his mouth. She brushed the crumb away with her thumb and left her hand there a moment longer than she needed to.

"This," she said. "I like this."

He looked at her mouth. She saw him do it, and did not have to wonder whether she was allowed to want the same thing.

The kiss was unhurried. No one moving past a door, no sound of footsteps to separate them. His hand stayed at her waist. When she leaned closer he moved with her, and when she drew back to look at him he waited there, smiling a little, as if the looking was part of it too.

She kissed him again.

* * *

The apples had gone cold.

They ate them anyway, sitting side by side with the little pot between them. Yuka found a piece of peel and held it up accusingly. Mau suggested it was a deliberate contrast of textures.

"You learned that answer from somebody else."

"I thought it sounded convincing."

"It sounds like you didn't cook the apples."

"That too."

She fed the piece to him on the spoon. He accepted it with a dignity that lasted until the skin stuck to his lip.

They stayed until the shadow of the low bank reached the edge of the blanket. Then she got up and began stacking bowls while he shook the crumbs out away from where they had been sitting. He looked at the sun, still well above the roofline, and put the last folded cloth in the crate before she could accuse him of giving their lunch a debrief.

"We can do this again," she said.

"We have to bring the bowls back before we can borrow them again."

"Not the bowls."

He looked up. Her hair had come loose at one side. She was holding the little pot with both spoons tucked under its lid.

"I know," he said.

They walked back before the afternoon work started. At the point where the path turned toward the house, she let his hand go. Neither spoke about it. He adjusted the crate under his arm, she carried the blanket, and together they went in through the kitchen door to find Momo waiting for her bowls.
