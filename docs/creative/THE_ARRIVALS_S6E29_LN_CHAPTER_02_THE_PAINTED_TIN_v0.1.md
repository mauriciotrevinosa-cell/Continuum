# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 33
## Chapter 40 — The Painted Tin

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Codex, 2026-10-22)
**Volume:** 33
**Reading order:** 40
**Source episode:** S6E29
**Continuity state:** Day452 after dinner. The regular crackers table in Seiko's room runs alongside the men's roof night. Seiko, Maki, Frieren and Mai have ordinary complaints, humour and company, with Turbo Granny on her small cushion. Frieren's cautious hope follows her Day448 conversation, without restored romance or a promise. Maki has no new knowledge about Mau/Yuka and discloses none. The women remain friends; no future rupture is prematurely enacted.

*When: Day452, evening. Seiko's room. Four cushions round the low table, one much smaller one, and a painted tin which has developed an opinion about opening.*

Seiko gave the tin to Maki.

"Don't bend the lid."

Maki stopped with both thumbs beneath the rim.

"You asked me to open it."

"And I'd like to close it afterward."

Mai held out her hand. Maki looked at it, then passed the tin across the table without comment. The lid had flowers painted on it, worn almost white at one corner. Mai turned it once, pressed near the edge and pulled.

Nothing happened.

From the little cushion beside Seiko, Turbo Granny said, "I could do it if you hadn't put me in this."

"You'd eat them all," Seiko said.

"Then I'd have got it open."

Frieren put down her cup.

Four heads turned toward her.

"No," said Seiko.

Frieren looked at her.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your hands did."

"It would have been a very small spell."

"It's a very small tin."

Maki reached across and took it back from Mai. She lifted the lid, carefully, and this time it came free with a sharp little pop which made Turbo Granny sit up.

The room smelled of crackers.

Seiko inspected the rim before allowing any to be taken.

* * *

Mai had a new complaint about the laundry.

Not a serious one, she said. The serious ones involved people leaving wet clothes in heaps. This involved somebody deciding that all the dark things could be kept together until their owners claimed them.

"They can," Maki said.

"Three people claimed my shirt."

"Were they right?" Seiko asked.

"It was my shirt."

"That doesn't answer her," said Turbo Granny.

Mai looked down at the cat. "And what have you contributed to the laundry?"

"Nothing. I keep my body clean."

Seiko made a small, disbelieving noise. Turbo Granny ignored it.

The shirt had finally been found over the back of a chair, inside out, with a folded towel beneath it. Mai had known it immediately because one cuff had a little uneven line of stitching which she refused to let anyone mend properly. The stitching stayed. She objected to being made to identify her own clothes by a defect.

"You could put your name in it," Maki said.

"It's one shirt."

"You have more than one."

"Thank you. I'd forgotten."

Frieren took a cracker and ate it while the sisters continued. Maki had pulled one knee up, the relaxed posture she never seemed to have anywhere else. Mai leaned back on her hands. Seiko poured the next cup before the kettle got too cool.

It had taken Frieren a long time to learn when not to help at this table. Sometimes the answer to a complaint was to let the complaint get larger until its owner laughed at it.

Tonight she could listen long enough to find out.

* * *

Maki's complaint was the spear.

"The flower catches," she said.

Mai looked at her at once.

"On what?"

"My sleeve. Sometimes. When I lift it too close."

"Then don't lift it too close."

"That's what I thought. Then I thought you might want to know."

Mai reached for another cracker with unnecessary care.

The silver flower was still tied below the blade. She had made it for her sister, and Maki had kept it there through all the times a sensible person would have put the decoration somewhere safer. Mai had checked it in the yard that afternoon, without being asked, and found nothing wrong with it except a loosened tie.

"I can move it down," Maki said.

"It would look stupid down there."

"It might look stupid up there."

"It doesn't."

Maki took a sip of tea.

Mai watched her over the plate. "You don't have to keep it on the spear."

"I know."

"I made it to see if I could."

"You could."

"It wasn't an instruction."

"I know."

Mai looked at Frieren, apparently seeking a less irritating witness. Frieren was looking at the flower Mai had described with the easy attention of someone who had spent a thousand years being interested in objects other people called unnecessary.

"A little lower," she suggested. "Not much. Enough that it clears the cuff."

Mai considered this, then looked back at Maki.

"Bring it tomorrow."

Maki nodded.

Seiko put another cracker on Mai's plate while she was not looking.

* * *

They had been there some time before Seiko asked Frieren about the book.

It was closed beside her cushion, with a clean piece of cloth over the cover. Frieren had brought it because she had been reading before dinner and had not wanted to leave it downstairs where someone would mistake it for an invitation to improve her page markers.

"Any use?" Seiko asked.

"Probably."

"To anyone else?"

"You'd be surprised."

Maki smiled into her tea. "That isn't an answer."

Frieren looked at the tin. "It's a preservation spell."

"For these?"

"Not for food."

Turbo Granny bent over her cracker, suddenly attentive.

"You should lead with that," she said.

"The author does," Frieren said. "Several times."

"An honest author," said Seiko. "I like him."

Frieren picked up the book but did not open it. Her fingers stayed along its edge. She could remember the bench, the cup at Mau's feet, his face when he asked what she was reading. The question had not meant everything she wanted it to mean. It had still been a question he chose to ask.

Mai noticed the pause.

"You look less like you're planning to throw it at someone."

Frieren looked up.

"I wasn't."

"I said less."

Maki set down her cup. She did not fill the space with anything Frieren had not offered.

"We talked," Frieren said. "The other afternoon."

"I saw you go out," Maki said.

"He doesn't remember everything. I know that."

Nobody said *but* for her.

She looked down at the book, then put it back on the floor without opening it.

"I was beginning to think I couldn't say anything to him unless it was important enough to hurt."

Mai's hand stopped over the plate.

"That sounds exhausting."

"It was."

"For you," said Mai. "I'm not offering him an excuse."

Frieren looked at her, and a small smile reached one corner of her mouth.

"I know."

Maki moved her knee, making a little more room beside her cushion. Frieren had not asked for room. She noticed anyway.

"He asked about this," she said, touching the cover. "At the end."

Seiko reached for the kettle. It was too cool now. She made a face at it, put it back and folded both hands round her own cup instead.

"And did you tell him?"

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Not all."

"He escaped, then," Mai said.

Frieren laughed once, very quietly.

* * *

Maki did not tell her what tomorrow would mean.

That was a relief. Frieren had spent enough time trying to interpret other people's gentleness. A cup put down in the right place could become a forecast if she let herself want it badly enough.

She wanted that afternoon to have meant something. She knew wanting did not make her a reliable reader of it.

"I'm pleased," she said at last. It sounded too small when she said it aloud. "And I don't know what I'm allowed to do with that."

"Keep it until tomorrow," Seiko said. "It isn't going off tonight."

"You just said the kettle had."

"The kettle has no sense."

Turbo Granny put a paw on the empty edge of the plate.

"Nobody asks if I'm pleased."

"Are you?" Mai asked.

"I'm cold."

Seiko moved the little cushion nearer the warm side of the room with her foot. Turbo Granny complained through the entire journey and fell silent when it stopped.

Maki watched the cat settle. She had been quieter since Frieren mentioned the book, and Mai looked at her once. Whatever was on her mind, she did not offer a neat answer on behalf of anybody else.

"Yuka needs her cuff fixed again," Maki said after a while. "I think she keeps finding the one thing in a room it can catch on."

"She's gifted," Mai said.

"You told her it would last."

"If she stopped pulling it."

"Ah. Conditions."

Frieren took the next cracker. The conversation went on without asking her to say anything else about hope.

* * *

They had to close the tin before leaving.

This was harder than opening it. Mai tried the lid the way it had come off. Maki turned it a little. Seiko told both of them not to turn it too much. Frieren watched with her hands folded beneath her sleeves.

"You can offer," Maki said.

"She said no."

"She might mean something different now."

Seiko took the tin back.

"I don't."

She pressed down with the heel of one hand and the lid settled into place.

The other three looked at her.

"A lifetime of practice," she said.

Turbo Granny peered over the edge of the table. "You shut it with three still inside."

"Those are for tomorrow."

"I don't know why tomorrow gets a vote."

Maki rose first and offered Seiko her hand, which Seiko ignored until she needed it. Mai picked up the cups. Frieren took the book and the cloth.

At the door she looked back. The tin was on its shelf again, flowers facing outward. The cat sat beside Seiko's cushion, staring at it with undiminished purpose.

"I could still open it," Turbo Granny said.

"Then I'll hear you," Seiko replied.

Mai waited in the corridor with the cups stacked against her chest. She tipped her head toward the book.

"If it preserves anything useful, let me know."

"Define useful."

"My shirt."

Frieren considered her.

"Only if you leave it in the pile long enough."

Mai looked at her for one second, then started down the corridor laughing.
