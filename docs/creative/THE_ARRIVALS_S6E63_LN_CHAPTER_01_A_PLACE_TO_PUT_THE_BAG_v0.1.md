## Chapter 1 — A Place to Put the Bag

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Codex, 2026-10-24)

**Reading order:** 1
**Source episode:** S6E63
**Continuity state:** Old New Days begins with the Mau of the new branch, without conscious access to the original or implanted S6 histories. First awakening remains in the clearing. *When: The first day. Leaves move above a face he does not know.*

Something tickled his cheek.

He tried to brush it away, missed, and opened his eyes.

Hair hung across the sky. Beyond it were branches, a torn piece of cloud, and a woman leaning close enough that he could see a fine crease in her sleeve. She moved the loose strand behind her shoulder.

"Can you hear me?" ᶠ

He nodded. His neck hurt when he did it.

The woman waited until he had worked an elbow beneath himself. She did not pull him up. When he wobbled, her hand came to his shoulder and stayed there until the grass stopped tilting.

"What's your name?" ᶠ

He took a breath to answer.

There was no answer.

He knew what she had asked. Could have explained what a name was, perhaps, if explaining it would have helped. He could feel the beginning of a response in his mouth. Something as ordinary as reaching into a pocket for an object which had always been there.

His hand went to the pocket instead.

Empty.

"I don't know." ᴹ

The woman looked at him more closely.

She asked where he had come from. Whether he remembered arriving. Whether there was anyone she should look for.

Each question made him search a different way. He tried places first. A doorway. The smell of a room. A face turned toward him across a table. He found the idea of those things, a knowledge that they ought to exist, and nothing that belonged to him.

By the last question he had stopped looking at her.

"That's enough for now," she said. ᶠ

He could have thanked her for that. He did not know how to do it without starting to cry, which seemed like a terrible first thing to do in front of a stranger.

Instead he looked at her ears.

They were long. He had noticed them immediately and somehow postponed deciding what to think about them.

She noticed his attention.

"I'm an elf." ᶠ

"Okay." ᴹ

A leaf turned slowly through the space between them and landed on his leg. He stared at it.

"Is that all?" she asked. ᶠ

"I don't know what I'm comparing it to." ᴹ

She considered this. Then she sat back on her heels.

The whole clearing lay in shades he could not have named beyond light and dark. There were differences in everything: the pale underside of the leaf, the deep shadow beneath her knees, the rough mottling of a trunk. He could see the tiny hairs along the stem when he picked it up. The world was very clear.

It felt a little far away.

He rubbed the leaf between his fingers until its sharp green smell rose, though the word came to him more readily than the thing it should have described.

The woman followed his gaze toward a bag lying in the grass.

"That arrived with you." ᶠ

He reached for it too quickly. She caught the strap before it could drag across his wrist, then let him take it.

Opening the zipper helped. His fingers knew the small, practical movement. Inside were clothes, a cap, things packed closely enough to suggest that someone had expected to carry them somewhere. He lifted a jersey out by its shoulders.

Letters crossed the back, above a number.

MAU T.

He said them aloud.

The woman leaned sideways to look.

"Maybe Mau is your name." ᶠ

"Maybe." ᴹ

He waited for the sound to do something to him. It remained a sound. But he could say it when she needed to call him, and that seemed better than making her ask whether he could hear every time.

"I can use that." ᴹ

"I'm Frieren." ᶠ

He repeated her name with the same care.

Frieren reached to adjust something in her hair. Her fingers stopped on it. For a moment her attention left him so completely that he turned, expecting somebody to be standing behind him.

There were only trees.

When he looked back, she was getting to her feet.

"We should find somewhere sheltered." ᶠ

He put the jersey away. He folded it badly, pulled it out, and tried again because it was the only object in the bag which had offered him any information.

Frieren watched without hurrying him.

Something broke across the inside of his head.

A few pieces of a word. An impression of pressure closing over itself. He flinched so hard that the cap fell from his lap.

"What is it?" ᶠ

"Did you hear that?" ᴹ

She stood very still.

The clearing filled with all the sounds he could identify: leaves, a bird, the bag settling against his leg. Whatever had passed through him was gone.

"No," she said. ᶠ

He pressed the heel of his hand against his forehead.

"Something said something. I think." ᴹ

Frieren crouched again. He looked at her hand, expecting her to touch his temple.

Instead a flower opened beside her shoe.

Another followed. Small heads lifted out of the grass in a widening scatter. Mau caught his breath and leaned forward. The shapes were delicate and absurdly definite, petals fitted around their centers, stems bending under a weight which had not existed a moment earlier.

He touched one with a fingertip.

It moved.

"How did you do that?" ᴹ

"Magic." ᶠ

The answer was offered as plainly as her name.

He looked at the flower, then at her. She had no look of expectation, no wish to be applauded. Her eyes were tired. Up close he could see that she had been tired before she found him.

"I don't know what's happening to you," she said. "But I'll help." ᶠ

He nodded. It took him a little while.

When they stood, she waited for him to settle the bag on both shoulders. He had put it on with the zipper half open. She pointed at it; he closed it; they started through the trees.

* * *

By evening he knew that Frieren disliked being interrupted while inspecting a door.

He had interrupted twice.

On the third door he stood quietly behind her until she lowered her staff.

"This one is a door," she said. ᶠ

"I'm glad." ᴹ

She looked over her shoulder at him.

"Some things pretend." ᶠ

He glanced at the other doors along the corridor. After a day containing an elf and flowers made out of nothing, he was unwilling to make an argument on behalf of doors.

The building smelled of old soot and wet timber. Downstairs, a long table stood crooked on a floor which had lost a board near the wall. There had been a hearth, a kitchen, rooms people had paid to sleep in. The roof above the part they had chosen still held.

Frieren tested the window latch in the first room, then the bed. A cloud of dust rose from the blanket. She stepped back before it reached her face.

Mau sneezed.

"That one can be yours." ᶠ

"Because I sneezed on it?" ᴹ

"Because the window closes." ᶠ

She chose the room beside it.

He set his bag on the bed, thought better of it, and moved it to a dry patch of floor. Frieren watched him test the boards with a foot.

"I don't want it getting wet," he explained. ᴹ

She nodded as though this made perfect sense.

They ate a little of what they could find and make safe. He asked what they would do in the morning. She said they would look at the rest of the building, and then the surrounding ground. Her attention went toward the window again.

"Are you looking for someone?" ᴹ

The spoon stopped in her hand.

He was about to apologize when she answered.

"Fern." ᶠ

He waited. The name had changed her voice so slightly that he could not have explained the difference.

"If somebody comes," he said, "we can ask." ᴹ

Frieren looked down at her bowl.

"Yes." ᶠ

Later, he stood in the corridor with the blanket she had found in a cupboard. His room was dark. There was a room on either side and the unfamiliar staircase beyond, and for a moment he could not persuade his feet to take him into any of it.

Frieren had her hand on her own door.

"Is something wrong?" ᶠ

He looked at the blanket.

"If I wake up and forget this too—" ᴹ

He had not intended to say it.

Frieren let go of the latch.

"Your bag will be there," she said. "The jersey has your name on it." ᶠ

"Maybe my name." ᴹ

"The one you're using." ᶠ

That was true. He had chosen to use it.

"And you'll be next door?" ᴹ

"Yes." ᶠ

He looked at the short strip of floor between their doors. Measured it with his eyes.

"Okay. Good night, Frieren." ᴹ

"Good night, Mau." ᶠ

He went inside, placed the bag where he could reach it from the bed, and left his shoes pointing toward the door.

Before he lay down, he pulled the jersey out again.

He folded it over the back of the chair, with the letters facing him.
