## Chapter 5 — Will You Hold Her?

*When: The stream is making too much noise for him to hold a thought in one piece.*

The woman with the white hair had given him water.

He remembered that because the cup was still in his hand. It had been warm when she passed it over, although the water inside was cold. He could remember another warm thing she had said, an instruction to sit down before she had to make him, and the quick irritation in her face when he apologized for making trouble.

He could not remember her name.

"Minerva," she said when he asked again. ᴹⁿ

He repeated it.

"Yes. Still." ᴹⁿ

She was not angry that he had forgotten. She was angry at something which had happened to him. He could tell those apart when he concentrated.

Behind them, through the open door of the shelter, another woman was arguing about whether questions were useful when every answer changed. Mau had answered some of them that morning. He had been sure he lived near the coast until someone asked him which coast. Then he had remembered a road instead.

The road had been wrong too.

Minerva looked toward the door and raised her voice.

"We're walking as far as the bend. That's all." ᴹⁿ

"I heard you," a voice answered. ᴱᶜʰ

"Then stop asking whether I'm sure." ᴹⁿ

Mau looked into the cup. The water shook a little. He could not tell whether it had done that before he started watching.

"You don't have to take me," he said. ᴹ

Minerva looked down at him.

"I know." ᴹⁿ

She waited while he got to his feet. His ankle hurt. A shallow scratch had dried beside the top of his shoe, and his hands were marked from branches he could not remember pushing aside. She had checked the injuries already. Whatever she could help with had not fixed the place inside his head where one day kept folding into another.

They walked along the stream.

He tried to notice things without requiring them to tell him who he was. A stone with a crack down the middle. The dark weight of water below a root. Three fallen leaves caught against the same stick.

A voice kept trying to assemble itself behind those observations. It had no direction he could turn toward. Sometimes it seemed as if there were too many things to look at; sometimes he lost the few he had managed to find.

Minerva shortened her stride to match his.

"That woman," he said. "The one I told you about." ᴹ

"Frieren?" ᴹⁿ

The name made him stop.

For a second he had a picture of a bed beside a window. A folded sleeve on a chair. He reached for the rest of the room and it receded.

"Is that what I said?" ᴹ

"Yes." ᴹⁿ

"Okay." ᴹ

He held the name as carefully as he held the cup.

At the bend there was a child.

She stood on the far side of a thin run of water, with one foot on a stone and the other drawn back. She was looking down at the stream as though she had intended to cross and had discovered, too late, that water continued moving while you made up your mind.

Mau stopped so abruptly that Minerva turned toward him.

The girl looked up.

Her clothes were too clean for the path. Her hands were empty. There was nobody on the bank behind her.

"Hello," Mau said. ᴹ

She looked at Minerva and then back at him.

"Are you lost?" he asked. ᴹ

It occurred to him, after he said it, that he might not be qualified to begin this conversation.

The girl nodded.

Minerva put a hand in front of Mau when he started toward the water.

"Your ankle." ᴹⁿ

"It's not deep." ᴹ

"That's not what I said." ᴹⁿ

She crossed first. The child watched her coming, then stepped backward with both feet together. Minerva stopped an arm's length away and crouched, her hands where the girl could see them.

Mau put the cup down. He wanted his hands free, though he did not know for what.

"What's your name?" he asked across the stream. ᴹ

"Ori." ᴼ

He liked the certainty of it.

"I'm Mau." ᴹ

The name was there when he needed it. He was surprised enough to smile.

Ori looked at his shoes.

"You're wet." ᴼ

"A little." ᴹ

"Did you fall in?" ᴼ

"I don't remember." ᴹ

She frowned at that. He understood the expression. He had seen it on nearly everybody who asked him something.

Minerva let the child take her hand. She carried her across the narrow water, set her down on the near bank, and looked back along the path.

"We should get inside," she said. ᴹⁿ

Mau bent to pick up the cup.

When he straightened, somebody said his name.

He turned toward the sound before he knew whose voice it was.

* * *

Frieren came down the bank with Yuta behind her.

Mau recognized the long ears first. Then the staff. The face was familiar in a way that hurt to try to understand. She stopped several paces away and watched him look at her.

Yuta's breath was coming hard. He had a branch caught in the edge of his jacket and did not appear to have noticed it.

"We found you," he said. ʸᵗ

Mau looked from one to the other.

"Frieren?" ᴹ

She nodded.

The relief on her face made him ashamed of the question.

"I'm sorry. I keep—" ᴹ

"You don't need to explain it now." ᶠ

Minerva stepped close enough to tell them where they had found him, how long he had been awake, what he had managed to eat. Frieren listened without taking her attention entirely off him. Yuta asked whether anyone else had been with him.

Mau tried to follow all the words. The account had him in it, but felt as if it concerned someone walking a little way ahead, always disappearing around the next bend before Mau could catch up.

A small hand closed around the loose fabric by his knee.

He looked down.

Ori was staring at the people who had arrived. Her fingers had gathered enough cloth to hold tightly without touching him.

"This is Ori," he said. ᴹ

Frieren lowered her eyes.

"We found her there." ᴹ

He pointed to the other bank. Minerva confirmed it. Yuta looked across the water and then uphill, searching for a sign of anybody who might have left her.

Ori held on harder when Frieren crouched.

"Hello, Ori." ᶠ

The child looked at her ears.

Frieren waited. After a while, Ori looked at her face instead.

"Are you his?" Ori asked. ᴼ

Mau opened his mouth, uncertain what answer belonged to the question.

"We're looking for him," Frieren said. ᶠ

Ori thought about that.

"You found him." ᴼ

"Yes." ᶠ

The child glanced up at Mau. He managed a smile. The name Frieren was beginning to slip away from the person in front of him again, and he concentrated on keeping them together.

"We should take her too," he said. ᴹ

"We will." ᶠ

He was glad of the answer, and immediately uncertain what to do next. He had thought that finding someone who knew the way home would make the next movements obvious. His bag was back at the shelter. He had no idea whether he had promised something to the women there. The cup was still in his hand, and it belonged to somebody.

He looked down at Ori. She was waiting for him to decide.

"Will you hold her?" he asked Frieren. ᴹ

Frieren looked at the child's grip on his trousers.

"Would you like that?" she asked Ori. ᶠ

Ori considered the question with a seriousness Mau could not have improved on.

Then she let go of his clothes.

Frieren put her staff within Yuta's reach and lifted her. The movement was careful at first; Ori had kept her knees stiff, unsure where to put them. Frieren adjusted her arm under the child's weight.

"There," she said. ᶠ

Ori set one hand on her shoulder. With the other, she touched the end of a strand of hair.

Mau watched until Yuta moved beside him.

"We have to go back for your things," Yuta said. "Then we're going home. You can lean on me." ʸᵗ

Mau nodded.

He looked at the woman holding Ori once more.

"Frieren," he said, trying it. ᴹ

She turned immediately.

"Yes." ᶠ

He did not have another question. He just needed to know she would answer.

They walked back along the water. Yuta took the cup when Mau began to spill it. Ahead of them, Ori rested her cheek against Frieren's shoulder, watching the stream recede behind them.
