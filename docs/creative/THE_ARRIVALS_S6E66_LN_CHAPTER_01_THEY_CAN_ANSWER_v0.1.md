# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 35
## Chapter 7 — They Can Answer

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Codex, 2026-10-24)
**Volume:** 35
**Reading order:** 7
**Source episode:** S6E66
**Continuity state:** Branch late S3 Trial, substantially different and milder than the original. Goddess is explicitly the author; Mau is tested over finite practical scenarios without deaths, centuries, wedding trauma or inherited original Trial memories. He learns to ask and distribute responsibility. The final scene opens the ten-day personal Return/Stay choice. After individual decisions, one coin is offered per stayer, including Mau in this branch: the causal basis for later arrivals and his gift to Kaneki. A coin requests a willing person's arrival; no forced summons or promise that every person can be reached. This branch-specific reward does not claim the original long-run gift had this same provenance. Ciel is private and present where admitted by the Trial; nobody else automatically hears her. Gray perception continues.

*When: Mau has carried the same bucket across the same floor four times, and the Goddess is waiting for him to notice why.*

The bucket leaked from the side nearest his leg.

He had turned it around on the second trip. That had kept his trousers dry and made the puddle larger where he had to walk. On the third he had attempted to plug the hole with the rag left beside the pump.

On the fourth he put the bucket down.

"Can I have a different one?" ᴹ

The room gave him no answer.

It was a small room, compared with what he had expected a trial made by a goddess to contain. A pump, a stove, a table with dishes, a door he could not open until he completed the work. Beyond the far window, rain threatened a row of seedlings in pots. Water had to reach one set of things and be kept away from another.

Every time he attended to one task, he lost ground on the others.

He had expected a creature. A dangerous place. An opponent he could recognize.

Instead he was beginning to hate a bucket.

"There is another person in the room," the Goddess said. ᴰⁱᵒ

Mau looked toward the chair beside the door.

A woman sat there with a towel in her lap. She had been there since he arrived. She had greeted him, asked whether he wanted anything, and watched him refuse help because the instructions had said *complete the work* and he had assumed the work had to be his.

She looked quite bored now.

"Can you hold that over the hole?" he asked her. ᴹ

She got up immediately.

It was so easy that he was irritated.

Then embarrassed.

"Thank you," he added. ᴹ

They crossed the room together. He filled the pot while she steadied the bucket, and afterward she took it to the pump while he moved the seedlings out of the rain. There was enough time to do both. There had been enough time for a while.

The door opened when the last pot was safe.

Mau looked toward it, then at the woman.

"Are you coming?" ᴹ

She smiled and disappeared with the room.

He found himself standing in a corridor with a damp mark on one trouser leg and the strong suspicion that the Goddess was enjoying herself.

"Was that really the test?" ᴹ

"You made it last much longer than it needed to." ᴰⁱᵒ

He could not think of a response which improved his position.

* * *

There were three rooms altogether.

The second contained people preparing to leave a place together. Mau was given a bag of provisions and asked to make sure everyone had what they needed. He counted people, counted portions, checked straps, and nearly sent a woman out without her walking stick because she had put it somewhere he did not think to look.

She told him before they reached the door.

He went back for it.

The room remained while he stood beside her, expecting to be told he had failed.

"Is that all right?" he asked. ᴹ

She adjusted the strap on her own bag.

"I told you before we went." ᵂ

He looked at the small group. Nobody was in danger. Nobody had been quietly sacrificed to teach him the importance of checking pockets. The mistake had been an actual mistake, and someone had helped correct it.

He began asking the others to check what he had packed for them.

By the time they left, several things had moved between bags. They knew how they preferred to carry their own belongings. He had known the total weight and very little else.

The third room looked enough like the common room to make his throat tighten.

There were chairs at the table. Familiar people sat in them. Frieren had a book under one hand. Rimuru was leaning back with his arms folded. Fern had stopped writing. Ori sat close to Frieren with her feet tucked up on the chair.

"They aren't here," Ciel said privately. ᶜ

"I know," he answered. ᴹ

Knowing did not stop him wanting to go to them.

The Goddess spoke from somewhere beyond the room.

"You may answer for them. If they remain here, the house remains with them. If they return, I will send them to the lives from which they came." ᴰⁱᵒ

Mau looked at the people at the table.

They were all waiting for him.

He understood at once which answer he wanted. The speed of it frightened him more than the rooms had.

"What do they want?" ᴹ

"You can decide." ᴰⁱᵒ

He looked at Yuta's chair. At the empty place beside it where Maki might sit. At the small body beside Frieren.

He had things he could say about each of them. Reasons he thought they had settled, people he thought they had found, dangers he thought waited in the worlds behind them. He knew some of what hurt. He knew almost nothing about the shape a whole other life might take when it called someone back.

"They can answer," he said. ᴹ

The room waited.

He took one step closer to the table.

"You have to ask them." ᴹ

Ori's imitation turned toward him. It was a little too obedient in the way it watched, and that helped him keep the next words steady.

"I'd like them to stay. That's my answer. It can't be theirs." ᴹ

The people vanished.

For a second he thought he had done something wrong.

Then a door opened behind him and he heard somebody swearing about how long he had been in there.

Anko.

He turned so fast that Ciel told him to slow down.

* * *

It was evening outside.

Mau had gone in before lunch. Someone had saved him a portion and then replaced it with supper when the waiting ran long. The bowl was under a cloth on the table nearest the entrance, still warm enough that steam rose when he lifted the cover.

He looked up at the people standing around him.

Actual faces were harder to bear for a moment. They moved before he looked at them. They interrupted one another. Frieren took Ori's hand off the hot side of the bowl without waiting for anyone to notice. Yuta asked whether Mau was hurt while Maki asked why nobody would give them a straight answer about the time.

"I'm okay," Mau said. ᴹ

Maomao made him sit before accepting the statement.

He was tired, hungry, and had a blister on one palm where he had carried the leaking bucket too long. That was what she found when she checked. No other injuries. The pressure in his head was where it had been before he went in. Ciel confirmed the limits of what she could observe without claiming to know every part of the Goddess's room.

Ori came to inspect his hand.

"What did you do?" ᴼ

"Carried water badly." ᴹ

She looked disappointed.

"Did you win?" ᴼ

He considered it.

"Eventually I asked somebody to help." ᴹ

Ori looked up at Frieren, as if checking whether that counted.

"That was sensible," Frieren said. ᶠ

Rimuru sat on the corner of a bench. He had been listening closely.

"And the rest?" ᴿ

Mau set the spoon down.

"She offered a choice. Stay or go back. I told her she had to ask you." ᴹ

It quieted the people nearest him.

Anko came to stand on the other side of the table. Fern's pen stopped. Someone farther back asked what he meant, and he explained it again, plainly, because it was too important to leave half-heard at the edge of a room.

The Goddess answered the next question herself.

Everybody heard her this time.

Ten days to decide. Each person's answer would be their own. Staying would carry the offer of a coin: a request for someone they wished to bring, if that person could be reached and chose to come. No one had to use it immediately.

Mau put his blistered hand flat beside his bowl.

He had spent part of the afternoon believing that the easiest thing would be to know everybody's answer before asking.

Across the table, Yuta had gone very still.

Mau left him his silence.
