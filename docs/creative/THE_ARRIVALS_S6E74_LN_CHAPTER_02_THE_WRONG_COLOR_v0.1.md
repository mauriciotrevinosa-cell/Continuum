Yuka woke because the hand beneath hers moved.

She had fallen asleep with her head against the back of the chair. Her neck ached. One leg had gone numb enough that she could not immediately put her foot where she meant to.

Mau was looking toward the window.

For a second she knew only that his eyes were open.

"Mau?" ʸᵏ

He turned toward the sound. The light caught his face.

His eyes were blue.

Yuka forgot the sentence she had been about to say. It was such a clear, impossible difference that she looked once toward the window, trying to find something in the room which could have put the color there.

When she looked back, it remained.

His brow tightened. He shut his eyes again and turned his face away from the light.

"Shoko," Yuka said. ʸᵏ

The name came out quietly. She tried again, louder, and the movement at the other side of the room answered her.

Shoko came to the bed, saw his face, and moved the curtain before asking him to look at anything else.

"Mau. Can you hear me?" ˢʰ

He opened his eyes a little.

Yuka watched Shoko notice.

That was worse than noticing by herself. Until then she had been able to keep a small, useless possibility that she had seen it wrongly. Shoko's expression took that away without giving her anything to replace it with.

"Where—" Mau said. ᴹ

"In the clinic. You lost consciousness last night." ˢʰ

He looked toward Yuka.

For one moment he seemed to recognize her completely. His hand tightened, and his mouth moved in a shape she knew from a hundred ordinary mornings.

Then he looked at the blanket as though it belonged somewhere he could not find.

"What day?" ᴹ

"Four hundred and seventy-six," Shoko told him. ˢʰ

He repeated part of the number without finishing it.

Yuka leaned nearer, careful not to come between Shoko and the bed.

"You were talking with Grandpa. He caught you. You're here." ʸᵏ

Mau looked at her hand on his.

His eyes closed again.

She waited for them to open. Shoko asked another question. The answer came as a breath, too quiet to hold a word, and he drifted away before either could reach the end of it.

Yuka sat back slowly.

The flower at her wrist had turned toward her palm. She put it right with the other hand, an action so familiar that it nearly undid her.

* * *

Gojo came before breakfast was finished elsewhere in the house.

Shoko told him what had changed without starting with what she feared it might mean. Ciel had already told Rimuru, who arrived behind him and stayed near the door until someone made room.

When Mau opened his eyes at Shoko's next attempt, Gojo leaned forward.

He did not ask him to hold the look longer once the discomfort became visible.

"Leave the curtain there," he said. ᴳ

Shoko looked at him.

"There's a filtering behavior. Not just the color. Something is trying to manage more information than it was managing yesterday." ᴳ

"Trying," she repeated. ˢʰ

"I didn't say doing it well." ᴳ

He stood back from the bed. For a moment his own eyes were uncovered, and Yuka could see why the resemblance frightened him even while he refused to turn it into a diagnosis.

"Can you help it?" she asked. ʸᵏ

Gojo looked toward her.

"I can help make the room less work for him. I can't take control of something I haven't understood because it looks familiar." ᴳ

Yuka nodded, unhappy with the answer and glad he had not given her a prettier one.

They made the room easier. Less direct light. Fewer people standing in the line of his sight when Shoko tried to wake him. Questions given one at a time. Ciel compared what changed with what remained, and Rimuru repeated the useful parts aloud.

The eye color did not go back.

Mau slept through most of the adjustments.

* * *

Fern brought food to the little table outside the clinic.

She did not set it in the room as an instruction for a frightened person to perform eating while someone unconscious lay two feet away. She put it where they could step out, wash their hands, and be people for a few minutes.

Yuka came out when Shoko said it was a good time.

Frieren was at the table with Ori.

The child had a piece of paper folded badly in both hands. Frieren held a cup away from the edge while Ori worked with great concentration on making the sides meet.

Yuka sat down opposite them.

"He woke a little," she said. ʸᵏ

Ori looked up immediately.

"Can he have breakfast?" ᴼ

"Not yet." ʸᵏ

Ori glanced toward her mother.

Frieren touched the paper.

"We can leave this for when he's ready." ᶠ

The child nodded, though she clearly disliked waiting for an audience to become available.

Yuka looked at Frieren. The older woman had heard the practical account from Fern. Her expression had remained quiet through it, the way it did when a problem had stopped being something she could solve by looking harder at it.

"His eyes changed," Yuka said. ʸᵏ

Frieren looked toward the door.

"Fern told me." ᶠ

"Does that mean anything to you?" ʸᵏ

Frieren took a moment.

"I remember what they looked like when he woke in the clearing. I remember how little mana I could find. I've learned that remembering what was there doesn't tell me everything that can happen to him." ᶠ

Yuka looked down at the food.

"I keep wanting somebody to recognize it." ʸᵏ

"I know." ᶠ

There was nothing competitive in the answer, no claim to have known him first and therefore to be the one who understood him best. Frieren sat with the same unanswered question and a child pulling at her sleeve to show the paper was finally folded.

She looked at the fold.

"That's better." ᶠ

Ori brightened.

Yuka picked up her spoon.

* * *

The morning went on in pieces.

People who needed to know heard that he was still breathing, still feverish, still not properly awake. The people who did not need to stand outside the clinic went to the work they had already agreed to do. Nobody called that forgetting him.

Yuta helped carry water and stayed for the next waking attempt. Maki brought clean clothes for Yuka without saying that sitting in yesterday's clothes proved anything about love. Mai came as far as the table, asked her own questions, and left with answers which did not satisfy her any more than they satisfied anybody else.

Later, Yuka saw the folded paper beside Mau's bed.

Ori had drawn a cup, a bowl, and a face which looked almost as startled as the one on the birthday bookmark. Under them, with help, she had put his name.

Mau did not wake to see it.

Yuka moved it where he would not have to turn toward the window when he did.

When his eyes opened briefly in the afternoon, they were still blue. She said his name, told him where he was, and watched him try to bring the room into focus.

His gaze reached the paper.

One corner of his mouth moved.

It was almost a smile.
