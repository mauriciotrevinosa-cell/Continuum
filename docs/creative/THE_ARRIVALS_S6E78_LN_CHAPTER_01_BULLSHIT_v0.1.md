# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 36
## Chapter 1 — Bullshit

**Volume:** 36

**Continuity state:** Day479, late morning, the clinic, continuous from V35#29. Shoko gives the lucid Mau a full examination with his consent: blue eyes, white left temple, two stiff present left fingers, soft left visual edge, the closed torso scar at the original Sukuna site and many fine closed marks, none of which this branch's history explains. Mau knows each limit and works around it without thinking; asked, he says "This is normal," and Shoko says "That's bullshit." *When: Day479, late morning. The curtain is still brown, the leaves outside still green. Yuka has been sent to eat and has come back with half a piece of bread, which she is pretending is all of it.*

Shoko did not let him get out of bed to be examined.

She examined him where he lay, which in Mau's experience was what Shoko did when she wanted to see the whole problem before the patient started arranging it for her.

"Hands first," she said. ˢʰ

He gave her the right one.

"Both." ˢʰ

He gave her the left.

She held it in both of hers and turned it over, slowly, the way she had turned it over in another clinic in another life, after a hive. She pressed the base of each finger with her thumb. Three of them curled when she asked. Two went as far as they had gone for months, and stopped.

"Does that hurt?" ˢʰ

"No." ᴹ

"When did it stop hurting?" ˢʰ

He opened his mouth. The true answer was *in the summer, a while after the dragon*. The other true answer was *it never hurt, because it never happened*. Both of them were his.

"A while ago," he said. ᴹ

Shoko looked at him over the hand.

She wrote something down.

* * *

She went over all of him.

Not quickly. Shoko never examined anyone quickly; she said speed was how you missed the thing that killed them later. She looked into his eyes with the small lamp and he let her, and when the light was too much he did the thing he had always done, narrowed the opening behind his eyes by a degree until the brightness was something he could hold. She saw his pupils change before the lamp moved.

"You just did something," she said. ˢʰ

"It was too bright." ᴹ

"I know it was too bright. I'm asking what you did about it." ˢʰ

"I made it quieter." ᴹ

She put the lamp down.

Then she lifted the blanket from his chest, with his nod, and looked at the place where yesterday there had been nothing.

It was not a wound. It had never been a wound, not here. It was a closed, puckered, silvered mark below his ribs, old as if it had been healing for a year and more, with smaller lines around it like cracks round a stone dropped into ice. Shoko did not touch it at first. She looked at it the way he had once seen her look at something she could not afford to be wrong about.

Yuka, in the chair, had put the bread down.

"That wasn't there the night he fell," Shoko said. Not to anyone. To the room. To her own ledger, which lay open on the table with yesterday's drawing of his chest in it, blank where this was. ˢʰ

"I know," said Mau. ᴹ

"You know." ˢʰ

"I know what made it." ᴹ

Shoko looked up.

He held her eyes. He made himself.

"A hand," he said. "A long time ago. Not here." ᴹ

The clinic was very quiet. Outside the half-open door somebody laughed in the kitchen and was told to be quiet, and stopped.

Shoko put the blanket back over him.

* * *

The rest went the same way.

The fine lines over his forearms and his throat and the sides of his face, a thousand of them, thin and pale, as if he had once walked through rain made of knives. She asked about them and he said *the same time as the hands, more or less*, and she wrote that down too. The white at his left temple, which had come in over two days like frost coming in over a window. The way he turned his head a little farther than he needed to when she held up her fingers at the left edge of his sight, so that the whole of them came into view.

"You're compensating," she said. ˢʰ

"I've had practice." ᴹ

"You haven't." ˢʰ

She said it plainly. Not angry. She said it the way she would have said *your temperature is down*.

"Four days ago you could see out of the left side of that eye like anybody else. Four days ago your fingers closed. Four days ago you didn't have this." She tapped her own chest, below the ribs, in the place where the mark was on his. "And now you've got all of it, and you're managing all of it like a man who's been living with it for a year." ˢʰ

Mau looked at the blanket.

"It's normal," he said. ᴹ

He did not mean it as a lie. He meant *for me it is*. He meant *I know what to do with all of this, you don't need to be frightened of it*. He said it the way you say *I'm fine* when you mean *please don't look at me like that*.

Shoko closed her ledger.

"That's bullshit," she said. ˢʰ

Yuka made a small sound that might in another week have been a laugh.

Shoko did not look at her. She looked at Mau. She had brought the coffee with her, the way she always did, and she picked it up now and held it in both hands, and did not drink it.

"I've been a doctor a long time," she said. "In two worlds. I've seen people come back with things missing. I've seen people come back with things they didn't leave with. I have never once seen anybody grow an old scar overnight." She put the cup down. "So don't tell me it's normal. Tell me it's normal *for you*, if that's what you mean. Tell me you don't want to talk about it yet. Tell me anything true." She leaned forward. "But don't sit in my clinic and tell me that's normal and expect me to write it down." ˢʰ

Mau looked at her for a long time.

"It's normal for me," he said. ᴹ

"Better." ˢʰ

"I don't want to talk about all of it yet." ᴹ

"Also better." ˢʰ

"And I'm not lying to you. I remember every one of them." He looked at his left hand on the blanket. "Where I got them. What it felt like. I just—" ᴹ

He stopped.

"Got them somewhere else," said Rimuru, quietly, from the stool by the wall. ᴿ

Everyone looked at him.

He had not said anything since he sat down. He was looking at Mau with an expression Mau knew from two lives and could not quite read in either.

"Yes," said Mau. ᴹ

* * *

Gojo came because Shoko asked Mau first, and Mau said yes.

He came into the clinic with his hands in his pockets and his blindfold down and stood at the foot of the bed for a moment as if he had walked into the wrong room. In this life Gojo had been many things to Mau. A teacher in the yard, for a while. The loud man on the roof. Somebody who had looked at him once, after the Trial, with bare eyes, and said *huh*, and nothing else.

In the other life—

Mau did not let himself finish the thought. Not yet.

"Shoko says something happened to your eyes," said Gojo. ᴳ

"They changed colour." ᴹ

"I heard." Gojo tilted his head. "Mind if I look?" ᴳ

"Go ahead." ᴹ

Gojo pushed the blindfold up.

He looked for a long time. Longer than he usually looked at anything. Mau held still, and then, because he knew what it was like to be looked at by those eyes, he let the opening behind his own eyes widen a fraction, just enough to look back.

Gojo went very still.

The clinic disappeared, for a heartbeat. Not into darkness. Into everything. The weave of the blanket, the grain of the wood, Yuka's pulse in the vein at her wrist and the cord of the bracelet against it, Rimuru's shape in the corner that was not a shape at all but a slow vast patience folded small. And Gojo. Gojo standing at the foot of the bed, a thing of light, looking at him.

Mau closed it again. Slowly. Degree by degree.

His nose was bleeding a little. He felt it start.

Shoko already had a cloth in her hand.

"Okay," said Gojo. Very quietly. ᴳ

He did not put the blindfold back down. He stood with his hands in his pockets and looked at Mau, and his face had done something it almost never did.

"That's mine," said Gojo. ᴳ

Nobody said anything.

"Not mine. Not exactly." He frowned. "It's a copy. There's a filter in there, something built round it to keep the flood out. Somebody made it carefully. Very carefully. The way you'd make it if you'd watched what happens to someone who doesn't have one." He looked at Mau. "I didn't make that." ᴳ

"No," said Mau. ᴹ

"Who did?" ᴳ

Mau thought about Ciel. Another Ciel, in another life, building walls round a flood so a boy would not drown in his own eyes. Building them over and over, every time something broke them.

The Ciel in him now had never had to.

"Someone who loved me," he said. ᴹ

Gojo looked at him a moment longer.

Then he put the blindfold down.

"Okay," he said again. He looked at Shoko. "His eyes are fine. Fine for what they are. I don't know what they are." He looked back at Mau. "Don't open them all the way again until you're stronger. That cost you more than you think." ᴳ

"I know." ᴹ

"Yeah," said Gojo, after a moment. "I think you do." ᴳ

He went out.

* * *

When he was gone, Shoko sat down on her stool and looked at Mau for a while without writing anything.

"I'm going to ask you something," she said. "And I want you to answer it like I'm your doctor, not like I'm somebody you're trying to keep from worrying." ˢʰ

"Okay." ᴹ

"Are you in danger?" ˢʰ

He thought about it honestly.

His body was weak, the fever only just gone. His hands were what they were. His eyes would hurt if he was careless. There were two hearts in him and he knew, now, roughly what the second one was, and what it had done the last time something asked it to hold more than it should.

And there were three lives in his head that did not fit into one past, and he did not yet know what that would do to him on a bad night.

"Not today," he said. ᴹ

Shoko heard what he did not say.

"And tomorrow?" ˢʰ

"I don't know." ᴹ

She nodded slowly.

"That's the first thing you've said this morning that sounded like a patient." ˢʰ

* * *

He had meant to say it then. Something. A beginning. He could feel all of it pressing behind his teeth: the yes in a dark room, the girl asleep, the black and the sound like a word dragged through a broken speaker. The faces in this house who had lived a whole life with him in it, and did not know it.

"I'll tell you," he said. "All of it. Everyone. I promise—" ᴹ

He stopped.

The word sat in his mouth.

He had promised a great many things. In another life, in a room under a ridge, he had written *I told you first* on a sheet of paper and left it on a shelf of blankets. In a garden of white stone he had promised a girl he would bring her home. In a dark room he had promised Rimuru, in everything but words, that he would bring back the answer before he chose.

And then he had chosen.

Rimuru, by the wall, had gone still.

Mau looked at his hands.

"I'll tell you," he said again, more carefully. "I want to. I just need a little time to find where it starts. Not a long time." ᴹ

He did not say *I promise*.

Shoko looked at him. Then at Rimuru. Then back.

"A little time," she said. "And you stay in that bed for it." ˢʰ

"Okay." ᴹ

"And the next time I ask you if something's normal—" ˢʰ

"I'll say *for me*." ᴹ

"Good." ˢʰ

She picked up her coffee at last, and drank it, and made a face because it had gone cold an hour ago.

* * *

Yuka had not said anything through all of it.

She sat in the chair with the half piece of bread forgotten on her knee and her hands holding each other in her lap, the flower at her wrist turned inward against her palm.

When Shoko went out to write up her notes and Rimuru went with her, saying he would bring tea, Yuka stayed.

She looked at Mau for a long time.

"You knew," she said at last. "Every one of them. When she asked." ʸᵏ

"Yes." ᴹ

"But you didn't have them. Four days ago. I was here." Her voice wavered and she stopped it. "I watched them come." ʸᵏ

"I know." ᴹ

She looked down at her hands.

"Is it going to hurt you?" ʸᵏ

"Telling you?" ᴹ

"Any of it." ʸᵏ

He looked at her. This Yuka. Who had sat by his bed four nights, who had learned where it was safe to put her hand on a blanket, who had a flower on her wrist that a boy had bought her from Hollis without knowing what it meant.

"Some of it," he said. ᴹ

She nodded as if she had expected that.

Then she picked up the bread again, and broke it in half, and held one half out to him.

"Then eat something first," she said. ʸᵏ

He took it.

It was the most ordinary thing anyone had done for him all morning. His throat closed round it before he could get it down.
