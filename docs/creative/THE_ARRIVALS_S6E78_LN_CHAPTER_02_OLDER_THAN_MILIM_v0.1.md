# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 36
## Chapter 2 — Older Than Milim

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S6 v0.1 (Claude, 2026-10-24)
**Volume:** 36
**Reading order:** 2
**Source episode:** S6E78
**Continuity state:** Day479, afternoon, the clinic. Rimuru brings tea and stays; Yuka has gone to wash and rest at Shoko's insistence. This branch's Rimuru knows the Mau/Yuka romance since Day448, admitted it on Day475, and has unresolved friction with Yuta over that silence. Mau thanks him for having told him, in this life, to tell Yuta and Maki. Asked how old he really is, Mau laughs and says "older than Milim, you're the one who worked it out", and this Rimuru never did; the original Rimuru's explanation belongs to a life this one did not live. Mau gives him the outline: another life in this house went much worse; in that life his Trial was long and killed him many times; this branch's Trial was an afternoon. No exact count is pressed on Rimuru, no confession to the house, no Window, shadows, weapons or Goddess. Rimuru says he will stand with him when he tells them. Ciel listens privately and says nothing until the end

*When: Day479, afternoon. Rimuru has brought two cups of tea, one of them too full, and is walking very slowly so as not to spill it.*

He spilled it anyway.

Not much. A dark ring on the clinic floor by the door, and a little more on his sleeve, and Rimuru stood looking at it as if it had personally betrayed him.

"Shoko's going to see that," he said. ᴿ

"She sees everything," said Mau. ᴹ

"I know. That's the problem." ᴿ

He put both cups on the table beside the bed, the fuller one closer to Mau, and sat down on the stool by the wall with his wet sleeve held away from himself, and for a little while neither of them said anything.

It was a good silence. Mau had forgotten, somewhere in four days of fever, how good a silence with Rimuru could be. In both lives Rimuru had been the person he could sit beside without having to make the silence into anything.

He reached for the cup with his right hand, and steadied it with the two fingers of his left that still did what he asked, and drank.

Rimuru watched him do it.

* * *

"Yuka's gone to wash," Rimuru said eventually. "Shoko made her. She said if Yuka fell asleep in that chair one more night she'd have two patients." ᴿ

"Good." ᴹ

"She didn't want to go." ᴿ

"I know." ᴹ

Rimuru turned his own cup in his hands.

"She thinks you're different," he said. ᴿ

Mau did not answer at once.

"I am different," he said. ᴹ

"I know. I can see it." Rimuru looked at the floor. At the dark ring of tea. "Ciel says you're coherent. Shoko says your body is yours, just more of it than there was. Gojo says your eyes are a copy of his with a wall built round them by someone who loved you." He lifted his head. "And you say you've got scars from somewhere else." ᴿ

"Yes." ᴹ

"I'm not going to push," said Rimuru. "I said I'd wait and I will. I'm just telling you what it looks like from this stool." ᴿ

"What does it look like?" ᴹ

Rimuru thought about it.

"Like someone came back from a very long way away," he said. ᴿ

Mau held the cup in both hands.

* * *

"I owe you something," he said. ᴹ

Rimuru looked up.

"After the gate. The small one. When I told you about Yuka." Mau looked at the tea. "You said I should tell Yuta and Maki. You said you wouldn't lie for me, but you wouldn't tell them either, because it was mine to tell." ᴹ

"I remember." ᴿ

"I didn't do it." ᴹ

"I know." ᴿ

"And then Milim walked in, and they found out the worst way they could, and you had to stand in that room and say you'd known for weeks." Mau put the cup down. "That was my fault. Not yours. I want you to know I know that." ᴹ

Rimuru was quiet for a long moment.

"Yuta's still angry with me," he said. ᴿ

"I know." ᴹ

"He said I let *soon* go on forever." Rimuru's mouth went crooked. "He's right. I did." ᴿ

"You told me to tell them," said Mau. "In—" ᴹ

He stopped.

*In both lives*, he had been going to say. Because in the other life, too, Rimuru had sat across a room from him, and refused to lie, and asked him to stay with them while it was difficult. And Mau had not.

"You told me," he said instead. ᴹ

Rimuru looked at him strangely.

"You were going to say something else." ᴿ

"Yes." ᴹ

"Is it part of the long way away?" ᴿ

"Yes." ᴹ

Rimuru nodded slowly and let it go.

* * *

They drank the tea.

Outside the half-open door the house went about its afternoon. Somebody was beating a rug in the yard, rhythmically, with the kind of fury that suggested the rug had done something. Momo's voice, then Okarun's. A burst of music from the far end of the house, three notes and a stop and an argument, and then the three notes again.

Mau listened to all of it.

In this life he had heard those sounds for more than a year without knowing what it would mean to lose them. In the other life he had lost them for eleven days and got them back changed. He found that he could hear both at once, the plain afternoon and the ache under it, and that it did not hurt as much as he had expected. It was simply true.

"Can I ask you one thing?" said Rimuru. ᴿ

"Okay." ᴹ

"Not about the scars. Something smaller." ᴿ

"Okay." ᴹ

Rimuru studied him.

"How old are you?" he said. "Actually." ᴿ

Mau looked at him.

"Shoko asked when your fingers stopped hurting and you said *a while ago*, like a man talking about something from years back." Rimuru shrugged a little. "You hold that cup like you've been holding cups that way for a long time. You turned your head for the blind side before she even moved her hand." He paused. "You don't look older. But you talk like someone who is." ᴿ

Mau laughed.

He did not mean to. It came up out of him all at once, the way laughter does when something is so familiar it catches you off guard. A real laugh, the first one since he woke, and it hurt his ribs and he did not care.

"Older than Milim," he said. ᴹ

Rimuru blinked.

"You're the one who worked it out," said Mau, still laughing. "You sat me down and did the arithmetic and you said, *Mau, I hate to tell you this, but you're older than Milim*, and you said it so seriously that I couldn't stop laughing for a whole—" ᴹ

He stopped.

Rimuru was looking at him with no recognition at all.

Kindly. Patiently. The way you look at someone telling you about a dinner you did not attend.

"I never said that," said Rimuru. ᴿ

The laugh went out of Mau like air out of a punctured skin.

"No," he said. ᴹ

He looked down at his hands.

"No. You didn't." ᴹ

* * *

The rug in the yard had stopped. The music had not. Somebody at the far end of the house was playing the same three notes over and over, trying to get them right.

Rimuru did not fill the silence.

He sat on the stool with his wet sleeve and his cup and waited, the way he had waited at the end of a long table in another life when Mau could not find the beginning of something.

"There was another one of you," said Mau at last. ᴹ

He said it carefully. Testing the floor.

"Another life. In this house. Same people. Same rooms, mostly. Same Ori, same Yuka, same everyone." He turned his cup. "It went worse. A lot worse. Things happened to me there that didn't happen here." He touched his chest, below the ribs. "This happened there." ᴹ

Rimuru did not move.

"And in that life," Mau said, "my Trial wasn't an afternoon." ᴹ

He watched Rimuru understand that.

This Rimuru knew the Trial. Everyone in this house knew the Trial. Three tests in an afternoon, a bucket and a choice and a voice that asked him to decide for everybody and let him give the decision back. He had come out of it tired and a little shaken and with a coin in his hand. That was the Trial, here.

"It was longer," said Mau. "Much longer. And I died in it." He paused. "More than once. A lot more than once. I lost count of the years and then I found the count again because I needed it." He looked at Rimuru. "The other you worked out how long it must have been, all added up. That's where the joke came from. Older than Milim." ᴹ

He tried to smile at it. It nearly worked.

"It was a good joke," he said. "You told it so I could laugh at it instead of the other thing." ᴹ

Rimuru was very still.

"How many?" he said, quietly. "Times." ᴿ

Mau looked at him.

"Not today," he said. ᴹ

Rimuru nodded.

He did not ask again.

* * *

After a while Rimuru got up and took Mau's empty cup and stood holding both cups, his own still half full, and did not go.

"Milim's going to be furious," he said. ᴿ

Mau looked up.

"When you tell her. Older than her." Rimuru's mouth twitched. "She'll want to fight you about it. She'll say it doesn't count if you were dead for some of it." ᴿ

Mau laughed again. Smaller. Real.

"She would," he said. ᴹ

"Of course she would." ᴿ

Rimuru looked at the cups in his hands.

"When you tell them," he said. "All of them. Whenever it is." He did not look up. "I'll be there. In the room." ᴿ

"You don't have to—" ᴹ

"Not because I knew about Yuka," said Rimuru. "Not to make up for that. That's a different thing and Yuta and I have to sort it out ourselves." He lifted his eyes. "Because you shouldn't have to stand up in front of everyone and do it alone. That's all." ᴿ

Mau looked at him.

In the other life, in a dark room, this same face had said *I'm asking you to stay with us while it's difficult*. And Mau had said *I'm sorry*, and Rimuru had said *don't do that instead of listening*, and then Mau had not listened.

He listened now.

"Okay," he said. ᴹ

Rimuru nodded once, as if something had been agreed, and went out with the cups.

* * *

When the door had swung half shut behind him, Ciel spoke for the first time since the tea.

"He never said that," she said. ᶜ

"No." ᴹ

"But the other one did." ᶜ

"Yes." ᴹ

She was quiet for a moment. He could feel her considering, the way he had felt another Ciel consider through a hundred floors, carefully, fitting a new fact against the old ones to see where it would sit.

"Was I there?" she asked. ᶜ

Mau closed his eyes.

"Yes," he said. "You were there." ᴹ

"Was I different?" ᶜ

"Not different," said Mau. "Just—" ᴹ

He did not know how to finish it.

*Tired*, he thought. *Frightened. Holding walls up inside me that you've never had to hold. Crying, sometimes, in a voice you've never had to use.*

"You'd been through more," he said. ᴹ

Ciel did not answer that.

But she did not go quiet either. She stayed close in the way she had, not asking, not reaching, simply there. And after a while, when the music at the end of the house finally got the three notes right and kept going into a fourth and a fifth, she said, softly:

"Rest. I'll tell you if anything changes." ᶜ

He rested.
