*When: Day480, afternoon. The clinic window is open a hand's width. Shoko has finally allowed the chair by it, on the condition that Mau does not try to stand up out of it without someone there.*

Yuka was reading to him.

Badly. On purpose. It was a book Fern had lent her about the grammar of very old spells, and Yuka had decided that the only way to survive it was to read every sentence in a different voice. The current voice was a pompous old wizard with a cold.

"*The subordinate clause of the binding,*" she read, through her nose, "*must at all times be governed by the primary—*" ʸᵏ

"You've done that voice already," said Mau. ᴹ

"I have not." ʸᵏ

"Chapter two. The bit about vowels." ᴹ

Yuka looked at him over the book.

"You were asleep for chapter two." ʸᵏ

"I was listening." ᴹ

She narrowed her eyes. He held her gaze with as much innocence as he could manage, which was not much, and she broke first and laughed and threw a cushion at him, gently, so that it landed on his knees instead of his chest.

It was the first time she had laughed at something he said since he woke.

He held the cushion and did not say anything, in case saying something made it stop.

* * *

She went back to the book. The wizard with the cold gave way to a very small, very nervous mouse who apologised for every grammatical rule as if it were personally responsible for it.

Mau sat in the chair by the window and listened and watched her.

The light came in through the gap and lay across her hands and the page. Her hair was up today, pinned with something plain. The bracelet was on her wrist, the dark braided cord, the small pale flower turned outward now, catching the light whenever she turned a page.

He had been watching her for a long time before he noticed that he was watching her the way you watch something you are afraid will go away if you look at it wrong.

He made himself stop.

He looked out of the window instead. At the yard. At the woodpile, and the pump, and the barn, and a piece of sky that was blue, plainly and ordinarily blue, the way it had been blue every day of his life and he had not seen it.

* * *

"*Your things.*" ᴰⁱᵒ

He went very still.

The voice was light. Not amused, this time. Just light, and close, and from nowhere at all, the way it had always been. He had heard it last in a dark room with a girl asleep beside him, saying *wait*.

Before that, in this life, he had heard it once. In a long white afternoon of three small tests, asking him to decide for everybody and then letting him give the decision back.

Yuka went on reading. She had not heard. Nobody ever heard but him.

Then the weight came down across his knees.

He looked.

A sword lay across his lap, on top of the cushion, sheathed. Long, slightly curved, the scabbard plain and dark. The hilt wrapped in something pale that had once been white and was now the colour of old bone. He knew the weight of it before he knew anything else. He knew exactly where to put his right hand so that the two stiff fingers of his left would not have to do more than steady it.

He had found it on the eighty-sixth floor of a tower that did not exist here. He had carried it through a field of a hundred statues and into a hall of grey nothing and held it at the throat of a thing that smiled.

And on the blanket beside his hand, small and round, a coin.

He did not have to touch it to know it was warm.

* * *

The mouse stopped apologising mid-sentence.

Yuka was staring at his lap.

"Where," she said slowly, "did that come from?" ʸᵏ

Mau did not answer at once.

He could feel the Goddess still there. Not speaking. Waiting, perhaps. Or deciding whether to say more.

"*And this,*" she said at last. Quieter. "*For my mistake.*" ᴰⁱᵒ

That was all.

He waited for the rest. For *I'm sorry*, or *I didn't know*, or *I should have measured it*. For any of the things he had thought, lying in the fever, that she might one day say.

She said none of them.

He felt her go, the way you feel someone leave a room behind you, and the afternoon came back.

* * *

"Mau." ʸᵏ

Yuka had put the book down. She was half out of her chair, one hand out toward the sword and stopped well short of it, the way she had learned to stop short of the blanket.

"That wasn't there," she said. "I was looking right at you. It wasn't there and then it was." ʸᵏ

"I know." ᴹ

"Is it dangerous?" ʸᵏ

He looked down at it.

It was a cursed tool. In another life Yuta had found it on the restored floor of a hall that had nearly killed them all, and sheathed it, and handed it to him without a word. In another life it had cut a line through a world.

"Don't touch the hilt," he said. "Not with your bare hand. It doesn't like people it doesn't know." ᴹ

Yuka sat back down very slowly.

"Okay," she said. "That's not a normal thing to say about a sword." ʸᵏ

"No." ᴹ

* * *

*Mau.* Ciel, sharp and close. *Something arrived. Two things. They weren't there and then they were. Nothing came through the door.* ᶜ

*I know.* ᴹ

*Did something bring them?* ᶜ

*Someone.* He looked at the coin. *You didn't hear her?* ᴹ

*Hear who?* ᶜ

He closed his eyes for a moment.

*Later,* he said. *I'll tell you. I'll tell all of you.* ᴹ

Ciel was quiet. Then: *All right. I'm watching your heart rate. It went up.* ᶜ

*I know.* ᴹ

* * *

Yuka was looking at the coin now.

"That too?" she said. ʸᵏ

"That too." ᴹ

"Is that one of the Trial coins?" ʸᵏ

He thought about how to answer that.

In this life the Trial had given every one of them who stayed a coin. He had given his to Kaneki. Kaneki had used it, with his own, and two people had come through the intake hall on a spring morning, a woman and a small girl with a wooden cup.

In the other life—

"No," he said. "It's a different one." ᴹ

"Who gave it to you?" ʸᵏ

He looked at her.

He had promised himself, in the fever, that the first thing he did when he could was stop hiding things from her. And he had promised himself, this morning, that he would not rush her into a story so large she would not have time to decide what she thought of the man telling it.

He did not know how to keep both promises at once. He tried.

"Someone who's done things to me before," he said. "Good and bad. Someone I'll tell you about." He paused. "She gave back some things of mine. From the long way away." ᴹ

Yuka looked at him for a long moment.

"The long way away," she repeated. ʸᵏ

"Yes." ᴹ

"Where the scars came from." ʸᵏ

"Yes." ᴹ

She looked at the sword. Then at the coin. Then back at his face.

"Okay," she said. ʸᵏ

That was all. *Okay.* She did not ask the next question. He could see it in her face, sitting there right behind her teeth, and she did not ask it. She picked up the book again and found her place and did not open her mouth to read.

"Yuka," he said. ᴹ

"I'm thinking," she said. ʸᵏ

He let her think.

* * *

Shoko came in ten minutes later with a cup of something hot and a clean cloth over her arm, and stopped in the doorway.

She looked at Mau. At the chair by the window. At the long dark sheathed sword lying across his knees on top of a cushion.

"No," said Shoko. ˢʰ

"It's mine," said Mau. ᴹ

"I don't care whose it is. You're my patient. You were sitting in a chair with a cushion when I left and now you're sitting in a chair with a sword." She put the cup down on the table, carefully, the way she put things down when she was deciding not to throw them. "Where did it come from?" ˢʰ

"It came back," said Mau. ᴹ

Shoko looked at Yuka.

"It appeared," said Yuka. "I was reading. It wasn't there and then it was." ʸᵏ

Shoko looked back at Mau.

"That's the second thing in two days that wasn't there and then was," she said. "First the scar. Now a sword." ˢʰ

"I know." ᴹ

"Is there going to be a third?" ˢʰ

He thought about the window in the dark. The shadows under the bed. The coin, warm on the blanket.

"Probably," he said. ᴹ

Shoko closed her eyes briefly.

"Corner," she said. ˢʰ

"What?" ᴹ

"The sword. It lives in that corner." She pointed. "Not on my patient. Not under my patient's pillow. Not across my patient's knees while he's supposed to be resting. In the corner, where I can see it, and where he can see it, and where nobody sits on it by accident." ˢʰ

"It doesn't like people touching the hilt," said Yuka helpfully. ʸᵏ

Shoko turned and looked at her.

"Of course it doesn't," she said. ˢʰ

* * *

Mau stood the sword in the corner himself, slowly, with Yuka's hand under his elbow and Shoko watching every step. It leaned against the wall in the angle between the window and the shelf of jars, dark and quiet, and looked like exactly what it was: something that did not belong in a clinic and had decided to stay anyway.

The coin he put in his shirt pocket. The left one.

He did not know why the left. His hand did it.

When he sat back down, Shoko examined him again, briefly, because she always examined him again after anything, and then took her cup and her cloth and went to the door.

She stopped there.

"Mau," she said. ˢʰ

"Yes." ᴹ

"When you tell us." She did not turn round. "Whenever it is. I'd like the sword to be part of it." ˢʰ

"It will be." ᴹ

"Good." ˢʰ

She went out.

* * *

Yuka sat down again in her chair with the book on her knees.

She did not open it.

"Can I ask one thing?" she said. ʸᵏ

"Yes." ᴹ

"Not about where it came from. You'll tell me that." She looked at the corner. At the sword. "Just—" ʸᵏ

She stopped and started again.

"Did you use it?" she said. "There. In the long way away." ʸᵏ

Mau looked at her.

"Yes," he said. ᴹ

"On people?" ʸᵏ

"On something that wasn't a person. Something that wanted to take everything." He swallowed. "I held it at its throat. And then I let it go, because there was someone I had to catch." ᴹ

Yuka was quiet for a long time.

Then she opened the book.

"*The subordinate clause of the binding,*" she read, in the voice of the wizard with the cold, "*must at all times be governed by the primary.*" ʸᵏ

"You've done that voice," said Mau. ᴹ

"I'm doing it again," said Yuka. "Because I want to. Shut up and listen." ʸᵏ

He shut up and listened.

In the corner, the sword leaned against the wall. In his pocket, over the heart that had always been his, the coin was warm.
