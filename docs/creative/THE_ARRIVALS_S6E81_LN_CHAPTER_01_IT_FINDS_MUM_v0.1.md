*When: Day481, mid-morning. The back step. The frost has gone off the yard but not off the shadow of the barn. Taihei is washing pots at the kitchen window with the shutter open, which is not a coincidence.*

The step was colder than he remembered.

He had sat on it a thousand times. In this life and the other. He had sat on it at dawn with a coin in his hand and decided to give it away. He had sat on it at dusk with Frieren's shoulder against his, not talking. He had sat on it at the reset hour, waiting, and in another life he had sat on it blind, with his arms wrapped to the elbow, listening to the yard.

It was cold every time. He had simply never noticed, because there had always been something else to notice.

He noticed now. He sat on the cold step with a blanket round his shoulders that Shoko had put there without asking, and looked at the yard in colour, and noticed everything.

* * *

"You're supposed to be resting," said Taihei, from the window. ᵀ

"I am resting." ᴹ

"You're resting on a step. Shoko said resting in a bed." ᵀ

"Shoko said the step." ᴹ

Taihei considered this.

"She said the step if somebody watches," he said. ᵀ

"You're watching." ᴹ

"I'm washing pots." ᵀ

"You're washing pots very slowly." ᴹ

Taihei looked down at the pot in his hands, which he had been washing, Mau suspected, for some time.

"It's a difficult pot," he said with dignity, and went on washing it, and did not move from the window. ᵀ

* * *

Ori came out a little after.

He heard her before he saw her. Small quick feet on the kitchen floor, then the door, then the step, and then she was beside him on the cold stone in her coat, with something held in both hands in front of her the way she held things she had been told were important.

She did not sit down. She stood beside him and looked at the thing in her hands, very seriously, and then turned in a slow circle on the step, and then stopped.

"Barn," she said. ᴼ

Mau looked at what she was holding.

A compass. Small, round, silver gone dull with handling. A fine break across the face, as if somebody had dropped it once on stone and picked it up and decided it still worked. The needle inside quivered and swung and steadied, pointing, as far as he could tell, at the barn.

He knew it. He had known it for years.

In this life it had always been Ori's. On a string round her neck, under a cushion where she had sat on it to see if the needle would stick, held up through a tree to see if it still found her mother on the other side. He had never once thought it had belonged anywhere else.

Now he knew where else.

In another life the same old silver had come to him wrapped, as an anniversary gift, from Frieren's own pocket. It had sat in his pocket through a week in a red gate and pointed at a woman with long pale hair every time he took it out. He had opened it on the last watch before a dragon, and she had been sitting beside him when he did.

He did not say anything.

"She's in the barn," Ori announced, to the yard in general. ᴼ

* * *

"Who is?" said Mau. ᴹ

Ori looked at him as if he had asked what colour the sky was.

"Mum," she said. ᴼ

"Ah." ᴹ

"It finds her." She held the compass out a little, so he could see the needle. Not close enough to take. Close enough to look. "Wherever she is. You know that. It always points at her." ᴼ

Mau looked at the needle.

"It's good at that," he said. ᴹ

"It's the best at it." She turned it slightly and watched the needle hold. "Mum says it's very old." ᴼ

"It looks old." ᴹ

"It's got a crack." She showed him. "That was before me. It still works, though." ᴼ

"I can see that." ᴹ

* * *

She sat down beside him at last. Not close. A little way along the step, with the compass on her knees, the way she would sit beside anyone in the house she liked and was not related to.

He knew that distance. In this life it had always been the distance between them. Ori had grown up with Frieren's hand on her head and Frieren's lap to climb into and Frieren's voice saying her name at night. Mau had been there too. Someone who carried things and fixed things and played games when he was asked. Someone she liked. Not someone she counted at the reset hour.

In the other life—

He made himself look at the yard.

In the other life a small girl had come to his door at night to inspect him and Frieren both, and satisfy herself that their evening had worked. She had taken the list of his named shadows down off Umaru's wall and carried it to the table with a pencil beside it, so that nobody would be counted wrong. She had stood beside his place with that list in her hand and pointed at line three, because she wanted to meet a knight.

That girl was sitting a little way along the step with a compass on her knees.

She did not know.

* * *

"You keep looking at me," said Ori. ᴼ

He turned.

She was watching him with the very serious face she used for important questions, her hands folded over the compass.

"Do I?" ᴹ

"Since you woke up. You look at me like—" She frowned, searching for it. "Like Mum does. When I've done something good and she doesn't want to make a fuss about it." ᴼ

Mau did not answer at once.

At the kitchen window, the difficult pot had gone very quiet.

"You didn't used to," said Ori. Not accusing. Just noticing, the way she noticed everything. ᴼ

"No," said Mau. ᴹ

"Why do you now?" ᴼ

* * *

He thought about how to answer her.

He could not tell her. Not this. Not here, on a cold step, with nobody else knowing, with Frieren in the barn and Ori's whole small life built round the fact of who her mother was. It would be like taking the compass out of her hands and telling her it had been pointing at the wrong person all along.

It had not been pointing at the wrong person. That was the thing. It pointed at exactly the right person. In this life, Frieren was her mother, and that was true, and it was good, and he was not going to stand in the middle of it.

But he was not going to lie to her either.

"I knew a compass like yours," he said. ᴹ

Ori looked at it, then at him.

"Once," he said. "A long time ago. A long way away. It looked almost exactly like that one. It had a crack too." ᴹ

"Did it find someone?" ᴼ

"Yes." ᴹ

"Who?" ᴼ

He looked at the needle, steady, pointing at the barn.

"Someone I loved very much," he said. ᴹ

Ori considered this.

"Does it still?" ᴼ

"I don't have it anymore." He smiled, a little. "I think it's somewhere it's needed more." ᴹ

She looked at the compass on her knees for a long moment. Then back up at him. He could see her thinking, the way she thought about everything, turning it over to see if there was a side she had missed.

"Is that why you look at me like that?" she said. "Because I've got one too?" ᴼ

"Something like that," said Mau. ᴹ

It was not a lie. It was not the whole truth. It was what he could give her on a cold step without taking anything away.

Ori nodded slowly, as if that settled something.

* * *

"Do you want to play?" she said. ᴼ

He looked at her.

"It's a new game," she explained. "I made it up this week. Mum hides somewhere, and I have to find her, but I'm not allowed to look. I can only use this." She held up the compass. "And I have to say where she is before I get there. If I'm right I get a point. If I'm wrong she gets a point." ᴼ

"Who's winning?" ᴹ

"Me." A pause. "Mostly. She cheats." ᴼ

"How does she cheat?" ᴹ

"She floats. Up. So the needle points at the sky and I can't tell where." Ori looked deeply offended by this. "That's not a hiding place. The sky isn't a place." ᴼ

Mau laughed. He could not help it.

"That is cheating," he agreed. ᴹ

"I told her." ᴼ

"What did she say?" ᴹ

"She said the sky is a perfectly good place and I should learn to point up." Ori scowled at the compass. "I'm going to tell her it's not allowed anymore. It's my game. I make the rules." ᴼ

"You should." ᴹ

"Do you want to play, though?" ᴼ

He looked at the needle.

"I can't run yet," he said. "Shoko would kill me." ᴹ

"You don't have to run. You can be the one who says where." She held the compass out to him. Properly, this time, close enough to take. "I'll do the walking. You tell me where she is. Using this. No looking." ᴼ

Mau looked at the compass in her small hand.

He took it.

* * *

It was warm from her hands. The silver was smooth, worn down by years of a child's thumbs. He turned it slowly on his palm and watched the needle swing and steady.

Barn.

"Barn," he said. ᴹ

"I already said barn," said Ori. ᴼ

"It's still barn." ᴹ

"But *where* in the barn? That's the hard part. You have to say where." ᴼ

He looked at the needle. At the angle of it against the barn's long side. At the place the shadow fell.

"By the tobacco," he said. "The left end. Near the door to the loft." ᴹ

Ori stared at him.

"How do you know that?" ᴼ

"It's where I'd go," said Mau. "If I wanted to read somewhere warm where nobody would bother me." ᴹ

Ori looked at the barn. Then at him. Then, without another word, she took the compass back and ran.

* * *

She was back in under a minute, at a speed Taihei watched from the window with open alarm, and behind her, unhurried, with a book under her arm and her hair loose and an expression of mild interest, came Frieren.

"He said by the tobacco," Ori announced. "The left end. And you were *there*." ᴼ

Frieren looked at Mau on the step.

"I was," she said. ᶠ

"He gets a point," said Ori. "That's a point for him." ᴼ

"That's a point," Frieren agreed. ᶠ

She stood at the bottom of the step with her book under her arm and looked at him. Not the way she had looked at him in the field, in the other life, with her arms closing on nothing. Not the way she had looked at him on a garden bench. She looked at him the way this Frieren had always looked at him: the student who had become a friend, the man who had shared a house with her daughter all her life and played whatever game he was asked to.

Kindly. A little curious. Nothing else.

"You're out," she said. ᶠ

"Shoko let me have the step." ᴹ

"With conditions," said Taihei, from the window. ᵀ

Frieren glanced at the window, then back.

"You look better," she said. ᶠ

"I feel better." ᴹ

She nodded. Then she looked down at Ori, who was holding the compass up to her as proof.

"He's good at it," said Ori. "He's better than you." ᴼ

"I don't cheat," said Frieren. ᶠ

"You *float*." ᴼ

"Floating isn't cheating." ᶠ

"It is in my game." ᴼ

Frieren looked at Mau over Ori's head. For one moment something in her face was almost a smile.

"Apparently," she said, "it is in her game." ᶠ

* * *

They played until Shoko came out and sent him back to bed.

Frieren hid. Ori ran. Mau sat on the cold step with the blanket round his shoulders and the compass in his hands and said *by the well*, and *behind the woodpile*, and once, triumphantly, *on the roof of the barn*, which Ori said was not allowed and Frieren said was perfectly allowed and Mau said was where he would have gone too.

Ori won, in the end. She always won. Frieren saw to it.

When Shoko came, Ori took the compass back without being asked, because it was hers, and held it in both hands, and looked at Mau.

"You can play again," she said. "Tomorrow. If Shoko says." ᴼ

"I'd like that," said Mau. ᴹ

"You're better than Mum," said Ori, and then, glancing up at Frieren, loyally: "But Mum's still the best." ᴼ

"Of course she is," said Mau. ᴹ

Frieren put her hand on Ori's head.

He watched her do it. Small and pale and ordinary, on the top of a child's head, the way he had watched it a thousand times in two lives.

He did not let anything show on his face. He had had a great deal of practice.

Then Shoko took his elbow and steered him inside, and behind him on the step Ori was explaining the rules again to Frieren, firmly, with particular emphasis on the sky.
