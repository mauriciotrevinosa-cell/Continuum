* * *

Kaisel waited in the clear ground where they had agreed to call him.

Up close, with the bag on her shoulder and no bench to retreat to, Yuka found he was bigger than he had been two days ago. This was demonstrably unfair. Mau was pretending not to notice her measuring the height between the lowered shoulder and the place she was expected to sit.

"You can still say no," he said. ᴹ

"I haven't said anything." ʸᵏ

"I know." ᴹ

She gave him the bag and climbed.

It was not elegant. Her knee went where her foot should have, and the hem of her coat caught, and Mau had to free it before she could turn. She would remember later that Kaisel never shifted once while she was doing this. At the time she had other concerns.

"How do you get down?" she asked. ʸᵏ

"We discuss that at the beach." ᴹ

He was grinning. She wanted to hit him, and could not let go long enough.

"I hate you." ʸᵏ

"Very early in the day for that." ᴹ

He climbed behind her, slower than she had, found a place for the bag where it would not slip, and showed her the ridge for her hands. His right covered hers briefly while she adjusted her grip.

"Slowly," he told Kaisel. ᴹ

The wings unfolded.

From below Yuka heard Milim announce that she was being left behind. Somebody told her she had an entire house to bother. The answer was lost as the wings beat and the yard fell away.

Yuka shut her eyes.

She had not decided to. One moment she was looking at Maki, and the next there was much too much distance where the grass had been.

Mau leaned forward so she could hear him.

"Eyes open. Just ahead, if down's too much." ᴹ

"Mine are open." ʸᵏ

"All right." ᴹ

She opened them.

The tops of the trees were beside her.

Not below yet. Beside, in the morning light, the last damp leaves trembling under the wingbeats. Then they passed over them and there was the river, and the house beyond it, and the tree lifting out of the middle of everything she knew.

She forgot her grip enough to wave.

Maki lifted a hand. Yuta did too.

"They can see us," Yuka said. ʸᵏ

"Yes." ᴹ

* * *

It was noisier than she had expected.

The wind took away anything said too softly, and made the simplest conversation require either shouting or Mau's mouth near her ear. For the first stretch she disliked how much of her attention went to sitting correctly. There was a whole country beneath them and she was thinking about where her left foot was.

Then Kaisel settled into a steady flight over the river. The wingbeats became something she could feel coming. Her shoulders eased.

Mau said nothing about it.

She liked him for that.

"Is that where we camped?" she called, pointing beyond a bend. ʸᵏ

He followed her hand.

"Farther east. Other side of the trees." ᴹ

She looked until she found the patch he meant. It was tiny from here. They had spent so long deciding where to put a tent in a patch of world she could now cover with two fingers.

"You picked the wet side," she told him. ʸᵏ

"I remember this accusation." ᴹ

"That's because it's true in this life." ʸᵏ

They followed the water.

* * *

On the rise she discovered that getting down involved being brave for longer than getting up.

Mau was on the ground already. He pointed out the foothold, moved out of the way, then offered his good hand for the last part. She took it and dropped the last small distance into the grass.

Her knees complained.

"I walked differently up there," she said. ʸᵏ

"You didn't walk up there." ᴹ

"That's what my knees are complaining about." ʸᵏ

They sat in a patch of sunlight out of the wind. Kaisel folded himself down some distance away, head lifted toward the sky. Yuka watched him while Mau unstoppered the bottle.

"Is he tired?" she asked. ʸᵏ

"No. We are." ᴹ

"Speak for yourself." ʸᵏ

He offered her the bottle. She took it with both hands.

They ate the first of the bread. Neither of them mentioned that she had said they did not need so much. The bread was good and the sun had warmed one side of the bag. Mau leaned against a low rock and tipped his face up. Yuka put the bottle down between their boots and watched the colour of the grass change when the breeze went over it.

"Do you remember this place?" she asked. ʸᵏ

"Yes. We stopped somewhere close to here." ᴹ

"Was I scared then too?" ʸᵏ

"You denied it then too." ᴹ

He told her it was no worse than before they left, and waited while she considered that answer beside the other things she could feel. She did not ask to see what he remembered of this hill.

When Yuka stood, he took another minute before joining her.

She spent it telling Kaisel how impressively she had got down.

* * *

The air grew warmer before the sea appeared.

Yuka smelled it and knew what it was without having to ask. Salt, something living and wet, a wind that had come a very long way without passing over a kitchen.

Then the hills opened.

She had seen water before. She knew how foolish that thought was even while she had it. The river was water. The lake was water. She had washed her hair in it, drunk it, fallen into it and complained about it.

This was too much of it to fit inside any of those memories.

It went all the way to the sky.

"Oppa." ʸᵏ

"I see it." ᴹ

"No. Look." ʸᵏ

He looked with her.

The curve of the beach. Little boats near the town. White water breaking in strips so long that she could not follow one end to the other before another came in behind it. She wanted to lean out to see more and moved back at once when she remembered where they were.

"Can we go down?" ʸᵏ

Kaisel was already turning.

They landed beyond the boats, away from the people pulling nets over the sand. Yuka got down more successfully this time and immediately discovered that sand could get into a boot without being invited.

Mau thanked Kaisel with his hand on the dark neck. She did the same. The great head dipped toward her once before he sank into their joined shadow on the sand.

She watched until the last of him was gone.

"He can still hear you?" she asked. ʸᵏ

"If I call him." ᴹ

The stallholder did not remember him.

Mau had known she wouldn't. Still, he recognised the cloth stretched above her stock, the stool with one short leg, the little bowl she kept her change in. He had looked at all of it through a wall that kept half his life away. Now he could remember choosing a flower at the next stall, the light on Yuka's wrist, the way she had helped with the knot.

They bought food on the way back down.

Yuka wanted to try the little savoury things arranged on a hot tray by the sea wall. Mau could identify only two ingredients. The seller explained the others, accepted the coins, and wrapped enough for both of them without appearing to care whether either had an interesting past.

Mau found that so pleasant he nearly bought more.

Yuka stopped him by holding up the bread left in the bag.

They sat on the wall and ate with the sea in front of them. One parcel was too hot to hold, then cool enough, then empty. Yuka brushed a crumb off his sleeve and tried to write the seller's explanation on the back of the budget page.
