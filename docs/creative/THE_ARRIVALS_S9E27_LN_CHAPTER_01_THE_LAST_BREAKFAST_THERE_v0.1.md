*When: B+118. Yuka says goodbye to the breakfast table in the water before saying goodbye to the room.*

Mau waits beside her with the little pack.

“Would you like a moment alone?” ᴹ

“You can mock it. You weren't the one who found it.” ʸ

“I ate there every morning.” ᴹ

“Because I have excellent judgement.” ʸ

He concedes this easily enough to make her suspicious. She looks over, sees the expression he is failing to conceal and gives him the empty cup to carry back.

Their last breakfast takes longer than they intended. The attendant remembers what Yuka liked. She thanks him, asks a question about one of the dishes and writes the answer on the back of a page which has already collected several completely different purposes. Mau notices the place where her drawing of the crab has begun invading the route.

He moves the paper before it meets the jam.

She catches his wrist and takes the bite he was saving.

“For the journey,” she says. ʸ

He looks at the empty fork.

“We're still here.” ᴹ

“Planning ahead.” ʸ

* * *

They check the room together.

The work is simple enough that they each finish their side and then inspect the place the other has already checked. Mau finds her hair tie beneath the little plate. Yuka finds a folded page he has put somewhere extremely safe and immediately forgotten.

“We'd never have managed separately,” she says. ʸ

He puts the page into storage with the rest of their things.

At the desk, the staff receive the key and the letter for the next mainland dispatch. Mau asks whether the letter needs anything else. It does not. It will go with the outgoing bag, travel by the ordinary route and reach Rimuru when the people carrying it reach him.

That seems a perfectly reasonable speed for news about stolen fruit.

Yuka thanks the woman who welcomed them. She means it so fully that the woman smiles before offering the polite answer her work requires. The young attendant from their arrival takes them back toward the clearing, where there is room for Kaisel to unfold without frightening anybody's breakfast off a table.

This time the young man waits to watch.

Mau lets Yuka climb first.

* * *

They follow the coast for a while before turning toward the mainland route.

Yuka has one hand against Mau's forearm and the other holding the page clear of the wind. He can see the place she has marked for their next stop, a little line drawn by the hotel attendant beside the road. There is a path to a high view and somewhere to eat before they carry on.

“We don't have to fly straight there,” she says. ʸ

“You have another plan?” ᴹ

She points down toward the beach they have just passed.

There is hardly anything on it. A narrow stretch between rocks, a few tracks which do not reach the water and a place where the shade would last long enough to sit.

Mau asks Kaisel for a slow turn.

Yuka puts the map away.

* * *

Their picnic contains too much bread.

Mau suspects the woman who packed it considered two people on a dragon likely to become separated from civilisation for longer than they intended. Yuka lays the food out on the ordinary cloth they have brought and begins with the little jar she has been curious about since breakfast.

He takes a piece of bread and waits.

“You first,” she says. ʸ

“A charitable offer.” ᴹ

“I want your honest opinion.” ʸ

He tries it. Waits long enough to make her lean closer.

Then takes another bite.

Yuka removes the jar from his reach.

They eat until neither wants anything else. Afterward she lies down with her head on his lap and asks him to tell her which of the birds beyond the rocks looks most likely to steal from them. He considers the question with the seriousness it has acquired since the hotel.

“That one.” ᴹ

“Why?” ʸ

“It isn't pretending not to watch.” ᴹ

She turns her face toward his stomach to hide her laughter. His hand finds the loose part of her hair and moves through it slowly until she stops asking questions.

* * *

They reach their next guesthouse before evening.

Yuka has sand in one shoe, one new line on the map and a lengthening argument about whether the bowl should go beside their bed or near the sitting-room window. Mau has no strong opinion about the location until she explains which one will make him more likely to put the little things in it instead of leaving them beside it.

He objects to the unfair accuracy of the prediction.

At supper, she asks whether he is sad to have left the island.

“A little,” he says. ᴹ

She nods, then takes his hand across the narrow table.

“We still have tomorrow.” ʸ

“And the day after.” ᴹ

“And the day after that.” ʸ

He begins smiling.

“I've lost count.” ᴹ

“I'll keep it for you.” ʸ

She draws the plate nearer before he can steal anything from it, then pushes it halfway back because he has apparently been included in her plans for the remaining bread.
