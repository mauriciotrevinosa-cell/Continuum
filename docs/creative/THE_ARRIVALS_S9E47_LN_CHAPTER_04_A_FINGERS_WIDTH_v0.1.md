*When: the upper part of him begins to slide, Yuka says the number.*

“Four.” ʸ

She doesn't know why. There's no reason for it. It is just the next one.

It comes out in a small flat voice, into a silence that goes from one side of the world to the other.

The slide stops.

* * *

He has gone over about as far as a man leans to look at something on the ground. He's tilted forward and to the left, the whole top half of him, from just above the hips. His hanging arm has swung out. There's a thing happening at his waist that a good many people will spend the rest of their lives trying not to have seen. He's open from the front, all the way around, on a line as neat as a ruled page.

He doesn't fall.

He ought to. There isn't one person who knows anything who doesn't know that. Shoko, with her hand on Gojo's mouth, has begun to do the sum, and the sum is very short.

Over his head there is something in the air.

It's so faint that on the panels it looks like a fault in the light. It's pale and rather smeared around the edges, and you'd need to know what you were looking at. It has eight spokes.

It's turning. They all hear it, very small and clear. It's about the noise of a bolt going home in a door a long way off.

*Click.*

* * *

There's a thing that used to happen on the twentieth floor.

He told her that too. It was in another life, and he told it as a funny story. There had been some big dull thing with claws that had taken a swing at him for the hundredth time. And on that hundredth time, for no reason he could feel, the claws had come up a little short. They didn't stop. Nothing stopped them. They only closed on the air a finger's width from where they should have closed, and they'd go on closing there, as long as he lived. It had seemed to him at the time a very small thing to have gone through so much for.

The end of the world has gone through Mau from front to back, and all the way around. It has told the place where he's standing, with absolute authority, *not here*.

It has been wrong by about a finger.

There's a strip at the back of him, on the right of the spine. It's no wider than that. Bone, a cord of muscle, some skin, the big dull stubborn root of him, about what you'd need to hang a coat on. It hasn't been cut. It wasn't missed. The line has gone all the way there and it's arrived, and it's *arriving*, it will go on arriving, and it's learned nothing, and it's never, ever going to finish.

*Mau,* someone is saying. *Mau. Mau. I've got you. Your left foot. Left foot. I need you to tell the soldiers. I can't reach them, it has to be you. Mau, they're yours, they don't cost anything to stand. Do you hear me? They don't cost anything to stand. Tell them to stand.* ᶜⁱ

He can see the ground. It's rather close, and it's very interesting. There's a good deal of himself going onto it.

He can't remember the word.

It's all right. They've been with him a long time.

* * *

The first thing to come up is a hand.

It comes out of the shadow at his feet, black, an ordinary hand with an ordinary vambrace on it, belonging to somebody who was killed in a corridor a long time ago. It takes hold of his trouser leg. It goes up, climbing, hand over hand.

There's another. There are ten.

People on the ridge see his legs go dark. Then they see the dark go up. Every soldier he has who can still hold a shape, all that are lying in the bottom of that shadow with the marks of the shrine on them, the three thousand, the four, are getting to their feet down there and looking up. There's no room. There isn't room for even one of them to come out. They don't try. They just send up an arm. They're coming up his thighs and over his hips in a black wet crawl, a hundred, a thousand, each with the same simple job. They get to the edge of the line. They reach in.

They take hold of the top half of him, and they lift.

It's the slowest thing anyone has ever seen. He comes back up by inches, his head rising, the hanging arm swinging in. There are hands inside him now. They've got him by the ribs and the belt of his spine, they're holding everything that's meant to be held, and they fold themselves into the line in rows, knuckle to knuckle, right the way around, like a great many men shoulder to shoulder in a very narrow trench. They close it. They stand there.

That's all they do. They stand. It has never cost him anything to have them stand. He paid for each of them once, and he has never had to pay again. It's about the first thing he ever wrote down about them.

A seam of black runs around Mau's waist, a hand wide and moving slightly. His head comes level.

He opens his eyes.

The right one, anyway.

* * *

Sukuna hasn't lowered his hands.

He's holding them just as they were. His lips are still a little parted around the shape of the word. He looks like a man who has reached the bottom of a stair in the dark and put his foot down, firmly, on a further stair.

Nobody has ever seen his face do this. There's nobody in the whole of a thousand years who has.

It goes on for some time.

Twenty paces away, the boy swallows. It looks as if it takes a great deal of work. There's blood down his chin and his one good eye keeps slipping and coming back, and he's got to look for what he wants to say. You can see him looking. He goes through several rooms. He seems to find it somewhere near the beginning, at noon, in a place where it was lying on the floor.

“Better,” Mau says. ᴹ

* * *

It takes a moment to reach him.

Then Sukuna's knees go.

He doesn't fall; he sits. There's a big flat piece of rock behind him and he sits down on it, hard. He has got both hands over the ruin of his face and his shoulders have begun to go, and it's a while before anyone can make out that the noise is laughter. It's not the howl from before. There's no strength for it. It's just coming out of him, a high thin wheezing helpless thing, like an old man at the end of the best joke he's ever been told. It goes on and on. He rocks. He has to stop to get his breath, and he looks up, and he sees the boy there, *standing*, and it sets him off again.

He holds up one hand toward Mau, palm out, shaking it, the way you do when you can't talk. *Wait. Wait.*

It's empty.

Anybody could see it. It's just a big tired hand. There's nothing behind it now and nothing in the purse; it has gone up into the sky, the whole lot. For the first time since before there were countries, the most dangerous thing in the world is an old man sitting on a rock, with no more cursed energy in him than a dead dog.

He doesn't seem to mind in the least.

* * *

On the ridge, nobody has said anything.

There are a few thousand people there, and they've all stopped having opinions. Some have their mouths open. Hinata has sat down on the ground. High up on the Tempest side a very large person has started to cry, enormously and without any shame, and nobody is telling him to stop. Rimuru hasn't moved. He's got a thread in him, one very thin thread, that he promised not to pull on. He hasn't pulled on it all day, and there's blood on his lip where he's been biting it.

Yuuki has stopped sitting forward. He has quietly, carefully, without seeming to, moved back.

Shoko takes her hand from Gojo's mouth. She has to think about how. Her fingers have stiffened.

He doesn't speak right away.

He looks down at his own hand, on his own waist. He looks at it as though someone has left it there. Slowly, one finger at a time, he takes it away. He breathes in, and it goes a long, long way down, farther than it has gone in some time. He has been holding that breath since a very good day in another world.

“A finger,” he says. His voice has gone completely. “He got it to a *finger*. Do you know, I never even—I've been going over that for—I had the eyes, I had every—” ᴳ

He stops. He's got water running down his face, a great deal of it, and he doesn't seem to have noticed. He's smiling. It's the most awful smile she has ever seen; it keeps coming apart and he keeps putting it back.

“Excellent grades,” says Gojo Satoru, and he sits down on the step and puts his head on her knee. ᴳ

Shoko stands there. She's a very tired woman with a job to do in a little while. She puts her hand on his hair.

“That's my son,” he says, muffled. ᴳ

“Shut up,” she says. “Shut up, shut up.” ˢʰ

She doesn't stop looking at the panel.

Neither does Yuka. Her lips are white. She hasn't let go of anything. There's nothing on her face you could call relief, because she's got the same eyes as her grandfather, and she can see what everyone up here who is laughing and weeping has forgotten to check.

The slates still say what they said.

Down in the field, the old man has finally stopped laughing. He wipes his eyes with a thumb. He sits for a moment longer, getting his breath. He puts his two hands on his knees, like anyone would. And he stands up.

He hasn't got a thing left in the world but what's on his bones.

He starts walking.

So does the boy.
