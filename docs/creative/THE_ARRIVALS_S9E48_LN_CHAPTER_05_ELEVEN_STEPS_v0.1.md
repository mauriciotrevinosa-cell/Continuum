# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 59
## Chapter 22 — Eleven Steps

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S9 v0.2 (2026-11-03)
**Volume:** 59
**Reading order:** 22
**Source episode:** S9E48
**Continuity state:** B+136 continuous. Sukuna refuses to yield. Mau ends it by hand against the bottom step. Records agree on Sukuna's irreversible end. No technique. Public terror is exactly what Makima wanted and she knows it.

*When: Mau takes the first step, he has to stop and work out how the second one goes.*

The left leg holds. He didn't expect that. It isn't really a leg now, it's a post with something wrapped around it. He drags the good one up beside it and stands there, swaying, at the top of a very long walk.

Eleven paces. He counted them on his way down. No, that isn't right; he didn't come down. He has been here all day.

*Ciel.*

*I'm here.* Her voice is very small and very close. She sounds as if she's been shouting for a long time in an empty building. *I'm here. I can't do anything. I told you. I can only talk. That's your foot. That's the other one.*

Two.

There's something the matter with his head. It isn't pain. It's that things keep arriving a little after they've happened. He looks at the stones and the stones are there, and a moment later he finds out what they are. He has a thing he came over here to do. He'd like to be clear about that. He keeps a good grip on it, like you do on a bucket on a dark stair.

Three. Four.

The hands in his waist shift their hold. He can feel them talking to each other, the way men do on a long haul. *Got it? Got it.* Somebody's brought him a brace for his knee, without being asked; it's made of somebody's forearm. Five.

The whole ridge is watching him walk. It's a very odd feeling. He isn't sure what they want.

Six is a bad one. He goes down on the post, and it folds, and he's on a hand and a knee, and there's a good deal of red going onto the stone from somewhere. He stays there and looks at it. It would be fine to stay here. He has done this part before too.

He gets up. Seven. It takes about as long as the whole of the morning.

* * *

Sukuna is watching him come.

He's lying on his back, where he rolled. Every one of those nine small lines has opened all the way down, and he's lying in what came out of them. His throat is a thing nobody should have to look at. But the eyes are all right. There are three of them still, and they're following Mau with bright, unhurried attention, like an old man on a bench watching a child learn to ride.

One of his hands moves.

It's the one with the three fingers. It creeps over the stone, feeling its way. It gets flat. It presses. You can see the whole arm start to tremble, from the shoulder down. There's something still going on in him. It's not much, a thread, a warm place, a last small mending at the back of everything. He's a very old man, and he doesn't know how to stop. The elbow begins to bend.

It slides away.

He looks at it, and then back at Mau, and the corner of his mouth moves.

He tries it again. He's in no hurry.

Eight.

Nine.

Mau can hear him breathing now. It comes and goes through the wrong place. He gets to ten, and he looks at the last one, and it seems a very long way. There's nothing to hold on to. So he just falls. He lets the post go and he falls forward, and it brings him down on his knees, more or less, with his one hand landing on a big warm chest.

They look at each other.

There's the sky up there behind him, Sukuna will be seeing. It has a line on it. It's his. It has started to go pink around the edges.

His lips move.

Mau has to get down close to hear. There's not much air to carry it. It comes bubbling out in a thread, and it takes its time, and there's so much pleasure in it.

“...Late.” ˢᵘ

Mau thinks that over.

“Yes,” he says. ᴹ

It's quite hard to say anything. He has a fairly good idea of what you say here, though. Someone wrote it down. It's very important, they went over it again and again at a table. If you can get them to say it, then it's done, and nobody has to do anything more.

“Yield,” he says. ᴹ

It takes the man a moment to make out what he's been asked.

Then his whole chest starts to shake under Mau's hand. There's no sound to it. There's only a lot of blood coming up, in small bright pulses, and those three eyes are screwed up tight, with water coming out of them. He's laughing. He's laughing so hard. He gets his head an inch off the rock and he moves it from side to side. It's the smallest thing. It's the most complete refusal anyone has ever seen.

Mau nods.

He'd thought so. He knows him pretty well.

* * *

There's a stone step just behind them, the bottom one of the old quarry flight. It's about a foot high. It's got a red smear on the edge of it already.

It's about three feet away.

His right arm's gone. His left hand has two bad fingers, and they won't close. He gets the other two and the thumb into the man's hair, right down at the root, and he makes a sort of hook. Something pops. He leans back. It's a big head. It's very heavy. He has got no legs to speak of and no strength in him at all, only the weight of a person falling over backward, and all those soldiers holding on. He falls over backward. Sukuna comes with him about a foot.

He gets up. He falls over backward.

That's all it is. It takes a long time. It doesn't look like anything from the ridge but one small figure going back and forth. They can see what he's doing about a minute before they can believe it. Nobody calls out. Nobody asks anyone to stop looking. A man three rows from the top has begun to say *no, no, no*, in a very quiet reasonable voice. His wife takes his arm, and he doesn't notice.

The head gets to the step.

Mau gets his knee up on the stone. He arranges things. It's fiddly, with one hand. All this time those three eyes have been on him, watching how he goes about it, with deep professional interest.

He gets his fingers in the hair, and he lifts.

Once.

It makes a small dull sound.

“Yield,” Mau says. ᴹ

The lips are still moving. They might be saying something. He leans down to listen, and there's a bubble that comes and goes. They're pulled up at one corner.

He lifts.

Twice.

He doesn't ask again.

He doesn't know how many it is, after that. He isn't counting; he hasn't got the head for it. He has it in the hand that this is a job that has to be done, and that nobody else is going to, and that it isn't done. His arm goes up and his arm comes down. It's quite restful. There is a great big warehouse in his head with a kitchen in it, and a shut door, and he isn't thinking about that. He's not thinking about anyone, or about what the sky looks like. He's so tired. It's nothing to do with anybody. It's just a sound, and then the same sound, and now it's a little wetter.

*Mau,* someone says. ᶜⁱ

Up. Down.

*Mau. Stop. You can stop now.*

Up.

*Mau, he's gone. He has been gone for a while. Look up. Mau, look at the table. Look. It's over.*

His arm has stopped. He looks at it. It's still up there, with something in the hand. He tries to put together what it is she's telling him, and it arrives very late, a piece at a time.

He opens his fingers. They don't want to. Two of them have stopped being any kind of fingers.

It stays where he put it, that heavy thing, on the edge of the step.

* * *

The clerk has raised the slate.

He has been holding it up for some time. His arm's shaking. He's a very neat, dry little man whom Gazel chose because he can't be surprised. He's got tears running into his collar. Testarossa reads hers before she looks up. She holds it at the same height as his.

*Yielded* has not been lit. Nobody has any doubts about that. There's the other word, on both copies, next to one name.

*Ended.*

The word comes down from both sides. It's a clerk's voice, not a large one. It says the name, and it says the result, and the time, because that's what it says on the form.

Nobody cheers.

You would think someone might. The roads are open, and the city's safe. There are people here from towns that would have burned. The worst thing that has ever walked in any world is dead on a step, and they all saw it. They have waited for this, some of them, for quite a long time. They look at the small figure down there, kneeling, with one arm, and his chin and his whole chest black in the evening light. It's turned its head now and it's looking in their direction. It's got a wheel over it. It's got something moving around its waist.

A child begins to cry, up in the delegations. Then a good many people find that it's time to go.

Makima sits back.

She does it slowly, with a small sigh, as you do when the good part's over and you find you've been holding your tea. There's nothing for her on the table. The little place she kept free is empty and it's going to stay that way. She looks at that for a moment and lets it go, quite easily. She has never in her life needed to be paid in only one kind of coin.

She looks along the benches, at all of those faces. She looks at the man with the brother on the road. He's staring down at the field, and he has one hand raised a little way in front of his chest, without seeming to know it, as you would to keep something off.

“There,” she says, and she smiles. “Now they know his name.” ᴹᵏ

* * *

Down on the field somebody has taken the rope away.

It's a long way off. It takes a while for that to reach him. He's still kneeling. He's looking up the slope, and it's all grey, all of it, every bit, the stones and the people going away and the long scarred sky. There's so much of it. And out of the grey at the very top, very small, coming down the steps much too fast, there's the only colour in the world.
