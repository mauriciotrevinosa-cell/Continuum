# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 60
## Chapter 13 — Two Bridges

**Status:** LN PRODUCTION DRAFT — S9 v0.2 (2026-11-03)
**Volume:** 60
**Reading order:** 13
**Source episode:** S9E51
**Continuity state:** B+142 afternoon. Ori's owed round, V58#10. Frieren is her mother and his teacher; she left the field before the end and asks what she has heard. Frieren offers slow study of the archive; nothing opened.

*When: Ori unfolds the map on his blanket, Mau sees there are now three bridges.*

“We improved it,” she says. ᴼ

Frieren has come in behind her and has gone straight to the reading chair, or at any rate to the chair that's there. She sits and opens a book. Nobody has asked her to explain why she's here. She may be planning to be here for quite some time, and she doesn't seem to feel that it requires comment.

Mau gets his left hand out from under the blanket. He's up on three pillows today, by special permission. He can lift a game piece if it's a big one, and if nobody makes him do it in a hurry.

“Where's the. New bridge. Go?” ᴹ

“You have to find out.” ᴼ

It's a fair answer. She sets out his pieces for him, with her small square serious fingers, then sets out hers. She looks at the board with her chin on her fist. She's a good deal better at this than she was a week ago. He has heard she's been playing Fern.

She moves. He moves. It takes him a long while, and she waits, swinging one foot. She has her mother's way of waiting.

He loses a piece. He looks at where it was, and he can't for the moment see how that happened.

“There's a boy at the inn,” Ori says. ᴼ

She hasn't looked up. She has moved another piece, and she's squaring it on its space.

In the corner a page turns, at exactly the same speed as the page before it.

“His father came to watch. He says you ate him.” ᴼ

The sounds from the yard come in through the window. Mau looks at the top of her head. There's a very straight parting. Someone did that for her, this morning, with great precision, and probably a thousand years of practice at parting things.

There's a good many ways you could go at this. Everyone has been so careful. They've been so kind.

“I bit him,” Mau says. ᴹ

Ori nods. She seems to be checking it against something.

“I went out,” she says. “Before. I took Agott. So I didn't see.” ᴼ

“I know. You did. That was good.” ᴹ

“Did you eat him?” ᴼ

Frieren's page doesn't turn.

He thinks about it. She has asked him a straight question. In his whole life, in either of them, nobody's ever been so entirely uninterested in hearing the wrong answer.

“No,” he says. “I spat it out.” ᴹ

“Was it nice?” ᴼ

“No.” He breathes for a moment. “It was. Really terrible.” ᴹ

She looks up then. She has got very large eyes, and they go over his face, slowly, from one side to the other. She takes in the eye that won't stay where it's put, and the bandage, and the bristle coming in all over his scalp, a good deal faster than hair ought to, with pale flecks in it that weren't there before. He's seen her do this with a page of numbers. He had a whole other life in which he was her father, and she knows nothing about that, and he has never said. What she knows is that he's terrible at this game, and that he has an account with her.

Whatever she was looking for, she seems to have found it. She looks back at the board.

“I'll tell him,” she says. “It's your turn. That's the bridge you don't want.” ᴼ

* * *

He loses. It's not close. She gathers up the pieces and informs him of his position in the wider accounts, which isn't good, and she goes off to see whether Ear is allowed in here yet. She takes the stairs one at a time, with the map held out carefully in front of her.

The room's quiet for a while.

“You hold your wrist wrong,” Frieren says. ᶠ

He turns his head. She hasn't looked up from the book. It's about what plants do in the winter, as far as he can make out.

“It's a very old fault. I told you eleven times. It drops at the end of the release.” ᶠ

“It worked.” ᴹ

“Four hundred of them worked. You'd have needed a hundred and twenty.” ᶠ

She reads for a little longer. Then she marks her place, with one thin finger, and sets the book on her knee. She looks at him across the room. He can never tell what she's thinking. He's had two whole lives to learn and it hasn't helped.

“You came back first,” she says. ᶠ

“Yes.” ᴹ

“I saw. I stood up. Ori was very surprised.” ᶠ

She considers this for a moment, as if it's something that happened to someone else.

“I told you receiving a thing and understanding it were different jobs. You've received it. How is it?” ᶠ

It takes him some time to answer. He can feel it from here. It's down there, a very long way below the floor of everything, and something's sitting against the door. It doesn't push or knock. It's just a thousand years, in a box, with a kitchen in it. He knows how a cut goes. Every now and then he finds that he knows something else, something about a river town, and he hadn't asked.

“Big,” he says. ᴹ

She nods.

“Mine was only a century, and I took a great many winters over it. A little at a time. I would have gone mad otherwise, and I'm quite hard to drive mad.” ᶠ

Mau looks at her.

“You don't have to open it,” Frieren says. “You don't have to do anything with it at all. If you ever want to, I've got time. I have a great deal of time. We could start with something small.” ᶠ

She picks up her book again and finds the place. Her eyes go back along the line.

“You can learn why someone fights,” she says, to the page, “without accepting what they did to get there.” ᶠ

“I know.” ᴹ

“You sometimes need to hear things you know.” ᶠ

Outside there's a scrabbling on the stairs, and a thump. A very excited dog is explaining something to a very small person, who's explaining in return that this is not a thing you do in a clinic. Frieren turns a page.

She's got about four hours left on the wall. She has written herself down, under *Nights*, in very small neat letters. He'll find that out tomorrow. Nobody had the nerve to ask her about it.
