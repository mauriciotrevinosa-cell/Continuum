## Chapter 15 — What They Call It

**Continuity state:** B+145. Away from the house. Hollis on the road; accounts travelling; Uraume and the body under the custodians' conditions; Yuuki's table, observer only. No new attack, no new plot beyond what was already moving. Observer rules still bar following.

*When: Hollis stops at the Ashby turning to water the mules, a woman takes her boy indoors.*

The road's open. That's the first thing anyone says, and they say it with their eyes on his wagon.

It's a good road again. He has come down it for two days with nobody asking him for a fee, past the place where the marker was, and the marker's gone. There are four carts behind him that didn't dare to come through last month. Everyone's selling. Salt's down by a third. He ought to be a very happy man.

“You're from up there,” says the fellow at the trough. “You've seen him.”

“I've sold him onions,” Hollis says. ᴴᵒ

The fellow looks at him, and then at his hands. He steps back a little, in a casual way, as you might from a dog you don't know. He says they've got it in Ashby that he's nine feet tall. They've got it that he has a wheel on his head and that there are soldiers living in his stomach, and he opens his shirt to let them out. A cousin was there. A cousin saw him tear out a man's throat with his teeth, then get up after he'd been dead a quarter of an hour, and walk across the field with his guts held in by hands.

“Is that true?”

Hollis thinks about it, and he pulls up the bucket. He has known that boy for a long time. He has seen him get told off by a very small girl about a shelf.

“He likes his onions fried,” he says. ᴴᵒ

The fellow nods slowly. It isn't what he asked, and he knows it. By the time Hollis has the mules back in the traces, a boy has been sent running along the row of houses with the news. Nobody comes out to buy.

Farther along, by the bridge, someone has chalked a thing on the wall. It's only a circle with some lines through it. There are eight of them. He has seen that on three walls now, since the border. He doesn't know if it's meant to keep something away, or bring it.

It is the second letter that week from a town which would like to know, respectfully, what the settlement's intentions are. Fern puts it with the others. It's quite a tall pile.

Rimuru reads the top one, standing up, with a piece of toast.

“They're thanking us,” he says. ᴿ

“Yes.” ᶠʳ

“It's the most frightened thank-you I've ever seen. Look at this. They've offered him a *statue*.” ᴿ

Fern doesn't say anything. She's making a note of the date.

* * *

There's a very high place, north of anywhere, where nobody has ever had a reason to go.

It's cold there. That's no trouble to the person who has walked for nine days to get to it. They came by the route the custodians named, and they took nothing with them from the field but what they'd asked for, wrapped in a long clean cloth. They carried it the whole way themselves. It's heavy. The head's been bound up with a good deal of care, and it's been hard to do anything about the shape of it.

They've made a place for it, on the rock. They've spent some time on that.

Now Uraume is cooking.

They've brought what's needed. It isn't anything grand. There's a small fire, kept low with one long pale hand, and a pan, and some things that were hard to get. They've made this dish perhaps two hundred times, over a period of a thousand years. It has to be got just right; he could always tell. He'd have looked at it, and then at them, and there'd have been that small movement at the corner of his mouth. It was a very long time before they learned that that meant *adequate*.

They plate it properly. They set it on the rock.

The wind blows a little. It's very clean up here. You can see a long way, in every direction, and over to the south there's a thin straight line across the sky that hasn't quite faded yet. It's still there. They've been looking at that for nine days. It is the finest thing he ever made.

He sent them away.

*Out*, he said. He'd looked at them when he said it. They've gone over it a good many times. They know what he was doing. He never once in all that time explained himself, and he didn't then.

They stay until the food has gone cold. Then they take the plate, and they eat it, standing up. It's very good. They clean the pan with snow.

There's nothing written down anywhere about what to do next. They've never needed to know.

“So,” says Yuuki Kagurazaka, and he slides a small piece of paper across the table. ʸᵘ

It's a nice table. It's in a nice room, a long way from the quarry, and they all came home by separate roads as they said they would. Nobody's following anyone. That was very clearly specified. He has got his feet up, and he has got that pleasant smile back on, and if you didn't know him you might say it fits as well as ever.

Laplace picks up the paper and looks at it. It's a child's drawing, or something like one, a circle with eight lines. He has seen it too.

“Well, that's a big scary fellow, sure,” Laplace says. “Bites people. Very rude. I thought we were all terribly worried about the slime?” ᴸᵃ

“We were.” ʸᵘ

Yuuki looks out the window for a while. He's still smiling. He's thinking about a man on a floor, in the evening, who had one hand and no energy and every reason to lie still. He has had some time to think about what you could do with a man like that, and what you couldn't. He has been very clever about the slime for years.

“I think,” he says, “we may have been looking at the wrong one.” ʸᵘ
