Frieren had taken his hand in her sleep and put it under her cheek.

That was the first difficulty.

The second was that she had also taken most of the blanket, although the morning coming through the east window was already warm. Mau lay still and considered what could be done about either problem without waking her.

Nothing, probably.

There was a little crease between her eyebrows. A strand of hair had caught against her mouth. He moved it with his free hand, and the crease disappeared.

"You're awake," she said.

"You're pretending."

"I'm resting."

"With my hand."

She adjusted her cheek on it.

"It's a good hand."

He looked at the ceiling for a moment, trying not to laugh enough to move her.

From the other side of the adjoining door came a soft knock, then a second knock much lower down, as though something had bumped the wood.

"You can come in," Mau said.

Ori appeared in the doorway with a red ribbon tied around her wrist.

The other end was tied loosely around Milim's ankle.

Milim floated behind her, asleep on her back, one arm trailing toward the floor. The ribbon had caught on the door handle. Ori stopped, went back, lifted it off, and came in again. Milim revolved very slowly.

Frieren opened one eye.

"She was going the other way," Ori explained.

"Where?" Mau asked.

"Toward the window."

"Good catch."

Ori looked pleased. She brought her floating passenger to the side of the bed and sat down, leaving enough slack that Milim drifted above the footboard.

"Are we going to breakfast?"

"As soon as she gives my hand back."

"You can have it," Frieren said.

She did not move.

Ori leaned over her and nudged Mau's shoulder. "I need you to decide something."

"That sounds serious."

"Milim says the person who wakes up first gets to choose what we do. But she doesn't wake up."

"That sounds like you've already won."

"She says floating counts as being up."

Frieren shut her eye again. "It doesn't."

"Thank you," said Ori, with the satisfaction of someone who had brought an appeal to the proper authority.

Milim made a small sound in her sleep.

Mau raised his voice a little. "Someone might finish the breakfast before we get there."

Her eyes opened.

For a moment she looked at all three of them upside down. Then she rolled over in the air, put her feet down, and discovered the ribbon.

"Oh. Good morning."

"You were escaping," Ori said.

"I was asleep."

"Both," said Mau.

Frieren finally released his hand. There was a warm mark on his palm from her cheek.

He sat up. Ori began untying the ribbon, and Milim waited, unusually patient, while Frieren found the end that had tightened against itself. The four of them bent over the knot as if it were the morning's first important undertaking.

By the time it was undone, Mau had lost the blanket altogether.

* * *

Rimuru was halfway through asking somebody to pass the bread when Milim caught him.

He had chosen his slime shape that morning. Milim scooped him from the bench and pressed him to her cheek.

"Rimuru!"

"I was using that seat."

"Good morning!"

"Apparently."

Mau took the bread that had been left suspended between two people and put it in reach of Rimuru's new position.

"There. Now you can still participate."

"You're enjoying this."

"A little."

"Brotherhood is dead."

"Morning, Oppa."

Yuka had saved a place with one hand and was eating with the other. She moved her cup as Mau sat down. Frieren took the place on his other side, and Ori went around to sit beside Milim before she could carry Rimuru away from the table altogether.

"You're late," Yuka said.

"There was a knot."

"That's a terrible excuse."

"It was a complicated knot."

Yuka glanced past him. Frieren was tearing a piece of bread in half without the slightest interest in defending the explanation.

"Cards after lunch," Yuka said. "Umaru says you're playing."

"Does Umaru know I'm playing?"

"It was her idea."

"Then why are you asking?"

"I'm telling you."

Across the table, Umaru raised her cup in confirmation.

"And no letting Milim win. She knows when you do it."

"Of course I know," Milim said. "He gets the same face."

"What face?"

She attempted an expression of enormous, gentle stupidity.

Rimuru started laughing. It made her hands wobble.

"All right," Mau said. "I deserve that."

Yuka slid him the jam before he could ask for it. Then she began explaining a disagreement about the rules which, from Umaru's immediate objection, had begun well before breakfast.

Mau listened. When he tried to settle it, both of them told him he had misunderstood.

Frieren put the other half of her bread on his plate.

"Eat first," she said.

He did.

Farther down, Momo was arguing with Okarun about whose turn it had been to fetch something. Someone laughed in the kitchen. A chair scraped, and another person squeezed in without requiring everyone to move. The table made room in small increments.

Mau reached for the jam and discovered Yuka had already taken it back.

"You were slow," she said.

* * *

Shoko's first question was how he had slept.

Mau sat on the edge of the examination bed and thought about the trapped hand, the ribbon, the long interval between hearing Ori and finally getting up.

"Pretty well."

She looked at him, then wrote it down.

"That sounded almost unqualified."

"I can try again."

"Don't."

She took his wrist. Her fingers were cool, her attention exact. The window stood open enough to let the air move through the clinic. On the desk, a page had been turned face down under a cup.

"Any dizziness?"

"No."

"Pain?"

"No."

"Anything you're planning to call insignificant until I ask the same question a different way?"

He considered this.

"Gojo's waiting outside."

"Unfortunately, that's chronic."

Mau laughed. Shoko kept her fingers on his wrist until he had finished.

She checked the things she always checked, asked him to follow her finger, and made him repeat an answer when he gave it too quickly. When she was satisfied, she let go.

"The short practice you agreed on. Not the short practice Satoru will describe afterward."

"I heard that," Gojo called from outside.

"Good. Saves me explaining twice."

Mau pulled his sleeve down. "What are you doing after this?"

"Finishing these. Then going out."

"Where?"

"I haven't decided. That's part of the appeal."

He smiled. "Sounds nice."

"I thought so."

She reached past him for the next sheet on the desk. He took that for the dismissal it was and got off the bed.

At the door he almost collided with Gojo, who had been leaning on the frame.

"How am I?" Gojo asked Shoko.

"In the way."

"Excellent. Stable condition."

Mau followed him out before he could ask for a second opinion.

* * *

Gojo put three small stones on the ground.

"That's the whole lesson?"

"Do you want more stones?"

"I'm trying to understand the budget."

"Walk through. Don't look down."

Mau looked at the stones, then at him.

Gojo smiled.

"You already did. Start again."

They worked in the shade, far enough from the door that nobody needed to duck around them. No techniques. No attempt to force his body into something it was not ready to hold. Gojo changed where he stood, lifted a hand, asked Mau a question at exactly the point where the answer made him forget his feet.

The second stone caught his heel.

"Again," said Gojo.

"You moved it."

"I moved me. You followed."

Mau went back. This time he kept his eyes up and shortened the step without thinking about it.

Gojo tapped his shoulder as he passed.

"Better."

After a while the three stones became an absurdly difficult arrangement for three stones. Mau stopped trying to anticipate what Gojo wanted and began to notice what he was actually doing. The small shift of weight. The shoulder that lied. The foot that didn't.

He got through once.

"There," Gojo said.

Mau turned, pleased, and kicked the last stone on his way back.

Gojo bent over laughing.

"You could've waited," Mau said.

"I really couldn't."

They sat down when the agreed time was up. Gojo sat first, which made it easier for Mau to follow without either of them having to announce anything about stopping.

For a few minutes they watched a procession of people going about things neither of them was responsible for. Coco passed with Tetia and a sheet she was protecting from the wind. Qifrey, coming after them, told them to walk rather than run. They slowed until they were out of sight.

Gojo held out his water.

Mau took a drink and passed it back.

"Doing anything later?" he asked.

"Several extremely impressive things."

"Such as?"

Gojo watched the doorway to the clinic. "Undecided."

Mau looked at him.

"Don't," Gojo said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You have an apprentice face. It's insufferable."

Mau smiled into his own shoulder.

They stayed until Gojo had finished the water.

* * *

Kaneki was moving the last of a stack of dry wood under cover.

There wasn't much left. He had already done the difficult part, making a space where the newer pieces could sit apart from the older ones. Mau stopped, looked at the two piles, and asked which was going where.

Kaneki pointed.

"Those first. Keep the gap underneath."

Mau picked up an armful.

"I had a system," Kaneki said after the second trip.

"I'm following it."

"You put that one backward."

Mau looked at the piece in his hand. Kaneki looked at him. Then the corner of Kaneki's mouth moved.

"Very funny."

"You checked."

They finished without needing much conversation. Mau held the cover while Kaneki secured it, and Kaneki waited while Mau worked a splinter out of his palm.

"Let me see."

"It's out."

Kaneki turned his hand over anyway, checked, and let go.

They sat for a little while with coffee. Mau's tasted stronger than he'd expected. He tried not to show it.

"You can put it down," Kaneki said.

"It's good."

"That's not what I said."

Mau put it down. Kaneki smiled properly this time.

"What have you got after this?" Mau asked.

"A few things. Nothing that needs doing this minute."

"That's new."

"A little."

Kaneki moved his chair a few inches so his knees were in the sun. Mau did the same.

The covered wood stayed exactly where they had put it.

* * *

Mai was trying to make a strip of cloth stay flat while Maki marked it.

"Hold that," Mai told Mau when he passed.

He held it.

"Not there."

He moved his hand.

"There."

Maki drew the line and sat back. Mai lifted the cloth, inspected it, and decided it would do.

"What is it?" Mau asked.

"A strap," said Mai.

"I got that far."

"Then you're doing well."

There was a second strip on the bench. Mai placed it under his hand without asking whether he had anywhere else to be. Maki's expression suggested she had been waiting for this to happen to somebody else.

"How was practice?" she asked.

"Three stones. Lost twice."

"Against the stones?"

"He was helping them."

Maki nodded as though this was a perfectly plausible tactical disadvantage.

Mai smoothed a frayed edge with her thumb. "You're leaning on it."

Mau straightened. "Do I get instructions before the mistake?"

"Then how would you learn?"

He stayed for the next strip too. Mai did not have to tell him where to hold it. When they were finished, Maki swept the scraps into her palm and Mai caught one that had stuck to his sleeve.

"You missed one," she said.

She put it with the others.

At the far end of the passage, Bocchi was standing with her guitar, looking in their direction and then away again.

Mau lifted a hand.

She lifted hers very quickly.

Mai glanced over. "Go. We've used you enough."

"That sounds generous."

"Don't ruin it."

* * *

Bocchi wanted to know whether he had a minute.

He asked if she needed help carrying something.

"No," she said. Then, with an effort: "Listening."

They found somewhere to sit where they would not be in the way. Bocchi settled the guitar against herself. Mau waited while she adjusted a string that, to his ears, had sounded right already.

"It's not finished."

"Okay."

"And I changed it this morning. So if it—"

"Bocchi."

She looked up.

"Do you want to tell me about it first, or play it first?"

She considered that. Her fingers stopped twisting at the tuning peg.

"Play it."

"Okay."

It began with a few notes spaced far apart. Then the gap shortened, and the tune found something beneath it that Mau hadn't known he was waiting for. He looked at her hands, then away so she wouldn't have to feel watched.

Partway through, she stopped.

"That part's wrong."

"Play it again?"

She did. This time she went farther.

Kita came looking for her before they were done. She paused when she heard the guitar, then leaned against the wall rather than interrupting.

When Bocchi stopped, Mau let the last note finish.

"The bit you played twice," he said. "I like the second way. It feels like it's going somewhere and then decides to stay."

Bocchi looked down at the strings.

"That's the bit I changed."

"Then I like your change."

Kita smiled. "I said something like that. With less staying."

"You said it sounded like a window," Bocchi told her.

"An open one."

Mau nodded. "I was going to say that next."

Bocchi laughed. Quietly, but he heard it.

Kita reached for her hand, waited until Bocchi had put down the pick, and took it.

"Nijika's got Ryo. We should go before she loses her."

Bocchi stood, then hesitated.

"When it's finished—"

"I'd like to hear it."

"Even if it's different?"

"Especially."

She nodded. This time she did not have to check his face to see whether he meant it.

He watched them go a little way, their hands joined and Bocchi carrying her guitar in the other one. Then he realized he had been tapping the changed part against his knee.

He was still trying to get it right when the call for lunch came.

* * *

At lunch he got the tune wrong, and Carmilla, passing behind him, supplied three notes under her breath.

Mau turned.

"You know it?"

"A little."

"She's changed it."

"I know."

There was something pleased in her voice. She went to find her place before he could say anything else.

Mau sat down between Yuka and Rimuru. Frieren was farther along with Fern, her book closed over one finger. Stark was listening to something Fern said with the careful attention of someone who knew a question was coming.

"How were your stones?" Rimuru asked.

"How do you know about the stones?"

"Satoru. He was very proud of them."

"They're talented."

Rimuru passed him a bowl. "I told him so."

Yuka wanted to know whether the practice was difficult. Mau described it, and she immediately arranged three crumbs beside her plate to demonstrate why he should have stepped differently.

"He kept moving," Mau said.

"So move the other way."

"I'll tell him you said that."

"Tell him to invite me. I'll show you."

Rimuru grinned at his bowl.

"Not a word," Mau told him.

"Wasn't going to say anything."

The meal took longer than Mau had expected. Somebody brought more water. Yuka got up to fetch it and came back caught in a conversation with Umaru, who had brought the cards to prove her point from breakfast. They spread four of them beside the crumbs and argued in lowered voices until Fern looked over.

"After lunch," she said.

They gathered the cards.

When the dishes began to move, Mau stood and took a stack to the kitchen. Rem was already there. She handed him a cloth rather than taking the plates from him.

"Those can go on the shelf when they're dry."

He wiped. She washed. A little later someone came in with the cups, and the work rearranged itself around another pair of hands.

He was reaching for the last bowl when Minerva appeared in the doorway.

"Tea," she said.

"Now?"

"When you've finished that."

She took a cloth and dried the bowl herself.

* * *

Sekhmet had occupied the bench before anyone else arrived.

"We could sit outside," Daphne said.

"You could," Sekhmet agreed.

Minerva set down the pot. "There's shade."

"There's shade here."

Mau looked at the stretch of bench between her feet and the end.

"Can I have a little of it?"

She moved one foot. He sat.

The others gathered at their own pace. Echidna brought a book, which she put beside her cup without opening. Carmilla sat near the end, where she could see the passage. Satella took the chair beside Mau and turned it a little toward the open window. Pride arrived last, on time.

"You have something in your hair," she told him.

Mau reached up.

"Other side."

He found a flake of dry wood.

"It's been a productive morning," he said.

"So I see."

Minerva poured. Mau reached for the pot when her own cup was still empty, and she surrendered it without comment.

Daphne was talking about peas.

Not growing them. Eating them before they were supposed to be eaten. She had discovered that the difference between these two stages was of great importance to other people.

"There are more on the plant," she said.

"There are fewer on the plant," Echidna replied. "That is the objection."

"They were good."

"An excellent defence. Unlikely to succeed with the person counting them."

Mau passed the plate of bread to Daphne before she had to reach across Satella.

"You could ask which ones are ready."

"I did ask."

"And?"

"I disagreed."

Sekhmet made a faint sound that might have been laughter.

The discussion lasted until Daphne had finished the bread nearest her. By then Echidna had opened the book, not to read but to show Carmilla a loose leaf she was worried about. The thread at the binding had worn through.

Mau leaned over to see.

"Can it be fixed?"

"Yes."

"Do you need—"

"Coco and I are doing it tomorrow."

"Oh."

"You may watch if you can resist improving the arrangement."

"I can watch."

Echidna looked at him over the book.

"I can try to watch."

"Better."

Carmilla touched the edge of the loose page and asked whether the sewing would show. Echidna explained. Satella listened until the conversation moved to a kind of stitch, then looked out of the window again.

"Is it cooler at the river?" she asked.

"A little," Mau said. "In the morning."

"I went yesterday," Carmilla told her. "Before everyone else."

"Was it quiet?"

"Very."

Satella turned her cup in both hands. "I might go."

"You should," said Minerva. "I'll come if you want."

Satella nodded. It took a moment, but the nod was definite.

Mau had been about to offer to go with her. He drank his tea instead.

Pride watched him over the rim of her cup.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing. How was your morning?"

He told them about the knot. Sekhmet wanted to know whether sleeping in the air was more restful, and Minerva said that was not the useful part of the story. Then he told them about the stones, which proved even less dignified on the third telling.

Carmilla laughed with one hand over her mouth.

"I heard a song," he added.

Her hand came down.

"The new part?"

"I think so. It was good."

"It is," she said.

They sat for another cup. Daphne wanted to know what the game after lunch involved, and Mau admitted that he was not certain he understood the rules anymore.

"You agreed anyway?" Pride asked.

"They're teaching me."

"Are they?"

"That's what they said."

She smiled.

When he got up, Sekhmet's foot moved back into the warm place he had left. Minerva caught the empty pot before he could pick it up.

"Your game," she reminded him.

He thanked her and went to find out whether he had been taught the rules or merely told them.

* * *

Umaru had chosen a table where there was room to put down the cards properly.

She had also chosen Mau's seat.

"There."

He sat.

Yuka was opposite him, Milim beside her. Ori had pulled up a chair to watch, bringing the ribbon with her. It lay coiled on the table, safe from Milim's absent-minded fingers.

Frieren sat a little apart with her book open. One foot rested against Mau's ankle under the table.

Umaru dealt.

"Are we using your rules or Yuka's?" he asked.

"The rules."

"That's encouraging."

The first round went quickly. Mau was still arranging his cards when Milim put hers down with a cry of triumph.

"You can't do that," Yuka said.

"I just did."

"It's the wrong one."

Milim examined it. "It's nearly the same."

Umaru took the card, returned it, and explained. Milim listened with the expression she wore when somebody was telling her a deeply unreasonable law of nature.

Mau waited. On the next turn he made precisely the mistake Umaru had just explained.

Yuka put her face in her hands.

"Oppa."

"I know."

"You were sitting here."

"I know."

"I think he's doing the face," Milim said suspiciously.

Frieren turned a page. "No."

Mau looked at her.

"That was real," she said.

Ori laughed so hard she had to move the ribbon away from her elbow.

They played again.

It became easier after Mau stopped trying to remember every instruction at once. He began to notice when Yuka's silence meant a good hand and when it meant she was waiting for somebody else to make a mistake. Milim could not conceal either condition. Umaru could conceal anything she wanted until she began to win, when her shoulders rose a fraction.

"You do that," he told her.

"Do what?"

He imitated it.

She stared at him, betrayed.

Yuka leaned across to study Umaru's shoulders. Milim did the same. Ori was now watching them instead of the cards.

"You're all distracting me," Umaru said.

"We're learning," said Mau.

She won that round anyway.

On the next, he kept the card he wanted to play and put down the less useful one. Yuka glanced up. A little later she made a choice that ruined whatever Umaru had been preparing.

Mau looked at Yuka.

She looked back, very innocent.

He lost by one turn.

"You helped her," Umaru accused him.

"I helped me. Badly."

"He's improving," Yuka said.

"I'm proud."

She kicked him lightly under the table.

Frieren's foot moved away from the confusion, then returned to his ankle when it was safe.

They let Ori take the next hand. Milim moved her chair over to advise her, which chiefly consisted of whispering the value of each card loudly enough for everyone to hear. Ori listened, thanked her, and made her own choice.

Mau leaned back while they argued about the result.

Umaru took his empty cup and pushed hers toward him. There was still some water in it.

"Here."

He drank.

"Another after this?" she asked.

"Yuta's waiting for me later."

"After that."

He looked at the half-finished game, at the room beyond it, where the light had moved across the floor without asking anyone to notice.

"After that," he said.

Umaru nodded and took her cup back.

Frieren lifted her eyes from the book.

"Listen to this."

All five of them paused. She read two lines, frowned at a word, and read them again. Mau asked her what the word meant. She began to explain, and Yuka went back to sorting the cards, listening. Milim lowered her head onto her arms. Ori rested her chin on her hand.

Umaru dealt the next round slowly enough that Frieren could finish.

* * *

Yuta had two practice weapons and no stones.

"Already better," Mau told him.

"What?"

"Nothing."

They started slowly. Yuta had him step out of reach, then back into it, without hurrying the return. Mau got the distance right and the timing wrong. The next time he reversed the problem.

"You're trying to finish before you start," Yuta said.

"That would save time."

Yuta smiled. "It would. Try it the other way."

There was no need to hit hard. Sometimes Yuta stopped halfway and let Mau see exactly where his weight had gone. Sometimes he let the mistake finish, a light touch to the shoulder or the wrist, and waited for Mau to work out why.

Once Mau found the gap. He stopped short, surprised by it.

Yuta nodded.

"Again."

They put the weapons away before it became something either of them would need to recover from.

Mau sat on the low step and rubbed the heel of his hand. Yuta sat beside him. For a while they watched the shadows under the trees grow long enough to join.

"Maki says you lost to stones."

Mau closed his eyes.

Yuta's laugh was quiet, but it lasted.

"Did you have a good day?" he asked when he had finished.

"Yeah. You?"

"Yeah."

"Yuka's getting very good at cards," Mau said.

"I heard."

"She looks at me like Maki when I do something stupid."

"I know that look."

Mau turned toward him. Yuta was trying to keep a straight face.

"Does it work on you?"

"Usually."

"Good. I thought it was just me."

Yuta picked a loose thread from his sleeve. "I like hearing her from the other room. When she's playing."

Mau understood that. The noise travelled farther than anybody needed it to, and sometimes that was the nice part.

"Come play with us," he said.

"I'm not very good."

"Neither am I. It's how they get you to stay."

"Does that work?"

"Usually."

Yuta laughed again. They sat until the light on the step had gone.

Then Yuta got up and held out his hand.

Mau took it.

"Food?"

"Please."

* * *

The evening meal was noisier than lunch.

Gojo came in late and did not explain where he had been. Shoko came in a little later with her hair disturbed by the wind. Mau caught Gojo looking up, caught himself about to smile, and concentrated on his plate.

Across from him, Maki noticed all three things.

She said nothing, which was worse.

Ori had a story to tell him. It involved a game she and Milim had been playing, a rule Milim had introduced after losing, and the argument that followed. Milim objected to the word *losing*.

Mau asked Ori what she had done.

"I said if it counted for her it counted for me."

"And?"

"She doesn't like the rule anymore."

"It was a bad rule," Milim said.

"You invented it."

"And I fixed it."

Rimuru nodded solemnly. "Good administration."

Frieren, beside Mau now, put down her spoon. "Don't encourage her."

"I was supporting a colleague."

"You were enjoying it," Mau said.

Rimuru smiled. "A little."

Yuka wanted to finish the card game. Umaru wanted to start a better one. They argued about whether these were the same thing while the plates were cleared.

Mau took his to the kitchen, dried what was handed to him, and went back before they had to come looking.

This time he won a round.

It was not a particularly impressive win. Milim had misunderstood something, Yuka had been laughing at her, and Umaru had taken a risk that did not work. Still, when he put the last card down, the silence was satisfying.

He lifted both hands.

"I would like to thank my teachers."

"No," said Umaru.

"For their patience."

"Absolutely not."

Yuka was already gathering the cards. "Again."

He lost the next one.

Later, when there were fewer people at the table, Bocchi came back with Kita to retrieve something from the room. Mau tapped the little rhythm against the edge of his cup.

He got it wrong.

Bocchi stopped, listened, and corrected him with two fingers against her own palm.

He tried again.

She nodded.

"That one."

Kita waited for her at the door. Bocchi caught up, glanced back once, and then they were gone.

Mau kept the rhythm for a while after that, quietly enough that Frieren did not have to raise her voice when she resumed telling him about the word in her book.

* * *

Milim was asleep before they reached the room.

This time she had begun drifting while Ori was still talking to her. Rimuru helped steer her around the corner, muttering that he was being promoted to furniture. Ori had kept the ribbon.

Mau followed them to the adjoining doorway and waited while Ori made sure the knot at the bedpost would hold. Frieren checked that there was enough length for Milim to turn without tangling herself.

"She'll say it counts as being up again," Ori said.

"You have the ruling," Mau reminded her.

Ori looked at Frieren.

"It stands," Frieren said.

Ori smiled, then came back for a hug before either of them could go. Mau held her until she let go. She reached for Frieren next.

"Tomorrow?" Ori asked.

"Tomorrow," Mau said.

She left the door open a little.

In their room, Frieren had put her book beside the bed with the place carefully marked. She sat down and began taking the ties from her hair. Mau undid the stubborn one she could not quite reach, then stayed behind her to separate the strands that had caught around it.

"You smell like wood," she said.

"It's better than the coffee."

She tilted her head, considering him.

"The coffee was this morning."

"Long day."

"Mm."

He finished with her hair and sat beside her. Through the floor came the softened sound of somebody moving a bench. A door closed farther along the passage. Someone laughed, then tried to laugh more quietly.

Frieren took his hand.

"Did you finish the game?"

"I won once."

"I saw."

"You were reading."

"You made it difficult."

He leaned against her, laughing, and she let his shoulder stay where it landed.

After a while she got into bed. He put out the light and found his place beside her in the dark. Her hair was loose against the pillow. He moved a strand away from her mouth before it could catch there again.

"Good day?" he asked.

"Yes."

She turned toward him.

"Yours?"

He thought of the tune. Of Kaneki putting his knees in the sun, of Ori winning an argument before breakfast, of the pause before Yuka realized he had finally won. He was going to tell her one of those things.

But he yawned instead.

Frieren smiled where he could barely see it.

"Tomorrow," she said.

He put his hand under her cheek.

"Tomorrow."
