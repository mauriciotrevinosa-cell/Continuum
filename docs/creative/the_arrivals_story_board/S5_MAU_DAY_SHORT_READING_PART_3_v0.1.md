Sekhmet had occupied the bench before anyone else arrived.

"We could sit outside," Daphne said.

"You could," Sekhmet agreed.

Minerva set down the pot. "There's shade."

"There's shade here."

Mau looked at the stretch of bench between her feet and the end.

"Can I have a little of it?"

She moved one foot. He sat.

The others gathered at their own pace. Echidna brought a book, which she put beside her cup without opening. Carmilla sat near the end, where she could see the passage. Satella took the chair beside Mau and turned it a little toward the open window. Pride arrived last, on time.

"You have something in your hair," she told him.

Mau reached up.

"Other side."

He found a flake of dry wood.

"It's been a productive morning," he said.

"So I see."

Minerva poured. Mau reached for the pot when her own cup was still empty, and she surrendered it without comment.

Daphne was talking about peas.

Not growing them. Eating them before they were supposed to be eaten. She had discovered that the difference between these two stages was of great importance to other people.

"There are more on the plant," she said.

"There are fewer on the plant," Echidna replied. "That is the objection."

"They were good."

"An excellent defence. Unlikely to succeed with the person counting them."

Mau passed the plate of bread to Daphne before she had to reach across Satella.

"You could ask which ones are ready."

"I did ask."

"And?"

"I disagreed."

Sekhmet made a faint sound that might have been laughter.

The discussion lasted until Daphne had finished the bread nearest her. By then Echidna had opened the book, not to read but to show Carmilla a loose leaf she was worried about. The thread at the binding had worn through.

Mau leaned over to see.

"Can it be fixed?"

"Yes."

"Do you need—"

"Coco and I are doing it tomorrow."

"Oh."

"You may watch if you can resist improving the arrangement."

"I can watch."

Echidna looked at him over the book.

"I can try to watch."

"Better."

Carmilla touched the edge of the loose page and asked whether the sewing would show. Echidna explained. Satella listened until the conversation moved to a kind of stitch, then looked out of the window again.

"Is it cooler at the river?" she asked.

"A little," Mau said. "In the morning."

"I went yesterday," Carmilla told her. "Before everyone else."

"Was it quiet?"

"Very."

Satella turned her cup in both hands. "I might go."

"You should," said Minerva. "I'll come if you want."

Satella nodded. It took a moment, but the nod was definite.

Mau had been about to offer to go with her. He drank his tea instead.

Pride watched him over the rim of her cup.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing. How was your morning?"

He told them about the knot. Sekhmet wanted to know whether sleeping in the air was more restful, and Minerva said that was not the useful part of the story. Then he told them about the stones, which proved even less dignified on the third telling.

Carmilla laughed with one hand over her mouth.

"I heard a song," he added.

Her hand came down.

"The new part?"

"I think so. It was good."

"It is," she said.

They sat for another cup. Daphne wanted to know what the game after lunch involved, and Mau admitted that he was not certain he understood the rules anymore.

"You agreed anyway?" Pride asked.

"They're teaching me."

"Are they?"

"That's what they said."

She smiled.

When he got up, Sekhmet's foot moved back into the warm place he had left. Minerva caught the empty pot before he could pick it up.

"Your game," she reminded him.

He thanked her and went to find out whether he had been taught the rules or merely told them.

* * *

Umaru had chosen a table where there was room to put down the cards properly.

She had also chosen Mau's seat.

"There."

He sat.

Yuka was opposite him, Milim beside her. Ori had pulled up a chair to watch, bringing the ribbon with her. It lay coiled on the table, safe from Milim's absent-minded fingers.

Frieren sat a little apart with her book open. One foot rested against Mau's ankle under the table.

Umaru dealt.

"Are we using your rules or Yuka's?" he asked.

"The rules."

"That's encouraging."

The first round went quickly. Mau was still arranging his cards when Milim put hers down with a cry of triumph.

"You can't do that," Yuka said.

"I just did."

"It's the wrong one."

Milim examined it. "It's nearly the same."

Umaru took the card, returned it, and explained. Milim listened with the expression she wore when somebody was telling her a deeply unreasonable law of nature.

Mau waited. On the next turn he made precisely the mistake Umaru had just explained.

Yuka put her face in her hands.

"Oppa."

"I know."

"You were sitting here."

"I know."

"I think he's doing the face," Milim said suspiciously.

Frieren turned a page. "No."

Mau looked at her.

"That was real," she said.

Ori laughed so hard she had to move the ribbon away from her elbow.

They played again.

It became easier after Mau stopped trying to remember every instruction at once. He began to notice when Yuka's silence meant a good hand and when it meant she was waiting for somebody else to make a mistake. Milim could not conceal either condition. Umaru could conceal anything she wanted until she began to win, when her shoulders rose a fraction.

"You do that," he told her.

"Do what?"

He imitated it.

She stared at him, betrayed.

Yuka leaned across to study Umaru's shoulders. Milim did the same. Ori was now watching them instead of the cards.

"You're all distracting me," Umaru said.

"We're learning," said Mau.

She won that round anyway.

On the next, he kept the card he wanted to play and put down the less useful one. Yuka glanced up. A little later she made a choice that ruined whatever Umaru had been preparing.

Mau looked at Yuka.

She looked back, very innocent.

He lost by one turn.

"You helped her," Umaru accused him.

"I helped me. Badly."

"He's improving," Yuka said.

"I'm proud."

She kicked him lightly under the table.

Frieren's foot moved away from the confusion, then returned to his ankle when it was safe.

They let Ori take the next hand. Milim moved her chair over to advise her, which chiefly consisted of whispering the value of each card loudly enough for everyone to hear. Ori listened, thanked her, and made her own choice.

Mau leaned back while they argued about the result.

Umaru took his empty cup and pushed hers toward him. There was still some water in it.

"Here."

He drank.

"Another after this?" she asked.

"Yuta's waiting for me later."

"After that."

He looked at the half-finished game, at the room beyond it, where the light had moved across the floor without asking anyone to notice.

"After that," he said.

Umaru nodded and took her cup back.

Frieren lifted her eyes from the book.

"Listen to this."

All five of them paused. She read two lines, frowned at a word, and read them again. Mau asked her what the word meant. She began to explain, and Yuka went back to sorting the cards, listening. Milim lowered her head onto her arms. Ori rested her chin on her hand.

Umaru dealt the next round slowly enough that Frieren could finish.
