*When: B+155, late morning. Kaisel sets them down in a dry field behind the Ashby road, and Mau sends him back into the dark before anyone has to look at him.*

“One hour,” Yuka says. “She said one hour, and pears.” ʸ

“She said no lifting.” ᴹ

“You can carry the pears.” ʸ

“Is that lifting?” ᴹ

“I'll carry the pears.” ʸ

It's a real market day. He can hear it from the road. There are about forty stalls along the wall of the town, and a man with a very bad flute. It is the first place he has been in three weeks that has no one in it who knows how he takes his tea. Yuka walks a little ahead with her hands in her pockets. She's got her good coat on, and she's looking at everything. He gives his attention to walking like somebody who doesn't use a cane because he has to.

He's looking forward to this. He'd like that known. He had been looking forward to it since yesterday.

It starts at the first stall. A woman selling onions looks up, with a smile already on her face and her mouth open to tell him the price. The smile stays there. Her eyes go to his neck. They go to his hair. She looks at him for about as long as it takes to breathe in, and then she looks down at her onions, very carefully, and she moves a few of them around.

He doesn't think anything of that.

At the next stall, a man stops talking. Then the two men beside him do. It's not a big thing. It's like when you walk across a room and someone has already shut the window. There's a space opening up in front of them along the row, a person wide, then two. People have found that they would like to be looking at a bolt of cloth. Somebody's child starts to say something in a high, carrying voice, and is picked up in the middle of the word and taken indoors.

The flute has stopped.

Yuka hasn't changed her pace. She has taken her hands out of her pockets.

On the wall behind the cheese seller, at about the height of a man's shoulder, someone has drawn a circle in chalk. It has eight lines through it. There's another one farther down. It's been done carefully, by someone who wanted to get it right, and at the bottom of it someone has left a small dish with a little salt.

He looks at that for a while.

*I see it,* Ciel says. ᶜⁱ

*What is it for?*

*I don't know. I don't think they know either.*

* * *

The pears are at the far end.

It's an old man, with a tray of them on a barrel. They're good pears, you can smell them from here. He has watched Mau come the whole length of the row. He has had plenty of time. He's a thin old man with a thin neck, and by the time Mau gets to him, he has both hands flat on the barrel, to keep them still.

“Morning,” Mau says. ᴹ

The old man's lips move.

“I'd like some pears. Six, if that's all right.” He has the purse out. It's the ordinary one, Umaru counted it. “How much?” ᴹ

“Please,” the old man says. ᴹⁿ

Mau holds out the coins.

The old man looks at them, the way you might look at something on the end of a stick. He doesn't take them. He starts to lift the pears onto a square of cloth with shaking fingers, one at a time, and then all the rest of them, the whole tray, more than anyone could want, and he gathers the corners and pushes it across the barrel with both hands. He's careful not to touch Mau's. He steps back from it. He steps back until the wall stops him.

“Please,” he says. “It's paid. It's all paid. Thank you. You don't have to do anything. It's paid.” ᴹⁿ

There is no sound in the whole of that market.

Mau is standing there holding out some money. He's got a very bad knee, and a pleasant expression that he has not yet worked out how to take off. He would like to say something. He's good at that, there's usually a joke. He looks for it. He's quite sure he had one.

He sees the old man's eyes. He knows that look. He has been on the other side of it, on one knee, in a field, with a person he loved crouched in front of him being very gentle.

Yuka reaches past him.

She takes three coins out of his hand. She looks at the tray, then at the little slate propped against the barrel, with the price in chalk. She works it out. It takes a moment, she's never been good at this. She adds one more. She walks up to the old man, right up, as close as you'd stand to ask someone the time. He has nowhere to go. She takes his hand off the wall, opens his fingers, one by one, and puts the money in his palm, and folds them shut.

“Six pears,” Yuka says. “That's what they cost.” ʸ

Her voice is perfectly clear. They can hear it at the other end of the wall.

She goes back to the barrel. She undoes the cloth and picks out six pears, taking her time, turning each one over to check for bruises. She puts them in her coat pockets. She takes the rest and sets them back on the tray, one by one, stem up, in rows. It takes quite a while. Nobody breathes. When she's finished, she folds the cloth and lays it on top, and she gives it a little pat.

“Those are good,” she tells him. “You should charge more.” ʸ

She takes Mau by the sleeve.

* * *

They walk back along the row, and it's a very long way. Nobody looks up.

She doesn't say anything until the field. She doesn't let go of the sleeve, either. Her jaw's so tight that he can see the muscle in it. When they get to the place where the road ends and the dry grass starts, she takes a pear out of her pocket and looks at it. For a moment he thinks she is going to throw it at the town.

She bites it. She chews for some time, breathing through her nose.

“It's very good,” she says, furiously. ʸ

He has started to laugh.

He doesn't know when that began. It's coming up from a long way down and it isn't a very nice laugh. It hurts his side. He leans on the cane and it goes on.

“You paid for pears,” he says, “like you were. Declaring war.” ᴹ

“He *thanked* you. He stood there and thanked you. For what? You wanted a *pear*.” ʸ

“I know.” ᴹ

“You saved that stupid road. His stupid brother's probably on it. Do you know how many of those people's—do you know what you *looked* like, lying there, did they even—” ʸ

She stops. She's got pear on her chin. There's water standing in her eyes, and she's so angry that she has gone red to the ears. It's the most colour he has seen all day. It's the only colour he has seen all day.

He stops laughing, somewhere in there.
