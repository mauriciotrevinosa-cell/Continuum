# The Arrivals — Light Novel — Volume 61
## Chapter 5 — Nobody Asked

**Status:** PRUEBA S10 v0.1 (2026-11-04). Sin marcas. No canon.
**Volume:** 61
**Reading order:** 5
**Source episode:** S10E03
**Continuity state:** B+156 morning. Opens arc 1, the keys. Native town, invented; name not fixed. Rumour of Mau eating Sukuna is false; the world acts on it. Echoes V53 #14. No violence.

*When: B+156, just after breakfast. Jinshi comes to the common table with his register under his arm, and he waits for Mau to finish swallowing, which he has never done for anyone.*

“There are people at the south gate,” Jinshi says. ᴶⁱ

Mau looks up. He has a pear in his hand. There are five of them left, and nobody will let him forget it.

“Whose?” ᴹ

“I don't know. They're not on anything. No notice, no letter. They came up the old road before light.” He opens the register and looks at it as if it might have improved. “They've asked for you. They didn't use your name. They asked for the one who got up.” ᴶⁱ

Across the table, Rimuru has stopped buttering something.

Yuka gets her boots.

* * *

It's a cold bright morning, and it's about a quarter of a mile to the gate. They take it at the speed of the cane. Rimuru comes along on the other side with his hands in his pockets, saying nothing, which is how you can tell. Bellion is looking out of the ground at Mau's heel. He has seen about thirty people, he reports, on foot. No weapons. No energy to speak of. Some of them have been walking for days.

*They've brought a cart,* Ciel says. ᶜⁱ

*What's in it?*

*It's closed. I could look. Do you want me to look?*

He doesn't know why he says no.

They are standing in the road outside the marker, in three rows, in what is plainly their best clothes. They've tried. It's the sort of good coat that gets taken out for funerals, with the dust of a long walk on it. There's a man at the front with a chain around his neck, and his mouth moves when he sees the cane. Then he gets down. It takes him a moment, because he is not young. He gets down on both knees in the road, and they all get down behind him, row by row, with a sound like a large animal settling.

Nobody says anything. A horse blows.

“What's this?” Rimuru says, quite gently. ᴿ

The man with the chain doesn't look at him. He's looking at the ground about a yard in front of Mau's boots. He has something in his arms, wrapped up in a clean cloth, and he holds it out with his head down and it shakes.

Mau goes forward. He can feel Yuka not stopping him. He takes the cloth, and it's heavy, and it clinks.

Keys.

There are nine or ten of them on an iron ring, big old black ones with the wards worn smooth. There's a book too, a long thin one, with a strap. He gets it open against his bad arm. It's columns. It's grain, wool, a mill, so many head of goats. It's a whole small town, written down very neatly in somebody's careful hand, and across the top of the first page, in fresh ink, somebody has added a word. He has to read that twice. It's a word you would use for what you pay a king.

He looks up.

“I don't,” Mau says. “Who are you?” ᴹ

The man tells him. He has been practising, you can hear that. It's a town called something. It's eleven days to the south. It's nobody's. They have never had any trouble with anyone. They've heard what happened at the quarry; a cousin was there. They've heard what he did to the worst thing in the world, and how, with his teeth, and they've heard he lay dead for a quarter of an hour, and got up. They understand. They would like him to know that they understand. They have brought everything. It's all there. It's correct, the clerk has checked it twice. They would just like to know what he requires. They'd like to have it in writing, if that wouldn't give offence.

His voice gives out around there.

It's very quiet. Mau stands there with a town in his hands.

He has a few things he could say. He could tell them he doesn't want it. He could tell them he got up because of a quite ordinary medical reason, and that he spat it out. He's got a wife behind him whose breathing has changed. He looks at those rows of good coats and bent heads, all the way to the back, where there's a boy of about twelve kneeling very straight, with his eyes shut.

“Nobody asked you to do this,” he says. ᴹ

“No, lord,” the man says, at once, with terrible relief. “No. We know. We wanted to come before anyone had to ask.” ᴹᶜ

Rimuru breathes out through his nose.

And the man lifts one hand a little from the road, without raising his head, and he makes a small movement toward the back. He has remembered something. He's anxious about that. He doesn't want it to seem as if they've forgotten.

“And we brought the others,” he says. “For you. So that you wouldn't have to— so that it needn't be anyone from— we thought, to begin with.” ᴹᶜ

Two men have got up, in the last row. They go to the cart. It's a plain farm cart with high sides and a tarpaulin lashed over it. They've got their shoulders hunched, and they're working at the knots with cold fingers and they can't look at it. The rope falls away. They take hold of the tarpaulin and draw it back.

Yuka says something, very quietly, behind him. He doesn't catch what.

There are seven of them in there, sitting on straw, with their hands tied in front. Someone has washed them. Someone has combed their hair. There's a big man with a broken nose and a woman with a brand on her arm, and a thin one who may be a thief, and they've all been given clean shirts. They've been sitting in that cart, in the dark, for eleven days.

Six of them look at the floor.

The seventh is an old man at the end. He's sitting up against the side with his tied hands in his lap. The light has gotten in his eyes, and he blinks at that for some time, and then he finds the person standing in the road. He takes in the cane. He looks at the neck. It takes him a little while, as it does with the old. He doesn't seem surprised. He's only so tired. He arranges his hands, so as not to be any inconvenience, and he lifts up his face.

“Please,” he says. “Make it quick.”
